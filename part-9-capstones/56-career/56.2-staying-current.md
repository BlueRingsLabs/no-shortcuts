# 56.2 Staying current without drowning

*About 4 hours. Prerequisites: none, except having finished enough of the course to be tired of it.*

This field produces more papers in a week than anyone can read in a year, a new model every few days, and an endless stream of posts
announcing that everything you know is obsolete. Most of it is noise. Some of it matters a great deal, and a year later it's obvious
which was which. The skill is not reading more; it's reading selectively, reading well, and having a way to tell durable ideas from
fashions before the year is up. This lesson is a method for that, and the last one in the course.

## What changes and what doesn't

Look back at what this course covered and sort it by how fast it changes:

- **Decades**: probability, statistics, linear algebra, optimization, information theory, the bias-variance trade-off, evaluation
  methodology, experimental design, data modeling, SQL, systems fundamentals. Parts I to III rest on these, and so does everything else.
- **Years**: architectures (the transformer is from 2017 and still here), training recipes, serving patterns, MLOps practice, the
  structure of RAG and agents.
- **Months**: specific models, benchmarks and leaderboards, libraries and their APIs, prompting tricks, pricing.

Invest your learning time in inverse proportion to the rate of change. Know the first group cold and you can absorb the third group
in an afternoon when you need it, and ignore it when you don't. The engineers who look obsolete after a paradigm shift are usually the
ones who learned a tool without the ideas under it.

## A reading system

**Sources, few and good.** Pick a small number and drop the rest:

- A handful of researchers and engineers whose judgment you've checked over time, preferably ones who say when they were wrong.
- One or two curated newsletters or paper digests.
- The papers and technical reports behind the systems you use.
- Conference proceedings (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL, KDD, SIGIR, MLSys) for your area, skimmed by title.
- The engineering blogs of companies operating at scale, for what works in production.

**Triage in three passes** (Keshav's method, adapted):

1. **Five minutes**: title, abstract, figures, conclusion. What's the claim? Is it relevant to you? Most papers stop here, and that's
   correct.
2. **An hour**: the method and the experiments, skipping proofs. Is the evaluation convincing? Against what baselines, tuned how well,
   with how many seeds, on which data?
3. **A day**: reproduce it or implement the core idea. Reserve this for the few papers a year that matter to your work. It's how you
   actually learn them.

**Questions to ask any result**, all of them from this course:

- Is the baseline strong and equally tuned (16.1, 46.4)?
- Is the improvement larger than the noise: seeds, confidence intervals, test set size (05.1, 39.1)?
- Could the test data have leaked into training (18.1, 39.1)?
- Does the benchmark measure what matters for your problem, or only what's easy to measure (43.1)?
- Is it compared at equal compute, equal data, equal parameters (35.4)?
- Would it survive the constraints of production: latency, cost, maintenance (49.3, 45.1)?

A result that fails several of these isn't necessarily wrong. It isn't yet known to be right.

## Learning by doing, on a schedule

Reading without doing fades within weeks. A routine that keeps skills alive without consuming your life:

- **One small reproduction per quarter**: take a result that matters to you and reproduce the core of it at small scale, the way this
  course's labs do. You'll learn what the paper didn't say.
- **Keep a lab notebook**: dated notes of what you tried, what happened, and what surprised you. Future you will search it more than
  any bookmark list.
- **Write**: a short post or internal note explaining something you learned. Explaining is the test of understanding, and a public trail
  of clear explanations is the best portfolio there is (56.1).
- **Teach**: answer questions from people a step behind you. You'll find out quickly which of your explanations were borrowed.

## Hype, and how to survive it

Every wave comes with confident predictions, rebranded old ideas, and demos selected from many attempts. Useful defenses:

- **Wait for replication.** Independent reproductions, open weights, third-party evaluations. The first announcement is marketing.
- **Look for the failure cases.** A system presented without its failure modes hasn't been evaluated, or has and they weren't shown.
- **Ask what's actually new.** Many "new" techniques are known ones with a new name and a larger model. Knowing the old name tells you
  their known limitations.
- **Separate capability from product.** A model that can do something in a demo is years, sometimes forever, from doing it reliably at a
  cost someone will pay.

None of that means cynicism about everything. Some waves are real; the transformer was, and the scaling results were. The point is to
decide on evidence, with the same tools you'd use to evaluate a model.

## Security doesn't wait

One area where falling behind has immediate costs: the security of the systems you build. New attack classes against ML systems and
LLM applications are published regularly, and supply-chain problems in the ML ecosystem (53.2) are ongoing. Follow the security advisories
of the frameworks and model hubs you depend on, the OWASP resources for LLM applications, and the MITRE ATLAS knowledge base, and keep your
dependencies patched. It's not optional, and it's the part of staying current most likely to matter on a Tuesday afternoon.

## The end of the course

If you've done the labs, you've implemented most of the ideas that modern ML systems are built from, at a scale where you could see them
work and fail. That's the durable part. The models will change, the libraries will change, and the job titles will certainly change; the
habits won't: measure before believing, compare against a baseline, look at the data, check for leaks, report the uncertainty, and write
down what you did so someone else can check it.

Go build something, and tell people honestly how well it works.

## Exercises

1. Choose five sources using the criteria above, and unsubscribe from everything else for a month. Note what you missed.
2. Take a paper from the last month in your area and apply the triage questions. Write a half-page review.
3. Pick one result and reproduce its core claim at small scale in a weekend. Write up what matched and what didn't.
4. Make a list of what you learned in this course, sorted into "decades", "years" and "months". Plan your next six months of learning
   from it.

## Further reading

- S. Keshav, "How to Read a Paper", *ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review*, 2007.
- Zachary Lipton, Jacob Steinhardt, "Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship", *ACM Queue*, 2019.
- The public reviews on OpenReview for ICLR, NeurIPS and TMLR papers: the fastest way to learn what experts object to in a paper you
  just found convincing.
- Stanford HAI, *AI Index Report* (annual): one data-heavy snapshot of the field per year, to calibrate the hype against.
- D. Sculley et al., "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems", *NeurIPS*, 2015. Still the best short description of what the
  job is.
- Richard Sutton, "The Bitter Lesson", 2019, and the arguments against it: read both and decide for yourself.
