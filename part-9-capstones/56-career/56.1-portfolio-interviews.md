# 56.1 Portfolio, interviews and ML system design interviews

*About 6 hours. Prerequisites: at least one capstone.*

You've done the work. Now someone has to believe it, usually in about ninety seconds of reading and a few hours of interviews, run by
people who are busy, tired of reading tutorials rebranded as projects, and trying hard not to hire someone who'll need babysitting. This
lesson is about making the evidence easy to see, and about the interviews, which test a narrower and more learnable set of skills than
the job does. It's opinionated. Hiring practices vary by company and country, and change; where they differ from what's here, believe the
company.

## What a portfolio is for

A portfolio answers one question: **can this person do the job without supervision?** A reviewer looks for signs of engineering judgment,
and the signs are specific:

- **A problem, not a technique.** "Predicting which customers will stop buying, for a team that can contact 500 a week" beats "XGBoost
  churn model". The first says you know why the model exists.
- **A baseline and an honest comparison.** The model beat the obvious rule by how much, with what uncertainty, on a split that respects
  time.
- **Something that went wrong**, found and explained. A leak you caught, a metric that lied, an approach that failed. Nothing signals
  experience like a well-explained mistake, and nothing signals inexperience like a project where everything worked the first time.
- **Engineering around the model**: tests, a pipeline someone else can run, monitoring, a README that starts with how to run it.
- **Writing**: a short design doc or report. Most of the job is writing things other people must understand.

Three deep projects beat twelve shallow ones. The capstones of this module were designed to be portfolio projects; if you do only one,
make it B or D, and do it completely.

What doesn't help: Kaggle-style notebooks that end at a leaderboard score, courses listed as projects, a model trained on a famous dataset
with a famous architecture and nothing else, and screenshots of chat conversations with an LLM. What helps a lot: contributions to open
source projects people use (a fixed bug with a test in a library counts for more than a new repository nobody uses), and writing that
explains something you learned the hard way.

## The README that gets read

The first screen of your repository is the portfolio. It should say, in order: what problem this solves and for whom; the result, in the
problem's terms, with a number and its uncertainty; one diagram of the system; how to run it (one command after cloning, ideally); what
you'd do next and what's known to be wrong. Put the long report behind a link. Delete the dead code and the notebooks named
`Untitled3.ipynb`.

## The resume

One page for up to ten years of experience. Each line a result with a number, not a responsibility: "cut p99 latency of the scoring
service from 900 ms to 120 ms by batching and moving feature lookups to a cache" says more than "responsible for ML serving". Tailor the
top third to the role. Link the portfolio. If a claim can't survive "tell me more about that" in an interview, delete it.

## The interviews

Most ML engineering loops contain some combination of the following. Ask the recruiter which ones and in what format; they'll usually
tell you, and it's a reasonable question.

**Coding.** Data structures and algorithms at a moderate level, or practical coding (parse this log, implement this data transformation,
write this class with tests). Practice both, out loud, with a timer. Talk while you work: the interviewer grades your reasoning as much as
the code. Clarify the input, state an approach and its complexity, write it, test it with an edge case you chose.

**ML coding.** Implement something small from scratch: logistic regression with gradient descent, k-means, attention, a training loop,
a metric like AUC. Every lab in this course is practice for this. The traps are the ones from the labs: numerical stability (log-sum-exp),
shapes, off-by-one in the sequence dimension, data leakage in preprocessing.

**ML theory and breadth.** Bias and variance, regularization, why a metric is misleading, calibration, what attention computes, why Adam,
what happens with class imbalance, how you'd detect drift. The self-check questions of each module are exactly this kind of question;
answer them all out loud, then check the lessons.

**ML system design.** The one that decides senior levels, and the most learnable once you know its shape. Below.

**Behavioral.** Past situations: a conflict, a failure, a decision under uncertainty, a time you disagreed with your manager. Prepare
six stories in situation, task, action, result form, with numbers, and include what you'd do differently. The failure story matters most:
choose a real one, own it, and show what changed afterwards.

## ML system design interviews

You get a vague product problem ("design a system that recommends videos", "detect fraudulent transactions", "a support chatbot for our
docs") and 45 minutes. There's no single right answer; the interviewer watches whether you drive the problem the way a senior engineer
would. A structure that works:

1. **Clarify the goal and the constraints** (5 minutes). Who uses it, what decision it drives, what success means in business terms,
   scale (users, requests per second, items), latency, freshness, budget, and what exists already. Write the numbers down.
2. **Frame it as ML** (5 minutes). What's predicted, from what, with what label, and where the labels come from (and how delayed or
   biased they are). The offline metric and the online metric, and why they might disagree (47.1).
3. **Data** (5 minutes). Sources, features, point-in-time correctness, feedback loops (47.2), privacy.
4. **The model**, briefly (5 minutes). Start with the baseline, then the model you'd actually ship, with one sentence on why. Resist the
   urge to spend twenty minutes on architecture; that's the most common failure in these interviews.
5. **Serving** (10 minutes). Batch or online (49.1), candidate generation and ranking for large catalogs, latency budget per stage,
   caching, the fallback when the model is down.
6. **Evaluation, rollout and monitoring** (10 minutes). Offline evaluation with slices, shadow and canary and A/B (50.2), what you'd
   monitor and alert on (51.1), how retraining works.
7. **Risks** (the remaining time). Fairness (54.1), abuse and security (44.1, 53.1), cost (52.1), what could make the whole thing a
   bad idea.

Draw boxes, write numbers, and state trade-offs explicitly: "I'd precompute candidates nightly because freshness of a day is acceptable
for this, which saves the online retrieval stage; if the product needs real-time trends, I'd add a streaming candidate source." The
interviewer will push on one part; follow them there and go deep.

For LLM application design questions, the same structure applies with Part VI's content: retrieval design and its evaluation (41.4),
agents only where needed (42.3), an eval suite (43.1), guardrails and prompt injection (44.1), gateway, cost and latency (45.1).

Practice by designing, on paper, one system per capstone and one per lesson of Module 47, then explaining it out loud to someone who'll
interrupt you.

## Negotiation, briefly

Get offers in parallel when you can. Ask for the full compensation breakdown (base, bonus, equity with its vesting and valuation, benefits)
and compare total value, adjusted for risk. Say a number based on market data for the role and location, not on your current salary. Be
polite and specific; the worst outcome of a reasonable, well-argued request is almost always "no".

> **Scar tissue.** I failed an ML system design interview I should have passed. The question was a recommendation system, and I spent
> thirty of the forty-five minutes on the model architecture, because that was the fun part. I never got to serving, evaluation or
> monitoring, which were what the interviewer was actually there to assess. The feedback was one sentence: "Strong modeling, no system."
> Fair. Now I set a timer when I practice.

## Things that will bite you

- **A portfolio of tutorials.**
- **No failure in any project**, which reads as either luck or dishonesty.
- **Claims that can't survive a follow-up question.**
- **System design answers that are all model** and no data, serving or evaluation.
- **Not asking what the interview will contain.**
- **Memorized answers** delivered without adapting to the question.

## Exercises

1. Rewrite the README of your best capstone following the section on READMEs. Time how long a friend takes to understand what it does.
2. Write six behavioral stories, including a failure, each under 250 words.
3. Do a 45-minute mock ML system design interview for "detect fake reviews on a marketplace", with someone timing you. Afterwards, list
   the parts you spent too long on.
4. Implement, without notes, in under 30 minutes each: logistic regression by gradient descent with a stable loss; multi-head attention
   with a causal mask; AUC from scores and labels.
5. Pick a job posting you'd want. Map each requirement to evidence in your portfolio, and list the gaps.

<summary>What good looks like</summary>

1. Your friend can say, after reading only the first screen, what problem it solves, how well, and how to run it. If they ask "so what
   does it do?", the first paragraph isn't doing its job.

2. Each story has numbers, your specific actions (not "we"), and a lesson. The failure story shows a real consequence and a change in how
   you work, not a humblebrag ("I care too much about code quality").

3. You spent under ten minutes on the model; you stated the label source (user reports, moderator decisions, with their biases and
   delays) and the adversarial nature of the problem (fraudsters adapt, so monitoring and retraining matter); you gave numbers for scale
   and latency; you covered evaluation with slices, rollout and abuse.

4. The loss uses log-sum-exp or a stable sigmoid cross-entropy; the mask is applied before the softmax with `-inf` above the diagonal;
   AUC handles ties (average ranks) and is checked against a library on random data.

5. Gaps become your next project, chosen to produce evidence for the requirements the posting cares about most.

## Further reading

- Chip Huyen, *Designing Machine Learning Systems*, O'Reilly, 2022, and *Introduction to Machine Learning Interviews* (free online).
- Martin Kleppmann, *Designing Data-Intensive Applications*, O'Reilly, 2017: the system design half of most ML system design
  interviews.
- Gayle Laakmann McDowell, *Cracking the Coding Interview*, 6th edition, CareerCup, 2015, for the coding rounds.
- The engineering blogs of companies you'd want to work for: how they describe their systems is how they'll expect you to.
