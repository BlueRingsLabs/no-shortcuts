# 55.1 Capstone A: the data platform

*About 60 hours. Prerequisites: Parts I and II. Data: [`lab_55_1_capstone_data.py`](labs/lab_55_1_capstone_data.py).*

There's no lesson here, only a spec, the way work arrives. A small online shop sells candles and soap across eight European countries. Its
data lives in the places data usually lives: a customers table replicated as a change-data-capture log, a product catalog with a price
history, a queue of order lifecycle events, order lines, a clickstream and a support-ticket export. Nobody has ever joined any of it
reliably. Your job is to build the platform that does, and that the next two capstones (B and D) stand on.

Generate the data first (20,000 customers, nine months, about 180 MB):

```bash
python part-9-capstones/55-capstones/labs/lab_55_1_capstone_data.py --out capstone-data --customers 20000
```

The raw files contain problems. Some are listed in the spoiler block at the end. Try to find them yourself first: that's the most
realistic part of the exercise, and the skill employers actually pay for.

## What to build

**1. Ingestion and a raw layer.** Load every file into a raw (bronze) layer as it arrived: no fixes, no dedup, with load metadata (file,
load time, row number). Raw is your audit trail and your replay source. Parquet on local disk, or a DuckDB or Postgres database (11.1,
Module 08); your choice, justified in the README.

**2. A clean layer.** Typed, deduplicated, validated tables:
- `customers`: the current state, rebuilt from the CDC log, with deletions honored (a deleted customer's personal data is gone from
  every clean and served table, 15.2 and 54.2).
- `customers_history`: slowly changing dimension type 2 (09.2): one row per version, with `valid_from` and `valid_to`.
- `product_prices`: SCD2 of prices, so any order line can be checked against the price in force at its time.
- `orders`: one row per order with its lifecycle timestamps (created, paid, shipped, delivered, refunded), from the event stream,
  deduplicated and correct regardless of arrival order.
- `order_items`, `sessions` (sessionized clickstream), `tickets` (with personal data masked in the text).

**3. A modeled layer** for analysis (09.2): a star schema with facts (order lines, sessions) and dimensions (customer, product, date),
plus daily aggregates: revenue, orders, refunds, conversion rate (sessions with an order), by country and channel.

**4. Data quality.** Tests on every clean and modeled table (13.3): keys unique and not null, referential integrity, accepted values,
freshness, row-count anomalies, and business rules (a paid amount matches its lines; nothing ships before it's paid). Quarantine bad rows
with a reason; never drop them silently. A report of what was quarantined, per run.

**5. Incremental processing.** The pipeline processes one day at a time, idempotently (13.1): rerunning a day gives the same result, and
late events (arriving days after their event time) update the right day. Demonstrate a backfill of the whole period, then a rerun of one
day that changes nothing.

**6. Orchestration.** A DAG (Dagster, Airflow, or a Makefile with honest dependencies, 13.2) that runs the daily increment, the tests,
and the report, and that stops downstream steps when upstream tests fail.

**7. A streaming path** (14.1, 14.2): replay `order_events.jsonl` through a local queue in `ingested_ts` order, and maintain per-hour
revenue with event-time windows and a watermark. Show what happens to late events with your chosen allowed lateness, and reconcile the
streaming numbers with the batch numbers for the same hours.

**8. Access and security** (15.1, 15.2): two roles (analyst: aggregates and pseudonymized customer ids; support: tickets for their
cases), enforced in the database or the serving layer, not by convention. Secrets out of the code.

## Deliverables

- A repository with the code, a README that someone else can follow from a clean machine to a populated platform in under 30 minutes,
  and a one-page architecture diagram.
- A **data contract** document for `orders` and `customers`: schema, semantics of each column, freshness guarantee, owner (15.2).
- The data-quality report for the full backfill, and a list of every problem you found in the raw data, with how the pipeline handles it.
- Answers to the business questions below, computed from the modeled layer, with the SQL.
- A short incident write-up: pick the worst data problem you found and write it up as if it had reached a dashboard (15.3).

## Business questions

1. Monthly revenue net of refunds, by country. Which month was best?
2. How did the price increase of mid-2025 change the number of orders and the average order value, by category?
3. What share of new customers came from each channel per month, and how did the partner marketplace's customers differ in refund
   rate and repeat purchases?
4. Conversion rate from session to order, by week. Is the change you see real (05.2)?
5. Median time from paid to delivered, by country, and the share of support tickets about late deliveries.

## How it will be judged

The same way a senior engineer reviews a platform: does it produce correct numbers (reconciled, tested, and reproducible from raw), does it
fail loudly, can someone else run it, and is it no more complicated than it needs to be. A Kubernetes cluster for 180 MB of data is a
point against you.

| criterion | weight |
|---|---|
| correctness: every planted problem found and handled, numbers reconcile between layers and between batch and streaming | 35% |
| reproducibility: one command from raw to modeled, idempotent reruns, a clean-machine README | 20% |
| data quality: tests, quarantine, report | 15% |
| design: layers, contracts, SCD2, choices justified in writing | 15% |
| security and privacy: deletions honored, roles enforced, PII masked | 15% |

<summary>Spoilers: the planted problems (read after you've looked)</summary>

- About 1% of order events are exact duplicates (at-least-once delivery).
- About 2% of order events arrive one to three days late; the file is in arrival order, not event order.
- On one day in late July, a producer wrote event timestamps in local time (UTC+2) without an offset.
- On one day in early April, order-line prices were exported in cents.
- A handful of order lines have negative quantities.
- Customer records have missing countries, changing emails and countries (so joins on email break), and GDPR deletions in the CDC log.
- A `marketing_opt_in` column appears in the CDC log partway through the period (schema evolution).
- Most products get a price rise on the same day; order-line prices must be checked against the price in force at the time.
- A new acquisition channel appears partway through with very different behavior; averages across the whole period mix two populations.
- Support ticket text contains email addresses.
- Web events without a customer id (anonymous sessions) are about 10% of the clickstream.
