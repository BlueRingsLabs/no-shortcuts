# 55.2 Capstone B: the tabular ML product

*About 60 hours. Prerequisites: Capstone A, Parts III and VIII. Data: Capstone A's platform.*

The shop from Capstone A wants to stop losing customers. The retention team can send about 500 personalized offers a week (a discount
code and a handwritten-looking note; each costs about €6 in discount and handling), and wants to send them to the customers most likely
to stop buying *and* most likely to be kept by the offer. They've heard about machine learning. You're going to give them a model, but
mostly you're going to give them a product: something that runs every week without you, tells them when it's wrong, and can be explained
to the person who asks why they didn't get a discount.

## The problem, precisely

Write it down before touching a model (47.1). A starting definition you may improve and must justify:

- **Population**: customers with at least one order in the 90 days before a scoring date.
- **Label**: churned if they place no order in the 60 days after the scoring date.
- **Scoring**: weekly, on Mondays, using only data available by Sunday midnight.
- **Action**: the top 500 by expected value get the offer. The value of keeping a customer is their expected margin over the next six
  months; estimate it from the data and state the assumptions.

The cost matrix, the capacity and the timing turn "predict churn" into a decision problem (18.3). Note what the model can't tell you:
whether the offer *causes* a customer to stay. The spec's last part addresses that.

## What to build

**1. A training set without leakage** (22.1, 18.1). Point-in-time correct features for many past scoring dates, from Capstone A's clean
layer: recency, frequency, monetary value, trends, categories, refunds, tickets, web activity, price sensitivity around the price rise,
tenure, channel, country. Every feature computed only from data with an event time *and an ingestion time* before the scoring date. Some
columns in the raw data leak the label; finding them is part of the job.

**2. Splits that respect time.** Train on earlier scoring dates, validate and test on later ones, with a gap equal to the label window
(18.1). Report how many customers appear in both train and test, and why that's acceptable or not.

**3. Baselines first** (16.1): "recency above N days", and a logistic regression. Then gradient-boosted trees (20.3). Tune honestly
(nested or with a separate validation period).

**4. Evaluation for the decision** (18.2, 18.3): precision and recall in the top 500 per week, expected value captured, calibration
(the expected-value ranking needs probabilities, not scores), lift over the recency rule, confidence intervals (05.1). Slices by country,
channel, tenure and age band; fairness review (54.1) of who gets offers and who doesn't.

**5. Explanations** (22.3): global feature importance you'd defend in a meeting, and per-customer reasons for the retention team. Check
the explanations for leakage and for features that are proxies for something you shouldn't use.

**6. Serving** (49.1, 49.2): a weekly batch job writing scores and reasons to a table, and an online endpoint that scores one customer
on demand for the support team, both from the same feature code (tested for train/serve consistency, 50.1). A container, a health check,
resource limits.

**7. Tracking and registry** (48.1, 48.2): every training run tracked with data version, code version, parameters and metrics; the
production model promoted through the registry with its model card (54.3), generated, not handwritten.

**8. Monitoring** (51.1): input drift, prediction drift, the share of the population by channel, and, once labels arrive 60 days later,
realized precision in the top 500. Alerts with thresholds you've justified, and a dashboard. Replay the nine months as if you'd deployed on
the first possible date and show what your monitoring would have said, and when.

**9. Retraining** (51.1, 50.2): a pipeline that retrains on a schedule or a trigger, evaluates the candidate against production on the
same recent data, and promotes only if it's better by a margin you set in advance. Shadow the candidate for one scoring week first.

**10. The causal question** (05.4, 50.2): design the experiment that would tell the retention team whether the offers work: a random
holdout within the top 500, the metric, the sample size for the effect they care about, and how long it takes. You can't run it on
synthetic data; write the plan as if you were going to.

## Deliverables

- The repository: features, training, evaluation, serving, monitoring, retraining, with tests and a README.
- A design doc (47.1) written before the model and updated after, including the decisions you changed and why.
- The model card, the evaluation report with slices, and the fairness review.
- The monitoring replay: a timeline of what your alerts would have said over the nine months.
- A one-page memo for the retention team: what the model does, how good it is in their terms (euros and customers), what it can't do,
  and how they'll know if it stops working.

## How it will be judged

| criterion | weight |
|---|---|
| no leakage: point-in-time features, time-respecting splits, leaking columns found and excluded | 25% |
| evaluation in the decision's terms, with intervals, calibration and slices | 20% |
| engineering: one pipeline from raw to scores, tested, reproducible, containerized | 20% |
| monitoring and retraining that would actually catch the problems in this data | 20% |
| communication: design doc, model card, memo | 15% |

A model with an AUC of 0.99 is a finding, not a result: find the leak.

<summary>Spoilers (read after you've looked)</summary>

- GDPR deletions in the CDC log happen after the customer's last order, and deleted customers never order again: any feature derived from
  deletion events near or after the scoring date leaks the label. So do support tickets and refunds opened after the scoring date, if you
  join on customer id without an event-time filter.
- Late-arriving order events: a feature computed by event time alone can include events that weren't available on the scoring date.
  Use ingestion time for availability.
- The partner marketplace channel appears partway through with customers who buy once and churn at more than 80%. The overall churn
  rate rises with their share while the churn of every other channel stays flat: a population shift your monitoring should explain
  rather than just flag.
- The price rise changes order values mid-period; monetary features computed across it drift for reasons unrelated to churn.
- One day of order lines in cents inflates monetary features for the customers who ordered that day, unless Capstone A caught it.
