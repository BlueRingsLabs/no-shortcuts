# 16.2 Generalization, bias and variance

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 16.1, 05.1 (the estimator bias-variance decomposition), 04.3. Lab: [`lab_16_2_bias_variance.py`](labs/lab_16_2_bias_variance.py).*

Every model you'll ever train fails in one of two ways. Either it's too simple to capture the pattern (it **underfits**),
or it's so flexible that it memorizes the noise in the training data along with the pattern (it **overfits**). The art of
machine learning, before any fancy algorithm, is steering between those two. You saw the numbers in 16.1's lab: a degree-15
polynomial had the lowest training error and a worse error on new data than a degree-3 one.

This lesson makes that precise with the bias-variance decomposition, gives you the diagnostic plots that tell you which way
you're failing, and tells you what to do about each. It's also, I think, the single most frequently asked conceptual
question in ML interviews, so it's worth understanding rather than reciting.

## Training error lies

The training error $\hat{R}_n(\hat{f})$ is computed on the same data used to choose $\hat{f}$, so it's **optimistically biased**: the model was
selected precisely because it does well on those points. The richer the hypothesis class, the more it can bend toward the specific sample, and the
bigger the gap between training error and the true risk $R(\hat{f})$. A model that fits every training point exactly (a 1-nearest-neighbor
classifier, a deep unpruned tree, a polynomial with as many coefficients as points) has zero training error and tells you nothing about its
real performance.

So: **never evaluate a model on data it was trained on.** Hold out data (a validation set for choices during development, a test set for the final
estimate), or use cross-validation (18.1). This rule has no exceptions that I know of.

## The bias-variance decomposition

Take regression with squared loss. The data is generated as $y = f(x) + \epsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon) = \sigma^2$.
You draw a training set $D$, fit $\hat{f}_D$, and predict at a fixed point $x$. The prediction depends on which training set you happened to draw, so
it's a random variable. Its expected squared error, over both the training set and the noise in the new $y$, decomposes as:

$$\mathbb{E}_{D,\epsilon}\big[(y - \hat{f}_D(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}} + \underbrace{\big(f(x) - \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{f}_D(x)]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_D\big[(\hat{f}_D(x) - \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{f}_D(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}$$

Derivation: write $y - \hat{f} = (y - f) + (f - \bar{f}) + (\bar{f} - \hat{f})$ with $\bar{f} = \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{f}_D(x)]$, square, and take expectations. The cross terms vanish
because $\epsilon$ has zero mean and is independent of $D$, and because $\hat{f} - \bar{f}$ has zero mean over $D$. It's the same algebra as the estimator
MSE decomposition in 05.1, now applied to functions. (Exercise 1 asks you to do it; please do.)

What each term means:

- **Noise** $\sigma^2$: variability in $y$ that no model can predict from $x$. Measurement error, missing information. It's the floor. More data doesn't reduce
  it; better features (information the current $x$ doesn't have) do.
- **Bias**: how far the *average* model (over all possible training sets) is from the truth. High when the hypothesis class can't represent $f$: a line fit
  to a curve. More data doesn't reduce it either.
- **Variance**: how much the fitted model changes from one training set to another. High when the model is flexible relative to the amount of data. More data
  reduces it; so does regularization.

Increasing model complexity typically lowers bias and raises variance. The total error is U-shaped in complexity, with a sweet spot in between. The lab computes
the three terms exactly by simulation (fitting hundreds of models on hundreds of freshly drawn training sets) for polynomial regression and k-nearest neighbors,
and you can watch the trade-off happen.

For classification with 0-1 loss, the decomposition doesn't split as cleanly (bias and variance interact), but the intuition carries over completely.

## Capacity knobs

Every model family has knobs that move it along the bias-variance axis:

| Model | More capacity (less bias, more variance) | Less capacity |
|---|---|---|
| Polynomial / basis expansion | higher degree, more features | fewer |
| k-NN | smaller k | larger k |
| Decision tree | deeper, smaller min leaf size | shallower, bigger leaves |
| Linear models | weaker regularization (smaller λ) | stronger regularization |
| Neural networks | more layers/units, less weight decay, less dropout, longer training | the reverse |
| Gradient boosting | more trees, deeper trees, higher learning rate | the reverse |

And there's the knob outside the model: **more data** reduces variance for any fixed model, which is why the right capacity depends on how much data you have.
The same network that overfits 1,000 examples may underfit 10 million.

## Diagnostics: learning curves and validation curves

Two plots tell you which problem you have:

**Validation curve**: training and validation error as a function of a capacity knob (degree, k, depth, λ).

- Both errors high and close together at low capacity: underfitting (high bias).
- Training error low and validation error high at high capacity: overfitting (high variance).
- Pick the capacity where validation error is lowest (and prefer the simpler model if the difference is within noise).

**Learning curve**: training and validation error as a function of training set size, for a fixed model.

- The curves converge to a **high** error: high bias. More data won't help. Use a more flexible model or better features.
- A **large gap** between training and validation error that shrinks as data grows: high variance. More data will help; so will regularization or a simpler
  model.

These two plots answer the question every ML project asks at some point, "should we collect more data or try a bigger model?", with evidence instead of opinion.
The lab draws both.

## What to do about each

**High bias (underfitting)**: add features (especially domain features, remember 16.1's fuel type), use a more flexible model, reduce regularization, train
longer (for iterative models), check that the problem isn't mostly noise (compare with the best achievable error if you can estimate it).

**High variance (overfitting)**: more data, regularization (17.2, 26.3), simpler model, fewer or better features, ensembling (bagging reduces variance, 20.2),
early stopping (26.3), data augmentation (for images and text).

**Neither, but still bad**: the problem might be mostly irreducible noise given your features, the labels might be wrong (10.3), or train and test might come
from different distributions (18.4, 51.1).

## A modern footnote: double descent

The classic U-shape isn't the whole story. When model capacity keeps growing past the point where the model can fit the training data exactly (the
**interpolation threshold**), test error can go *down* again: the **double descent** curve described by Belkin et al. (2019). Hugely overparameterized models
(modern neural networks, but also random-feature regression and even boosting in some regimes) generalize well despite fitting the training data perfectly,
because among the many interpolating solutions, the training procedure picks a particularly smooth one (minimum norm, remember 02.3 and 03.3). 16.3 goes into
it a bit more. It doesn't invalidate bias-variance; it shows that "capacity" is subtler than the number of parameters.

## Things that will bite you

- **Reporting training error.** Or validation error on the set you tuned on, as if it were a test error (18.1).
- **Adding complexity to fix an underfitting that's really a feature problem.**
- **Collecting more data to fix a bias problem.** It won't.
- **Choosing capacity by a single noisy validation number**: look at the curve, and at the uncertainty (05.1).
- **Assuming "more parameters = more overfitting"** in every regime.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Let $\bar{f} = \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{f}_D(x)]$. $y - \hat{f} = \epsilon + (f - \bar{f}) + (\bar{f} - \hat{f})$. Squaring gives three squares and three cross terms. $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2] = \sigma^2$.
   $(f - \bar{f})^2$ is a constant (bias²). $\mathbb{E}_D[(\bar{f} - \hat{f})^2]$ is the variance. Cross terms: $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(f - \bar{f})] = 0$ ($\epsilon$ has mean zero),
   $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(\bar{f} - \hat{f})] = 0$ ($\epsilon$ is independent of $D$ and has mean zero), $\mathbb{E}_D[(f - \bar{f})(\bar{f} - \hat{f})] = (f - \bar{f})\,\mathbb{E}_D[\bar{f} - \hat{f}] = 0$.

2. The prediction is the average of k noisy targets: its variance is roughly $\sigma^2/k$ (plus the variability of which neighbors are picked), so it decreases with k.
   But larger k averages over neighbors that are farther away, whose true $f$ values differ from $f(x)$, so bias grows. k = 1: very low bias near the data, high
   variance (it copies one noisy label). k = n: predicts the global mean everywhere: zero variance in the "which neighbors" sense, maximal bias.

3. High variance, and more data is still paying off: get more data if possible; meanwhile regularize, simplify, or ensemble. If both are at 14% and flat: high bias
   (or irreducible noise); more data won't help; add features or capacity, and check label quality.

4. You're close to the best known achievable error, so the remaining gap is small; large further gains probably need new information (features, better labels)
   rather than model tuning. Also question whether the last 0.02 is worth it for the business.

5. Averaging B models with the same expected prediction $\bar{f}$ leaves the expectation (and thus the bias) unchanged, while the variance of the average is smaller
   than the variance of a single model, by a factor up to B when the models are uncorrelated (20.2 derives the exact formula with correlation).
