# 16.3 Learning theory: PAC, VC and the curse of dimensionality

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 16.1, 16.2, 04.3 (Hoeffding), 02.1 (high-dimensional geometry). Lab: [`lab_16_3_learning_theory.py`](labs/lab_16_3_learning_theory.py).*

Learning theory has a reputation for being beautiful and useless. The beautiful part is true. The useless part isn't, as
long as you don't expect it to give you numbers: most bounds are far too loose to tell you how many examples your specific
problem needs. What they give you is something better than numbers: the **shape** of the answers. Why more data helps and
by how much. Why a richer model needs more data to be trusted. Why a test set of 100 examples can't distinguish models that
differ by 1%. Why nearest neighbors in 500 dimensions behave strangely. And, more recently, why all of that seemed to break
for deep networks and what we learned when it did.

This lesson is the theory I actually use when reasoning about models. No measure theory, a couple of short proofs, and a lab
that checks every claim numerically.

## Generalization for a single hypothesis

Start with the simplest case: one fixed classifier $h$, chosen *before* looking at the test data. Its test error on $n$ i.i.d. examples is an average of
bounded variables (each 0 or 1), so **Hoeffding's inequality** (04.3) says:

$$P\big(|\hat{R}_n(h) - R(h)| > \epsilon\big) \le 2e^{-2n\epsilon^2}$$

Set the right side to $\delta$ and solve: with probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$|\hat{R}_n(h) - R(h)| \le \sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}$$

With $n = 1{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$, that's about $\pm 0.043$. With $n = 10{,}000$, about $\pm 0.014$. The $1/\sqrt{n}$ again. This is why test sets need to be large to
separate models that are close, and why the "we got 91.2% vs 90.8%" comparison on 1,000 examples means nothing (05.1).

## Many hypotheses: why training error is optimistic

The single-hypothesis bound fails for the model you *trained*, because $\hat{f}$ was chosen using the same data. With a finite hypothesis class $\mathcal{H}$, apply
Hoeffding to every hypothesis and take a **union bound**: the probability that *any* of them has a gap larger than $\epsilon$ is at most $|\mathcal{H}|$ times the
individual probability. So, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, for **every** $h \in \mathcal{H}$ simultaneously, including the one ERM picks:

$$R(h) \le \hat{R}_n(h) + \sqrt{\frac{\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}$$

That's the essence of generalization bounds: **true error ≤ training error + a complexity penalty that grows with the size of the hypothesis class and shrinks with
the amount of data.** It explains, formally, 16.2's observation that richer models overfit more: they get more chances to fit the sample by luck.

The same argument explains a very practical danger: every time you try a model configuration and look at the validation score, you're adding a hypothesis to
the set you're choosing from. Try 1,000 configurations against the same validation set, and the winner's validation score is optimistic by roughly
$\sqrt{\ln(1000)/n}$. That's why you keep a separate **test set** that you look at once (18.1), and why leaderboards that people submit to thousands of times
end up overfitted to their test sets.

### PAC learning

The **probably approximately correct** framework (Valiant, 1984) formalizes "learnable": a class is PAC-learnable if, for any $\epsilon$ (approximately correct)
and $\delta$ (probably), a number of examples polynomial in $1/\epsilon$ and $1/\delta$ suffices for ERM to find a hypothesis with error at most $\epsilon$ above the best in the
class, with probability at least $1 - \delta$. For finite classes, the bound above gives a sample complexity of order
$\frac{1}{\epsilon^2}\left(\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)$ (or $\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\ldots)$ in the realizable case where some $h$ has zero error). The takeaway:
data requirements grow with the **logarithm** of the number of hypotheses, and with $1/\epsilon^2$ for the precision you want.

## Infinite classes: VC dimension

Linear classifiers form an infinite class ($|\mathcal{H}| = \infty$), yet they obviously generalize. What matters isn't how many hypotheses there are, but how many
genuinely **different ways they can label a sample**.

A class **shatters** a set of $m$ points if, for every one of the $2^m$ possible labelings, some hypothesis in the class realizes it. The **VC dimension** is the size of
the largest set the class can shatter.

- Thresholds on a line ($x > t$): VC dimension 1.
- Intervals on a line: 2.
- Linear classifiers (half-planes) in $\mathbb{R}^2$: 3. Three points in general position can be split in all 8 ways; no four points can (the XOR labeling of a
  square fails). In $\mathbb{R}^d$, linear classifiers with a bias have VC dimension $d + 1$.
- Axis-aligned rectangles in the plane: 4.

The VC bound replaces $\ln|\mathcal{H}|$ with roughly $d_{\text{VC}}\ln(n/d_{\text{VC}})$:

$$R(h) \le \hat{R}_n(h) + O\left(\sqrt{\frac{d_{\text{VC}}\ln(n/d_{\text{VC}}) + \ln(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)$$

Rule of thumb that falls out of it (and that practitioners have used for decades): you want a number of examples comfortably larger than the number of effective
parameters, often quoted as 10 per parameter for simple models. It's a heuristic, not a law, and the next section is about where it fails spectacularly.

(Other complexity measures refine this: **Rademacher complexity** measures how well the class can fit random noise on *your* data distribution; margin-based
bounds explain why SVMs with large margins generalize regardless of dimension, 19.3.)

## No free lunch

The **no free lunch theorem** (Wolpert, 1996) says that averaged over *all possible* target functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected performance
off the training set. A model can only beat chance on a problem because its assumptions (smoothness, linearity, locality, invariances) match the problem. There's no
universally best algorithm, which is why baselines and empirical comparisons (16.1, Module 18) are not optional, and why the inductive biases built into architectures
(convolutions for images, 27.1; attention for sequences, 29.1) matter so much.

## The curse of dimensionality

Many methods rely on "nearby points have similar labels". In high dimensions, "nearby" stops meaning much:

1. **Space is mostly empty.** To cover the unit hypercube $[0,1]^d$ at resolution 0.1 per axis takes $10^d$ cells. With $d = 20$, that's $10^{20}$ cells: no dataset fills
   them. To capture 1% of uniformly distributed data in a sub-cube, its side must be $0.01^{1/d}$, which is 0.63 in 10 dimensions and 0.955 in 100: the "local"
   neighborhood spans almost the whole range of every feature.
2. **Volume concentrates near the surface.** The fraction of a $d$-dimensional ball's volume within 5% of its surface is $1 - 0.95^d$: 40% in 10 dimensions, 99.4% in
   100. Almost all points are near the edge; almost none are "central".
3. **Distances concentrate** (you measured this in 02.1): the nearest and farthest neighbors of a random point are almost equally far away, relative to their size.
   Nearest-neighbor methods, clustering and kernels with a fixed width lose their discriminating power.

Why does ML work at all, then? Because real data isn't uniform in high dimensions. It lies close to **low-dimensional structure** (the **manifold hypothesis**: natural
images of faces occupy a tiny, smooth, low-dimensional region of pixel space), and features are correlated. The effective dimension is much lower than the nominal one.
Methods that exploit structure (linear models' additivity, trees' feature selection, CNNs' locality, representation learning in general) beat the curse; methods that rely
on raw Euclidean distances in the original space suffer from it (19.1).

## When the classical story broke: overparameterized models

By the VC reasoning, a neural network with 100 million parameters trained on 1 million examples should overfit catastrophically. It doesn't. Zhang et al. (2017) showed that
standard image networks can fit *random labels* perfectly (so their capacity, in the VC sense, is enormous) and yet generalize well on real labels. Capacity-based bounds are
vacuous for these models.

What we've understood since, in simplified form:

- **Implicit regularization**: among the infinitely many parameter settings that fit the training data, the optimization procedure (gradient descent from small
  initialization, SGD noise) finds particular ones, often low-norm or "simple" in some sense. For linear least squares with more features than points, gradient descent
  from zero converges to the **minimum-norm** interpolating solution (02.3), which can generalize well.
- **Double descent** (16.2): test error vs capacity rises toward the interpolation threshold (where the model can just barely fit the data, with a huge-norm, wildly
  oscillating solution) and falls again beyond it, as the minimum-norm solution among many interpolators gets smoother. The lab reproduces it with random features and
  plain linear algebra, no neural network required.
- **Benign overfitting**: under some conditions, fitting noise exactly costs little, because the noise is absorbed by many directions that don't affect predictions much.

None of this makes regularization, validation or bias-variance thinking obsolete. It means **parameter count is a poor measure of capacity**, and that what the
training procedure prefers matters as much as what the model class allows.

## Things that will bite you

- **Trusting differences smaller than your test set can resolve.**
- **Selecting among many configurations on one validation set** and reporting its score as if it were unbiased.
- **Distance-based methods on raw high-dimensional features.**
- **Rules of thumb** ("10 examples per parameter") applied to regimes where they don't hold.
- **Taking bounds literally** as sample-size calculators.
