# 16.1 Framing: what learning actually is

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: Part I, Module 10. Lab: [`lab_16_1_framing_baselines.py`](labs/lab_16_1_framing_baselines.py).*

The most expensive mistakes in machine learning happen before anyone writes a line of model code. Predicting the wrong
target. Optimizing a metric nobody cares about. Using features that won't exist at prediction time. Building a deep network
for a problem that a SQL query with a threshold solves at 95% of the value. Skipping the baseline, so nobody can tell whether
the fancy model is any good. I've watched people spend months on a model whose best version was worse than "predict the same
value as last week".

So Part III starts with framing: what it means to learn from data, how to turn a vague business wish into a precise learning
problem, and how to set up the baselines that keep you honest for the rest of the project.

## What "learning" means, formally

You have examples $(x_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \ldots, n$, drawn from some unknown distribution $\mathcal{D}$ over inputs and outputs. You want a function $f$ that
predicts $y$ from $x$ well on **new** examples from the same distribution. "Well" is measured by a **loss** $\ell(f(x), y)$, and the quantity you care
about is the **risk** (expected loss):

$$R(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}}[\ell(f(x), y)]$$

You can't compute it, because you don't know $\mathcal{D}$. What you can compute is the **empirical risk**, the average loss on your sample:

$$\hat{R}_n(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f(x_i), y_i)$$

**Empirical risk minimization (ERM)** picks the function with the lowest empirical risk from a chosen set of candidates, the **hypothesis class** $\mathcal{H}$
(all linear functions, all trees of depth 5, all networks of a given architecture):

$$\hat{f} = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \hat{R}_n(f)$$

Almost every algorithm in Parts III and IV is ERM with a particular $\mathcal{H}$, a particular $\ell$, often a regularization term, and a particular
optimizer. Linear regression: linear $\mathcal{H}$, squared loss, closed form (02.3). Logistic regression: linear logits, log loss, gradient methods (17.3).
A neural network: a huge $\mathcal{H}$, cross-entropy, SGD (03.3).

The central question of the next two lessons: $\hat{R}_n(\hat{f})$ is what you minimized, $R(\hat{f})$ is what you care about, and they're not the same.
The gap between them is **generalization**, and it's why we evaluate on data the model hasn't seen (18.1).

## The kinds of learning problems

- **Supervised**: labeled pairs $(x, y)$.
  - **Regression**: $y$ is a number (price, demand, time to delivery).
  - **Classification**: $y$ is a category (fraud or not, which product category, which digit). Binary, multiclass, multilabel (several labels at once).
  - **Ranking**: order items by relevance (search results, recommendations, 23.2).
  - **Structured prediction**: sequences, trees, segmentation masks.
- **Unsupervised**: only $x$. Find structure: clusters (21.1), low-dimensional representations (21.3), density and anomalies (23.3).
- **Self-supervised**: create labels from the data itself (predict the next word, a masked patch, whether two views are of the same image). The engine behind
  foundation models (30.1, 35.2).
- **Reinforcement learning**: learn actions from rewards through interaction (Part VII).
- **Semi-supervised and weak supervision**: few labels plus lots of unlabeled data, or noisy labels from heuristics.

## Framing a business problem

The steps I go through before any modeling. They also become the first half of the design doc in 47.1.

**1. What decision will this change?** A model is only valuable if someone acts on its output. "Predict churn" is not a decision. "Decide which customers get a
retention call this week, given a team that can make 500 calls" is. The decision determines almost everything else: which errors matter, what threshold to use,
how often to predict.

**2. Is ML even needed?** Try the simplest possible answer first: a rule, a lookup, a SQL query, a heuristic an expert would use. ML is justified when the pattern
is too complex for rules, the rules would change too often, or the scale is too big for humans. It's not justified because it sounds better in a slide deck.

**3. What exactly is the target?** This is where most projects quietly go wrong. "Churn" could mean no purchase in 30 days, no purchase in 90 days, a cancelled
subscription, or a closed account. Each definition produces a different model and a different business outcome. Define it precisely, with a time window, in
writing, and check with the people who'll use it. The lab shows how much the churn rate (and which customers count as churners) changes with the window.

**4. When is the prediction made, and what's known at that moment?** The **prediction time** fixes which features are legitimate. Anything that happens after it
is **leakage** (18.4): it will make offline metrics look amazing and the production model useless. For each feature, ask "would I have this value, with this
content, at the moment of prediction?".

**5. What does an error cost?** A false positive in fraud detection annoys a customer; a false negative loses money. In medical screening, the asymmetry is much
larger. Costs determine the metric (18.2) and the threshold (18.3).

**6. What's the baseline?** Before training anything, compute the performance of trivial predictors:

- Regression: predict the mean (or median, for absolute error) of the training target. Or the last known value, for time series (23.1).
- Classification: predict the majority class; or predict the class prior as a probability (the best constant for log loss).
- A simple rule or heuristic that a domain expert would use.
- The current system, if one exists.

A model that doesn't beat the right baseline by a margin that matters isn't done, however sophisticated. In this lesson's lab, a one-line physics rule
(CO2 is proportional to fuel burned) beats a linear regression on the fuel consumption data. That's not embarrassing, it's information: the baseline told us
the model was missing something (fuel type changes the grams of CO2 per litre), and with that one domain feature the error drops from about 18 g/km to under 1. And the baseline tells you what "good" means: 97%
accuracy is terrible if 98% of transactions are legitimate.

**7. How will you know it works in production?** Offline metrics are a proxy. The real test is the effect on the decision (an A/B test, 05.3, 50.2), and the
monitoring that tells you when it stops working (51.1).

## Proxies and Goodhart's law

Often you can't measure the real target, so you predict a **proxy**: clicks instead of satisfaction, arrests instead of crime, spend instead of health need.
Proxies can be fine, and they can be disastrous. "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" (Goodhart's law, via Strathern). A
recommender optimized for clicks learns clickbait. A widely cited 2019 study (Obermeyer et al.) found that a healthcare algorithm using past medical costs as a
proxy for medical need systematically underestimated the needs of patients who had historically had less access to care. The model was accurate at predicting
its target; the target was wrong for the decision. Question every proxy, and look at who it fails (54.1).

## The i.i.d. assumption, and where it breaks

ERM's promise (low empirical risk implies low true risk) assumes training and future examples come from the same distribution, independently. In practice:

- **Time**: the world changes. A model trained on last year's data predicts next year (distribution shift, 51.1). Evaluate on the future, not a random split
  (18.1).
- **Groups**: rows from the same customer, patient or device are correlated. Split by group (18.1).
- **Feedback**: the model's decisions change the data it will be trained on next (a fraud model only gets labels for transactions it didn't block, 05.4, 47.2).
- **Selection**: your training data covers who you served, not who you'll serve.

Every one of these makes offline evaluation optimistic. Keeping them in mind from the framing stage is cheaper than discovering them in production.

> **Scar tissue.** A friend who runs a small gym asked me for a model to predict which members would cancel. It scored beautifully and was
> useless: the members it ranked as most likely to leave had already decided. They'd moved away, changed jobs, or asked to cancel and been
> told to wait until the end of the month. Nobody could keep any of them. The question he needed answered was which members would respond
> to a call or a discount, a different target, different data, and arguably a different model. I'd answered the question I could, not the
> one he had.

## Things that will bite you

- **A vague target** that means different things to different people.
- **Features unavailable at prediction time** (leakage).
- **No baseline**, or a baseline that's too weak (majority class on a balanced problem, the mean on a time series with a trend).
- **Proxies optimized into nonsense.**
- **Random splits for temporal or grouped data.**
- **Building ML when a rule would do.**
