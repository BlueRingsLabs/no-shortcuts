# 22.1 Feature engineering and pipelines

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 10.1 to 10.3, 16.1, 18.1, 18.4, 20.4. Lab: [`lab_22_1_features_pipelines.py`](labs/lab_22_1_features_pipelines.py).*

Here's an uncomfortable truth about applied machine learning: on tabular problems, the features matter more than the model. Swapping a random forest
for gradient boosting buys you a point or two; figuring out that "days since the customer's last order" matters, and computing it correctly as of the
prediction date, can buy you ten. 16.1 made this point with fuel types. This lesson makes it systematically: what to do with each kind of raw data,
and how to package all of it into a **pipeline** that computes exactly the same features in training and in production, fitted only on the data it's
allowed to see.

The second half of that sentence is where most real bugs live. Most of the "the model is worse in production" problems I've been asked to debug were
caused by features computed differently at serving time, not by any modeling mistake. The pipeline is the defense.

## The pipeline is the model

A trained model isn't the estimator's weights. It's the whole function from raw input to prediction: imputation, encoding, scaling, feature construction and the
estimator, with every fitted parameter (means, category lists, encodings, vocabulary) learned from the training data. In scikit-learn that function is a `Pipeline`,
usually with a `ColumnTransformer` routing each column type to its own preprocessing:

```python
pre = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler()), numeric_cols),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="infrequent_if_exist", min_frequency=20), categorical_cols),
    ("hi_card", TargetEncoder(), high_cardinality_cols),
])
model = make_pipeline(pre, HistGradientBoostingClassifier())
```

Three rules follow, and they answer the module's self-check:

1. **Fit everything inside cross-validation.** `cross_val_score(model, X, y)` refits the scaler, the encoders and the imputer on each training fold. Fit the scaler on all the data first and the
   validation folds have contributed their means and variances to the training features. For a `StandardScaler` on plenty of data the leak is tiny; for target encoding, feature selection,
   PCA on few samples or oversampling, it's large (18.1, 18.4). The rule has to be the same for all of them because you won't reliably tell which is which, and because of rule 2.
2. **Serve the fitted pipeline, not a reimplementation.** If production recomputes the scaling with a hand-written formula, or one-hot encodes in the order the categories happen to appear today,
   the model sees different inputs than it was trained on: **training-serving skew**. Serialize the whole fitted pipeline and version it with the model (48.1).
3. **Unseen values will arrive.** A new country, a new product category, a missing field that was never missing in training. Decide in the pipeline what happens (`handle_unknown`, an "other" bucket,
   an imputer) instead of finding out from a stack trace at 3 a.m.

`set_output(transform="pandas")` keeps column names through the pipeline, which makes debugging and interpretation (22.3) far less painful. Write custom steps as small classes with `fit` and
`transform` (or `FunctionTransformer` for stateless ones) so they participate in the same rules.

## Numeric features

- **Scaling** (standard, robust, min-max): required for distance- and gradient-based models (k-NN, SVMs, linear models with penalties, neural networks), irrelevant for trees (20.1).
- **Skew**: log, Box-Cox or Yeo-Johnson for right-skewed amounts (income, prices, counts) in linear models; a `QuantileTransformer` maps anything to uniform or normal ranks. Trees don't care.
- **Clipping / winsorizing** extreme values when outliers are errors or when a linear model shouldn't extrapolate their effect.
- **Missing indicators** alongside imputation, because missingness is often informative (20.4 showed the linear model needing one).
- **Binning**: rarely worth it for trees (they bin for you) and a lossy way to add nonlinearity to linear models; **splines** (`SplineTransformer`) do that job better.
- **Ratios, differences and interactions from domain knowledge**: debt-to-income, price per square meter, fuel type × consumption (16.1), "amount relative to this customer's usual amount". Trees can
  approximate a ratio only with many splits; handing it to them directly is often the single best improvement available.

## Categorical features

- **One-hot** for low cardinality; `min_frequency` or `max_categories` to fold rare levels into an "infrequent" bucket; `handle_unknown` for new levels.
- **Ordinal encoding** when there's a real order (sizes, education levels), or for trees when you're fine with arbitrary splits.
- **Target encoding** for high cardinality, out-of-fold with smoothing (18.4). scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder` cross-fits internally during `fit_transform`.
- **Native categorical support** in boosting libraries (20.4).
- **Frequency / count encoding**: replace a category by how common it is. Cheap, leak-free, sometimes surprisingly predictive.
- **Hashing** (`FeatureHasher`): map categories into a fixed number of columns with a hash function. No vocabulary to store, handles unseen values, collisions are the price. Standard for
  very high-cardinality and streaming settings (ad tech).

## Dates and times

A raw timestamp is almost never a good feature (it's a trend proxy that won't extrapolate, 20.1). Extract what matters:

- **Components**: hour, day of week, month, day of month, is-weekend, is-holiday (with a holiday calendar for the right country).
- **Cyclical encoding** for linear and distance-based models: hour 23 and hour 0 are adjacent, so use $\sin(2\pi h/24)$ and $\cos(2\pi h/24)$. Trees handle raw components fine.
- **Elapsed time**: days since signup, since last order, since last login. Usually the strongest features in churn and propensity models, and the easiest to compute wrong (next section).
- **Time zones**: decide whether "hour of day" means the user's local time or the server's UTC, and be consistent. A model trained on UTC hours serving users in five time zones has learned
  something about your servers.

## Aggregates, and doing them as of the right time

Most of the signal in business data is in **related tables**: a customer's orders, a card's transactions, a device's logs. You turn them into per-entity features by aggregating over
windows: count of orders in the last 30/90/365 days, total spend, mean basket size, days since last order, share of spend by category, number of distinct devices, trend (last 30 days vs the
previous 30).

Every one of these must be computed **as of the prediction time**, using only data before it (18.4's point-in-time rule). The standard setup for training data is a set of **cutoff dates**: for
each cutoff, compute features from data before it and the label from a window after it, then stack several cutoffs to get more rows. The lab builds exactly this for the shop: "will this
customer order in the next 60 days?", with features from before the cutoff and validation on a *later* cutoff (18.1's temporal split). Feature stores (49.1) exist to make this systematic and
to serve the same aggregates online.

## Text, briefly

For text fields in a tabular model (a product title, a support ticket, a free-text reason): TF-IDF over words or character n-grams into a linear model is a strong baseline (34.2 covers it
properly); `HashingVectorizer` avoids storing a vocabulary; sentence embeddings from a pretrained model (41.1) capture meaning rather than words. Any of them slots into a `ColumnTransformer`.

## Feature selection

With a strong regularized model or a tree ensemble, selection is less important than people think; it's mostly for speed, cost (features cost money to compute and maintain) and interpretability.
Options: filter by variance or mutual information (06.2), wrapper methods like recursive feature elimination, embedded methods (L1, 17.2; tree importance, with its bias, 20.1). Whatever you use
runs **inside** the cross-validation (18.1's noise example). And a feature that's expensive, fragile or slow to arrive in production deserves to be dropped even if it helps a little offline.

> **Scar tissue.** For about a week, a small classifier I ran on my own VPS served predictions computed on scrambled inputs. Training
> one-hot encoded a categorical column with pandas, which orders categories by what it sees; the API rebuilt the encoding from each
> request, and a new category shifted every column after it by one. No error, because the vector still had the right length. A fitted
> encoder, saved with the model and used in both places, is the fix. A test that scores the same rows through both paths is the alarm.

## Things that will bite you

- **Preprocessing fitted on all the data** before CV.
- **Training-serving skew**: features recomputed differently in production.
- **Aggregates computed with data after the cutoff.**
- **Raw timestamps and IDs as features.**
- **New categories in production** with no plan for them.
- **UTC hours for local behavior.**
- **A feature that exists offline but not online** (or arrives two hours late in production).

## Exercises

1. Why must scaling be fit inside the cross-validation loop? Give the statistical reason and the engineering reason.
2. Design the features and the label for "will this subscriber cancel in the next 30 days?" from a subscriptions table, a payments table and a support tickets table. State the cutoff logic.
3. When is one-hot encoding better than target encoding, and vice versa?
4. Why is hour-of-day encoded as sine and cosine for a linear model but left raw for gradient boosting?
5. A feature "number of support tickets in the last 7 days" is strongly predictive offline. In production, tickets are loaded into the warehouse with a 48-hour delay. What happens, and what do you do?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Statistical: the fitted parameters (mean, standard deviation) would include the validation folds, so the validation data influences the training features, and the CV estimate becomes (slightly,
   for scaling; badly, for data-dependent steps like target encoding or selection) optimistic. Engineering: in production the scaler is fitted once on training data and applied to new data; CV
   should simulate exactly that, and the only reliable way to guarantee the same behavior is to make the scaler part of the pipeline that is both cross-validated and deployed.

2. Pick cutoff dates (e.g. the first of each month). For each subscriber active at the cutoff: label = cancelled within the next 30 days. Features from data strictly before the cutoff: tenure,
   plan, price changes, payments in the last 30/90 days, failed payments, days since the last payment, tickets in the last 7/30/90 days, ticket categories, trend in usage. Exclude anything
   recorded after the cutoff (a cancellation reason, a refund triggered by the cancellation). Validate on later cutoffs than you train on.

3. One-hot: low cardinality, linear models that need a separate coefficient per level, no leakage risk. Target encoding: high cardinality, where one-hot produces thousands of sparse columns; it
   compresses each level to one number, at the cost of leakage risk (do it out-of-fold with smoothing) and of losing any structure other than the level's mean target.

4. A linear model treats a raw hour as a number on a line: 23 and 0 are maximally far apart and the effect is monotone. Sine and cosine put the hours on a circle, so adjacent hours are adjacent and a
   periodic pattern is expressible as a linear combination. Trees split raw hours into intervals and can carve out "22 to 2" with two splits, so they don't need it (it doesn't hurt either).

5. Offline, the feature was computed from the complete warehouse, so it includes tickets from the 48 hours before the cutoff. Online, those are missing: the feature is systematically lower, and the
   model underestimates risk for exactly the customers who just complained. Recompute the training feature as it would have been available (exclude the last 48 hours, or use the load timestamp
   instead of the event timestamp), or get a real-time source for tickets. Then re-validate.
