# 22.2 Hyperparameter optimization

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 18.1 (nested CV), 16.3 (selection optimism), 20.3, 20.4, 04.2. Lab: [`lab_22_2_hpo.py`](labs/lab_22_2_hpo.py).*

Hyperparameters are the settings you choose before training: the learning rate, the tree depth, the regularization strength, the number of neighbors.
Choosing them is an optimization problem, but an unpleasant one. The objective (a cross-validated score) is **expensive** (each evaluation trains
models), **noisy** (a different split gives a different score), has **no gradient**, and lives in a space mixing continuous, integer, categorical and
conditional parameters. This lesson covers the methods that work, the one that people use most and shouldn't (grid search), and the trap that catches
everyone: the more configurations you try, the more your best score lies to you.

Let me set expectations up front: on most tabular problems, tuning a reasonable model moves the needle by a point or two. Features (22.1) and
validation (18.1) move it more. Tune last, tune with a budget, and never let a tuning run be the reason your reported number is wrong.

## Grid search and why random search beats it

**Grid search** evaluates every combination of a few values per parameter. With 6 parameters at 5 values each, that's 15,625 fits, and it spends almost all of them on
redundant variation of parameters that don't matter.

**Random search** (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012) samples configurations at random. The argument for it is geometric: usually only a few hyperparameters matter much for a given problem (the
**low effective dimensionality** of the objective). With 9 trials, a 3 × 3 grid tries only 3 distinct values of the important parameter; 9 random trials try 9 distinct values of it. Random search
never wastes trials by holding the important parameter fixed while varying an irrelevant one. The lab simulates exactly this and random search finds better optima with the same budget.

Practical rules for random search:

- Sample scale-type parameters (learning rate, regularization, $C$, $\gamma$) **log-uniformly**. The difference between 0.001 and 0.01 matters as much as between 0.1 and 1.
- Set generous ranges first, look at where the good trials landed, and narrow.
- It parallelizes trivially, and you can stop it at any time with a valid result.

## Bayesian optimization

Random search ignores what it's learned. **Bayesian optimization** builds a cheap **surrogate model** of the objective from the trials so far and uses it to choose the next trial, balancing
**exploitation** (near the best so far) and **exploration** (where the surrogate is uncertain) through an **acquisition function**, typically expected improvement.

- **Gaussian process** surrogates (23.4) model the objective as a smooth random function with uncertainty. Excellent for a few continuous parameters and small budgets.
- **Tree-structured Parzen estimators** (TPE, the default in Optuna and Hyperopt) model the *distribution of good configurations vs bad ones* instead of the objective, handle categorical and conditional
  parameters naturally ("`max_depth` only if `booster == 'gbtree'`"), and scale better. The workhorse in practice.

With a budget of a few dozen trials, Bayesian optimization typically reaches a given quality in fewer trials than random search; with hundreds of cheap trials, random search catches up. The lab
compares them on a real model with the same budget and shows the honest result on our data, whichever way it falls.

## Stop bad trials early: multi-fidelity methods

Most trials are bad, and you can often tell early: after 50 boosting rounds, after 2 epochs, after training on 10% of the data. **Successive halving** starts many configurations on a small budget,
keeps the best fraction, gives them more budget, and repeats. **Hyperband** runs several successive-halving brackets with different trade-offs; **ASHA** is its asynchronous, parallel version. In
Optuna, a **pruner** (median, successive halving, Hyperband) kills a trial whose intermediate score is worse than the others' at the same step. scikit-learn has `HalvingRandomSearchCV`. For
neural networks, where one trial costs hours, this is the difference between a feasible search and an infeasible one (26.5). The lab counts how many trials the pruner kills and how much time it saves.

## The best score is optimistic

16.3 and 18.1 made the point; here it bites hardest. Each configuration's CV score is the true score plus noise. The best of $N$ noisy scores is biased upward, and the bias grows with $N$ and with
the noise. Run 500 trials and your best CV score is partly a measurement of your luck. The lab demonstrates it with a search over hyperparameters that *do nothing*: the best CV score climbs
with the number of trials while the held-out score stays flat.

What to do:

- **Keep a test set out of the search**, or use **nested CV** (18.1), and report that number, not the search's best.
- **Reduce the noise** of each evaluation: more folds, repeated CV, larger validation sets. The search then optimizes more signal and less luck.
- **Prefer simpler configurations** among those within noise of the best (the one-standard-error rule, 17.2).
- **Don't run the search forever.** Past a point, extra trials mostly fit the validation noise.

## What to tune, roughly in order of importance

| Model | Tune first | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Gradient boosting (20.4) | learning rate with early stopping; `num_leaves`/`max_depth`; `min_child_samples` | subsampling, column sampling, L2 |
| Random forest (20.2) | `max_features`; `min_samples_leaf` | `n_estimators` (just "enough") |
| Linear models (17.2) | regularization strength (log scale) | L1/L2 mix |
| SVM (19.3) | $C$ and $\gamma$ jointly (log scale) | kernel |
| k-NN (19.1) | $k$; distance and weighting | feature scaling choices |
| Neural networks (26.5) | learning rate (by far) | batch size, weight decay, architecture width/depth, schedule |

## Practicalities

- **Put the whole pipeline in the search** (22.1): preprocessing choices are hyperparameters too, and they must be refitted per fold.
- **Seeds and reproducibility**: seed the sampler, record every trial (Optuna's storage, or an experiment tracker, 48.1). A search you can't reproduce is a search you can't defend.
- **Budget in time, not trials**, when trial costs vary a lot.
- **Look at the results**, not just the winner: which parameters mattered (Optuna's importance plots), whether the optimum sits on the edge of a range (widen it), whether the top 10 trials
  agree (a flat optimum is good news).

## Things that will bite you

- **Grid search over 6 parameters.**
- **Linear sampling** of learning rates and penalties.
- **Reporting the best CV score** from the search as the model's performance.
- **Tuning on the test set**, directly or through repeated peeking.
- **Preprocessing outside the search pipeline.**
- **Weeks of tuning** on a model whose features haven't been looked at.

## Exercises

1. Explain with a picture why 9 random trials explore an important parameter better than a 3 × 3 grid.
2. A learning rate is searched uniformly on $[0.0001, 1]$. What fraction of trials fall below 0.01? What should you do instead?
3. Why does the best CV score among $N$ trials overestimate the chosen model's true performance, even if every CV estimate is unbiased?
4. Describe successive halving with 81 configurations, a minimum budget of 1 unit and a reduction factor of 3. How many total units does it use, compared with running all 81 to 81 units?
5. When would you prefer a GP-based Bayesian optimizer over TPE, and vice versa?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Project the trials onto the important parameter's axis. The grid's 9 points land on only 3 distinct values (each repeated 3 times, once per value of the unimportant parameter). The random trials
   land on 9 distinct values. If the objective depends mostly on that one parameter, random search has sampled it three times more densely at the same cost.

2. $0.01 / 1 \approx 1\%$ of the interval, so about 1% of trials, while the good region for many models is exactly $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$. Sample $\log_{10}(\text{lr})$ uniformly on $[-4, 0]$: each decade gets a quarter of the trials.

3. Each estimate is unbiased individually, but you *select* the maximum. $\mathbb{E}[\max_i(\mu_i + \epsilon_i)] \ge \max_i\mu_i$, with the gap growing with $N$ and the noise variance. The selected configuration's true score is
   $\mu_{\text{chosen}}$, which is below its observed score by (on average) the selection bias. It's the winner's curse.

4. Rung 0: 81 configs × 1 unit = 81. Keep 27, give each 3 units: 81. Keep 9 × 9 units: 81. Keep 3 × 27 units: 81. Keep 1 × 81 units: 81. Total 405 units, against 81 × 81 = 6,561 for the full run: about 16 times cheaper,
   at the risk of killing configurations that start slowly and finish strong (Hyperband hedges that with several brackets).

5. GPs: few (under ~20) continuous parameters, very expensive evaluations, small budgets, where a well-calibrated surrogate is worth its cubic cost in the number of trials. TPE: many parameters,
   categorical and conditional spaces, larger budgets and parallel workers, where GP surrogates are awkward or slow.
