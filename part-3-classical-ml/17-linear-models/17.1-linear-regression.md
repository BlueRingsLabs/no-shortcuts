# 17.1 Linear regression three ways

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 02.3 (least squares as projection), 04.4 (least squares as MLE), 05.1, 16.2. Lab: [`lab_17_1_linear_regression.py`](labs/lab_17_1_linear_regression.py).*

You've already derived linear regression twice: as an orthogonal projection (02.3) and as maximum likelihood under Gaussian
noise (04.4). The third way is the statistician's: linear regression as a tool for **inference**, where the question isn't only
"how well does it predict?" but "what does each coefficient mean, how sure are we about it, and can we trust that?". In ML circles
this part gets skipped, and then people present coefficient tables to executives as if they were facts about the world. They
aren't, unless the assumptions behind them hold, and checking those assumptions is what this lesson is about.

Linear regression is also the model I'd pick first for more problems than you'd expect: fast, stable, interpretable, easy to
monitor, and hard to beat when the relationship is roughly additive and you've engineered sensible features (16.1's fuel type
lesson). Understanding it deeply pays off in every model after it.

## The model

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_d x_{id} + \epsilon_i, \qquad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \text{ i.i.d.}$$

The OLS estimate is $\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ (computed with QR or SVD, never with the explicit inverse, 02.3).

## Inference: how sure are we about each coefficient?

Under the model's assumptions, $\hat\beta$ is itself a random variable (it depends on the noise in the sample), with

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta] = \beta, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$$

(Derivation: $\hat\beta = \beta + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\epsilon$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(A\epsilon) = A\,\sigma^2 I\,A^\top$, 04.3.) Estimate $\sigma^2$ by the residual variance
$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n - p}\sum_i r_i^2$, where $p$ is the number of coefficients including the intercept (dividing by $n - p$ makes it unbiased, the same idea as
Bessel's correction). Then:

- **Standard error** of coefficient $j$: $\text{SE}(\hat\beta_j) = \hat\sigma\sqrt{[(X^\top X)^{-1}]_{jj}}$.
- **t-statistic** for $H_0: \beta_j = 0$: $t = \hat\beta_j / \text{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$, compared with a Student-t with $n - p$ degrees of freedom (05.2).
- **Confidence interval**: $\hat\beta_j \pm t_{n-p,\,0.975}\,\text{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$.

`statsmodels` prints all of that in one summary table; scikit-learn doesn't (it's built for prediction, not inference). The lab computes the standard errors by hand and checks
them against statsmodels, and then checks by simulation that the 95% intervals actually cover the true coefficient 95% of the time, when the assumptions hold.

## Interpreting coefficients

$\beta_j$ is the expected change in $y$ for a one-unit increase in $x_j$, **holding all the other features constant**. That last clause does a lot of work:

- **Units matter.** A coefficient of 0.0001 on income in dollars and 10 on age in years says nothing about which matters more. Standardize features (or report effects
  for a meaningful change, like an interquartile range) before comparing magnitudes.
- **"Holding constant" may be impossible.** If engine size and cylinders are strongly correlated, "one more liter of engine at the same number of cylinders" barely
  happens in the data, and its coefficient is poorly determined.
- **Categorical features** enter as one-hot columns with one level dropped (the reference); each coefficient is a difference from the reference level. Change the
  reference and every coefficient changes, the predictions don't.
- **Log transforms**: with $\log y$ as the target, $\beta_j$ is approximately the relative change in $y$ per unit of $x_j$ (a coefficient of 0.05 ≈ +5%). With $\log x_j$ as a
  feature and a plain $y$, $\beta_j/100$ is the change in $y$ per 1% increase in $x_j$. With logs on both sides, $\beta_j$ is an elasticity: percent change
  in $y$ per percent change in $x_j$. The lab checks the log-target case numerically, because the approximation breaks for big coefficients: the exact effect is
  $e^{\beta_j} - 1$, and for $\beta_j = 0.5$ that's +65%, not +50%.
- **Interactions** ($x_1 x_2$) let the effect of one feature depend on another. 16.1's fuel-type × consumption feature was exactly this.
- **It's not causal** (05.4). A regression coefficient is a conditional association. It becomes a causal effect only under the assumptions of 05.4 (all confounders
  included, no mediators or colliders adjusted for). Most business regressions don't meet them.

## The assumptions, and how to check them

The coefficient estimates are unbiased under weaker conditions than the inference needs. What matters for what:

1. **Linearity** (in the parameters): the expected $y$ is a linear function of the features as given. Violation biases everything. *Check*: residuals vs fitted values
   and vs each feature; curves in those plots mean you're missing a transformation or interaction.
2. **Independence of errors**: violated by time series (autocorrelation) and grouped data (several rows per customer). The estimates stay unbiased; the **standard
   errors are too small**, often badly. *Fix*: cluster-robust standard errors, or models for the dependence.
3. **Homoscedasticity** (constant error variance): violated when errors grow with the prediction (revenue, prices, anything multiplicative). Estimates stay unbiased;
   standard errors are wrong. *Check*: residual spread vs fitted values. *Fix*: **heteroskedasticity-robust** ("sandwich", HC3) standard errors, or model $\log y$, or
   weighted least squares (02.3).
4. **Normality of errors**: needed only for exact small-sample t-tests; with moderate $n$, the CLT (04.3) takes care of it. The least important assumption, and the one
   people check most.
5. **No perfect multicollinearity**: otherwise $X^\top X$ is singular (02.2). Near-collinearity inflates standard errors. *Check*: the **variance inflation factor**
   $\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ comes from regressing $x_j$ on the other features. VIF above 5 or 10 is the usual warning.

The lab builds a heteroskedastic dataset and shows the classical 95% intervals covering the truth much less than 95% of the time, and the robust ones fixing it.

## Outliers, leverage and influence

Least squares squares the residuals, so a few points can dominate the fit (02.3, 04.4):

- **Outliers**: large residuals.
- **High leverage**: unusual feature values (far from the others in $x$-space). Leverage is the diagonal of the hat matrix $P$ (02.3): $h_{ii}$, between 0 and 1, averaging
  $p/n$.
- **Influence**: how much the fit changes if you remove the point. **Cook's distance** combines residual and leverage. A high-leverage point with a large residual can
  single-handedly rotate the regression line.

Don't delete influential points automatically: find out why they're unusual (a data error, 10.3, or a real and important case). If you need a fit that's robust to them,
use **robust regression**: the **Huber loss** is quadratic for small residuals and linear for large ones, combining least squares' efficiency with MAE's robustness
(04.4). The lab shows one bad point dragging OLS and barely moving Huber.

## Regression as a predictive model

For prediction, the inference machinery matters less and the usual ML workflow applies: train/validation/test splits (18.1), feature engineering (22.1), regularization
(17.2). A few habits specific to linear models:

- **Standardize** features for regularized models and gradient-based fitting (03.3).
- **Transform** skewed targets and features (log) when relationships are multiplicative; transform predictions back carefully (predicting $\log y$ and exponentiating gives
  the conditional median, not the mean, under log-normal errors; there's a correction factor).
- **Prediction intervals** are wider than confidence intervals for the mean: they include the noise $\sigma^2$ for a new observation. Don't confuse the two when telling someone
  how uncertain a forecast is.

> **Scar tissue.** In an econometrics assignment, a classmate fit a house-price regression and reported, with great confidence, that each
> extra room lowered the price. On another sample the sign flipped. Rooms and square meters moved together almost perfectly, so the model
> could shift weight between them freely, and it did. The predictions were stable; the story was noise. Coefficients answer causal
> questions only under assumptions nobody in that room had checked, least of all the two of us who wrote the report.

## Things that will bite you

- **Coefficients read as causal effects.**
- **Comparing coefficients across features in different units.**
- **Classical standard errors with heteroskedastic, grouped or time-correlated errors.**
- **Multicollinearity** making individual coefficients unstable (while predictions are fine).
- **A single influential point** driving the conclusion.
- **Confusing confidence intervals** (for the mean response) **with prediction intervals** (for a new observation).

## Exercises

1. Derive $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$.
2. A model of `log(price)` has a coefficient of 0.12 on `has_garden`. Interpret it precisely. What about a coefficient of 0.3 on `log(area)` in the same model?
3. You regress salary on years of experience and age, which are correlated at 0.95. The coefficient on age has a huge standard error and a sign you didn't expect. What's going
   on, and what are your options?
4. Residuals fan out as fitted values grow. What's wrong with the reported p-values, and what would you do?
5. Explain the difference between a 95% confidence interval for the mean response at $x_0$ and a 95% prediction interval at $x_0$. Which is wider and why?

   robust (HC3) standard errors, or model `log(y)` if the variance grows proportionally with the level, or weighted least squares.

5. The confidence interval covers the *mean* of $y$ at $x_0$: its width comes only from uncertainty in $\hat\beta$ and shrinks to zero as $n$ grows. The prediction interval covers a
   *new observation* at $x_0$: it adds the noise variance $\sigma^2$, so it's wider and never shrinks below roughly $\pm 1.96\sigma$.
