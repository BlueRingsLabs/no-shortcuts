# 17.3 Logistic regression and GLMs

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 17.1, 17.2, 03.2 (Hessians, Newton), 04.1 (distributions), 04.4 (MLE, cross-entropy). Lab: [`lab_17_3_logistic_glm.py`](labs/lab_17_3_logistic_glm.py).*

Linear regression predicts a real number with Gaussian noise around it. Most of what you'll be asked to predict isn't that. It's a yes/no (will this
customer churn, is this transaction fraud), a count (how many claims, how many failures per week), a positive skewed amount (claim size, time to
repair). You can shove those into linear regression, and people do, and the result predicts probabilities of 1.3, negative counts and
confidence intervals that make no sense.

**Generalized linear models** fix this with one idea: keep the linear predictor $\eta = x^\top\beta$, but pass it through a **link function** that
maps it to the right range, and use the likelihood of the right distribution. Logistic regression is the famous special case, and the one you'll
use most. It's also, not by accident, exactly a one-layer neural network with a sigmoid output (17.4 makes the connection explicit).

## Logistic regression

Model the probability of the positive class as

$$P(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(x^\top\beta) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x^\top\beta}}$$

Equivalently, the **log-odds** are linear: $\log\frac{p}{1 - p} = x^\top\beta$. That's the link function (the **logit**), and it's also how you interpret the coefficients.

### Interpreting coefficients

$\beta_j$ is the change in log-odds per unit of $x_j$, others fixed. Exponentiate it and $e^{\beta_j}$ is the **odds ratio**: the odds are multiplied by $e^{\beta_j}$
per unit of $x_j$. A coefficient of 0.7 means the odds roughly double.

What $\beta_j$ is **not** is a change in probability. The effect on $p$ depends on where you start: $\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j} = \beta_j\,p(1 - p)$, which is largest at $p = 0.5$
and tiny near 0 or 1. Doubling the odds takes 1% to about 2%, and 50% to 67%. When someone asks "how much does this feature change the probability?",
the honest answer is "it depends on the customer", and the usual summary is the **average marginal effect**: the average of $\beta_j\,p_i(1 - p_i)$ over the data.
Also: "odds" and "probability" are different words, and a surprising number of dashboards use them interchangeably. Don't let yours be one of them.

### Fitting: cross-entropy, gradient, Hessian

MLE for a Bernoulli model (04.4) gives the **binary cross-entropy** loss, the average negative log-likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}(\beta) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \big[y_i\log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\big], \qquad p_i = \sigma(x_i^\top\beta)$$

Using $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$ (03.1), the gradient and Hessian come out beautifully:

$$\nabla\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{n}X^\top(p - y), \qquad \nabla^2\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{n}X^\top DX, \quad D = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1 - p_i)\big)$$

The gradient has the same form as linear regression's, "features times residual", which is not a coincidence (it's true for every GLM with its canonical link).
The Hessian is positive semidefinite, so the loss is **convex** (03.3): no local minima, and any decent optimizer finds the global one.

Newton's method (03.2) on this loss has a name: **iteratively reweighted least squares** (IRLS). Each Newton step is exactly a weighted least squares
problem (02.3) with weights $p_i(1 - p_i)$ and a "working response" $z = X\beta + D^{-1}(y - p)$. Statisticians have fit GLMs this way since the 1970s; it
converges in a handful of iterations. The lab implements both gradient descent and IRLS and gets the same answer as statsmodels.

A useful property: with an intercept, the optimality condition for the intercept says $\sum_i p_i = \sum_i y_i$. The fitted probabilities average to the observed rate
on the training data. Logistic regression is, in that weak sense, calibrated by construction, which is one reason it's still the default in credit scoring
and medicine (18.3 has the stronger sense).

### Separation, and why the default is regularized

If some linear combination of the features separates the classes perfectly, the likelihood keeps improving as $\|\beta\| \to \infty$: sharper and sharper sigmoids,
probabilities closer to exactly 0 and 1. The MLE doesn't exist. Statsmodels complains; unregularized gradient descent just keeps growing the weights until
you stop it. It happens more than you'd think: small datasets, many features, a rare category that's always positive.

The fix is regularization (17.2): an L2 penalty gives a finite, unique solution. This is why scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` is **regularized by default**
(`C=1.0`, where `C` is the *inverse* penalty strength). Which also means its coefficients are shrunk, and not the MLE a statistics textbook reports. If you
want inference (standard errors, p-values), use statsmodels; if you want predictions, use scikit-learn with tuned `C`; if you use scikit-learn for
inference, you'll be wrong in a way nobody catches until an auditor does.

### Inference

The same machinery as 17.1, with the Hessian in place of $X^\top X$: $\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\hat\beta) = (X^\top \hat DX)^{-1}$ (the inverse Fisher information), giving
**Wald** standard errors, z-statistics and confidence intervals for coefficients and odds ratios. They're asymptotic (no exact t-distribution here), and they
behave badly near separation. Likelihood-ratio tests (compare the log-likelihoods of nested models, 05.2) are the more reliable alternative for
"does this group of features matter?".

### Imbalanced classes

With 1% positives, logistic regression is still consistent, and the intercept soaks up the base rate. If you **undersample** the negatives to speed up training
(common in ad-click and fraud pipelines), the slopes stay fine and only the intercept shifts, by exactly $\log(\text{sampling rate of negatives})$. Correct
it, or every probability you serve will be too high. Class weights have a similar effect. 18.4 covers imbalance in general; the point here is that
reweighting and resampling change what the probabilities mean.

## Generalized linear models

A GLM has three parts:

1. A distribution for $y$ from the **exponential family**: Gaussian, Bernoulli/binomial, Poisson, Gamma, inverse Gaussian, Tweedie.
2. A linear predictor $\eta = x^\top\beta$.
3. A **link function** $g$ with $g(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x]) = \eta$.

Each distribution has a **canonical link** that makes the math nicest (gradient = features times residual, convex loss), and a **variance function**
saying how the variance depends on the mean:

| Target | Distribution | Canonical link | Variance | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real, symmetric noise | Gaussian | identity | $\sigma^2$ (constant) | linear regression |
| Binary / proportion | Bernoulli / binomial | logit | $\mu(1 - \mu)$ | churn, fraud, conversion |
| Count | Poisson | log | $\mu$ | claims, failures, visits |
| Positive, skewed | Gamma | inverse (log in practice) | $\mu^2$ | claim size, duration, cost |
| Non-negative with exact zeros | Tweedie ($1 < p < 2$) | log | $\mu^p$ | total claim cost per policy |

The variance column is the real content. Poisson says the variance equals the mean; Gamma says the standard deviation grows proportionally with the mean
(constant coefficient of variation), which is the heteroskedasticity 17.1 warned you about, built into the model instead of patched with HC3.

Fitting is the same for all of them: maximize the likelihood, by IRLS or L-BFGS, with an optional penalty. Goodness of fit is measured by the
**deviance** (twice the log-likelihood gap to a saturated model), the GLM generalization of the residual sum of squares; the right metric to compare
count or cost models is the deviance of the appropriate distribution, not MSE (18.2).

### Counts, rates and exposure

Poisson regression with a log link: $\log\mathbb{E}[y] = x^\top\beta$, so effects are **multiplicative**, and $e^{\beta_j}$ is a rate ratio.

Real count data usually comes with **exposure**: policies observed for different lengths of time, stores open for different numbers of days, pages
with different traffic. What you want to model is the rate, $\mathbb{E}[y] = \text{exposure} \times e^{x^\top\beta}$, i.e.

$$\log\mathbb{E}[y] = \log(\text{exposure}) + x^\top\beta$$

$\log(\text{exposure})$ enters as an **offset**: a feature whose coefficient is fixed at 1. Forget it and the model learns "long exposure causes claims" and
attributes it to whatever features correlate with exposure. In scikit-learn, the equivalent is fitting `y / exposure` with `sample_weight=exposure`; in
statsmodels, pass `offset=np.log(exposure)` (or `exposure=`). The lab does it both ways and shows what happens without it.

### Overdispersion

Poisson forces variance = mean. Real counts are nearly always **overdispersed** (variance > mean), because of heterogeneity your features don't capture.
The point estimates survive; the standard errors are too small, the same failure mode as 17.1's heteroskedasticity. Check the Pearson statistic
$\frac{1}{n - p}\sum_i \frac{(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{\hat\mu_i}$: about 1 is fine, 3 means your standard errors are too small by a factor of about $\sqrt{3}$. Fixes: quasi-Poisson
(scale the standard errors), **negative binomial** regression (variance $\mu + \alpha\mu^2$), or robust standard errors. Too many zeros for any of those: zero-inflated
or hurdle models.

### Why not just log-transform the target?

Because $\log 0$ is undefined (so people add 1, and the results depend on the 1), and because $\mathbb{E}[\log y] \neq \log\mathbb{E}[y]$ (17.1's retransformation problem).
A Poisson or Gamma GLM with a log link models $\log\mathbb{E}[y]$ directly: multiplicative effects, zeros allowed, predictions on the original scale with no correction
factor. For counts and costs it's usually the cleaner choice.

## Things that will bite you

- **Reading logistic coefficients as changes in probability.** They're log-odds.
- **Using scikit-learn's default (regularized) `LogisticRegression` for inference.**
- **Undersampling negatives and serving uncorrected probabilities.**
- **Perfect separation** producing enormous coefficients and warnings you ignored.
- **Poisson without an exposure offset** when observation windows differ.
- **Trusting Poisson standard errors** on overdispersed data.
- **Evaluating count or cost models with MSE** when the model optimizes a deviance.
