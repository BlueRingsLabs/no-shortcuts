# 20.2 Bagging and random forests

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 20.1, 16.2 (bias-variance), 05.1 (bootstrap), 04.3 (variance of sums). Lab: [`lab_20_2_random_forests.py`](labs/lab_20_2_random_forests.py).*

20.1 ended with the tree's biggest flaw: high variance. Resample the training data and you get a different tree, with a different root split and
different predictions on a third of the test points. **Bagging** takes that flaw and turns it into the method: train many trees on different
resamples, and average them. The instability that makes one tree unreliable makes a crowd of them diverse, and diverse errors cancel. Add one
more trick, randomizing the features each split may use, and you have the **random forest**, which for twenty years has been the model you try
first on a tabular problem when you don't have time to think. It's still an excellent choice. I have shipped more random forests than I'd admit in
a room full of deep learning people.

## Averaging reduces variance, up to a point

Take $B$ identically distributed predictions $\hat f_1, \dots, \hat f_B$ with variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$. Their average has variance

$$\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac{1}{B}\sum_b \hat f_b\Big) = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\sigma^2$$

(expand the variance of the sum: $B$ variance terms and $B(B-1)$ covariance terms, 04.3). Two lessons in one formula:

- As $B$ grows, the second term vanishes, but the first doesn't. **Correlation sets the floor.** Averaging a thousand identical trees gives you one tree.
- The **bias** of the average is the bias of each member (16.2): averaging reduces variance, never bias. Bag a model that's too simple and you get a very stable, still-too-simple
  model. The lab bags decision stumps to show it.

So the recipe for a good ensemble: members with **low bias** (deep trees), **high variance** (which averaging will cut), and **low correlation** (so the averaging actually cuts it).

## Bagging

**B**ootstrap **agg**regat**ing** (Breiman, 1996): draw $B$ bootstrap samples (05.1: $n$ rows with replacement), fit a deep tree to each, predict by averaging (regression) or voting /
averaging class probabilities (classification). The bootstrap makes the trees different; deep trees keep the bias low.

A free side effect: each bootstrap sample leaves out about $(1 - 1/n)^n \approx e^{-1} \approx 36.8\%$ of the rows. For each row, the trees that didn't see it form a small ensemble that can
predict it honestly. That's the **out-of-bag (OOB)** estimate: a validation score without a validation set, close to what cross-validation would give, at no extra cost
(`oob_score=True`). Handy, with the usual caveat that grouped or temporal data needs grouped or temporal validation (18.1), which OOB doesn't do.

## Random forests

Bagged trees are still quite correlated: if one feature is strongly predictive, nearly every tree splits on it first, and the trees look alike. Breiman's random forest (2001) adds
one change: **at each split, consider only a random subset of `max_features` features**. The strong feature is sometimes unavailable, other features get their turn, the trees
become less correlated, and the $\rho\sigma^2$ floor drops. Each tree gets a bit worse; the forest gets better.

`max_features` is the forest's most important knob. Classic defaults: $\sqrt{d}$ for classification, $d/3$ for regression. (scikit-learn's regressor defaults to all features, i.e. plain
bagging, which is a conservative choice; try smaller values.) Lower values mean less correlation and more bias per tree. The lab measures the tree-to-tree correlation directly
as `max_features` changes.

The other knobs, in rough order of importance:

- `min_samples_leaf` (or `max_depth`): how deep each tree grows. Deep is usually fine (averaging handles the variance); on noisy data, a leaf size of 5 to 20 can help and makes the
  model much smaller.
- `n_estimators`: more is better or equal, with diminishing returns; see the next section.
- `max_samples`: bootstrap sample size. Smaller samples mean more diverse trees and faster training.
- `class_weight`: for imbalance, with 18.4's caveats.

**Extremely randomized trees** (`ExtraTrees*`) go further: pick split thresholds at random too, instead of optimizing them. Even less correlation, faster training, often
comparable accuracy.

## Why adding trees doesn't overfit

The module's self-check. As $B \to \infty$, the forest's prediction converges to the expectation of a single random tree's prediction over the randomness (bootstrap and feature
sampling), given the training data. Each added tree moves the average closer to that limit, reducing the $\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$ term. Nothing in the process fits the training data
*harder* as $B$ grows: tree 400 isn't trained to correct tree 399's mistakes. So test error decreases (noisily) toward a plateau and stays there; the lab plots it.

What *can* overfit in a random forest is each tree (too deep on noisy data, raising the variance the average starts from) and the forest's settings when you tune them on the test
set (18.1). The number of trees isn't one of the dangers; it's a compute budget.

Boosting (20.3) is different: each new tree is fitted to the current ensemble's errors on the training data, so more rounds do fit the training data harder, and past some
point the test error rises. That's why boosting needs early stopping and forests don't.

## Using forests well

- **Probabilities** are averages of leaf frequencies and are pulled toward the middle (18.3): calibrate if you need them.
- **Importance**: impurity-based importance inherits 20.1's bias. Use permutation importance on held-out data (22.3), and be careful with correlated features, which share (and dilute)
  importance.
- **Quantile regression forests** keep all the target values in each leaf instead of their mean, and give conditional quantiles, a cheap route to prediction intervals.
- **Size and latency**: 500 fully grown trees on a million rows is gigabytes of model and many microseconds per prediction. Leaf size, fewer trees, and `max_samples` fix that.
  The accuracy cost ranges from nothing (noisy data, where deep leaves were fitting noise anyway) to noticeable (clean data with fine structure; the lab's case). Measure it and let
  the latency budget decide.
- **No extrapolation**, same as trees.

Against gradient boosting, a tuned forest usually loses by a little on accuracy and wins on robustness: fewer ways to get the hyperparameters badly wrong, no early stopping to
manage, trivially parallel. A strong first model and a strong baseline for the second one.

## Things that will bite you

- **Bagging high-bias models** and expecting improvement.
- **Using the regressor's default `max_features`** without trying smaller values.
- **OOB scores on grouped or temporal data.**
- **Impurity importance** with high-cardinality or correlated features.
- **Deploying 2 GB forests** where a leaf-size change would give a 50 MB one with the same accuracy.

## Exercises

1. Derive $\operatorname{Var}(\bar f) = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$.
2. Show that the probability a given row is left out of a bootstrap sample of size $n$ tends to $e^{-1}$.
3. Why does reducing `max_features` sometimes improve a forest even though each tree gets worse?
4. Your random forest's test error keeps decreasing slightly from 300 to 1,000 trees. Should you worry about overfitting? What would you check instead?
5. You bag 500 logistic regressions on a large dataset. Why is the improvement over a single logistic regression tiny?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\operatorname{Var}\big(\sum_b \hat f_b\big) = \sum_b\operatorname{Var}(\hat f_b) + \sum_{b \neq b'}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat f_b, \hat f_{b'}) = B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2$. Divide by $B^2$: $\frac{\sigma^2}{B} + \frac{B-1}{B}\rho\sigma^2 = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$.

2. Each of the $n$ draws misses the row with probability $1 - 1/n$, independently: $(1 - 1/n)^n \to e^{-1} \approx 0.368$.

3. The ensemble's variance floor is $\rho\sigma^2$. Fewer candidate features per split make the trees less correlated (lower $\rho$), at the cost of slightly higher bias and variance per tree. When the
   correlation drop outweighs the per-tree loss, the forest improves.

4. No: more trees can't overfit in the sense of fitting noise harder; the decrease is the variance term shrinking. Check instead whether the extra trees are worth their cost (latency, memory),
   and whether the per-tree settings (leaf size, `max_features`) are well tuned by honest validation.

5. Logistic regression is a low-variance, stable model: on a large dataset, bootstrap samples produce nearly identical fits ($\rho \approx 1$, small $\sigma^2$). There's little variance to remove,
   and bagging can't reduce its bias. Bagging helps unstable models.
