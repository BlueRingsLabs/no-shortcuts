# 18.1 Splits and cross-validation that don't lie

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 16.2, 16.3 (selection optimism), 05.1 (standard errors), 05.2. Lab: [`lab_18_1_splits_cv.py`](labs/lab_18_1_splits_cv.py).*

Here's a thing that happens in every company that does ML. A model scores beautifully offline, gets deployed, and underperforms. Someone
blames "drift". Sometimes it is drift. More often, the offline number was never an estimate of production performance in the first place,
because the evaluation leaked, or tested the wrong thing, or was tuned on so many times that it memorized the test set. The model was fine; the
measurement was wrong.

This module is about measurement. It's the least glamorous part of ML and the part that most separates people who ship models that work from
people who ship models that demo well. I'd rather you get this right with logistic regression than wrong with a transformer.

## The one question

Every evaluation design answers one question: **how well will this model perform on the data it will actually see after deployment?**

Every rule below follows from taking that question literally. The test data has to be *like* future data, in the ways that matter, and it must
not have influenced any decision about the model. When a split violates either condition, the number it produces answers a different question,
usually a flattering one.

## Train, validation, test

- **Training set**: fit the parameters.
- **Validation set**: make decisions: hyperparameters, features, model family, early stopping, thresholds. Every decision made using it leaks a little
  information into the model, so its score becomes optimistic (16.3: the best of many looks better than it is).
- **Test set**: estimate final performance, **once**, after every decision is made. If you look at the test score, change something and look again, it's
  a validation set now, and you don't have a test set anymore.

This isn't bureaucracy. In the lab, you'll "tune" a model by trying 200 random feature subsets and keeping the best test score: it looks
several points better than it is, with no training-set leakage at all. Just repeated looking. Kaggle calls this "overfitting the public leaderboard", and
people who've done it know the feeling when the private leaderboard is revealed.

**How big?** The test set's job is to give an estimate precise enough to make decisions. For accuracy near $p$ on $n$ test examples, the standard error is
$\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ (05.1): about 1.4 points at $n = 1{,}000$ and $p = 0.8$, 0.4 points at $n = 10{,}000$. If you need to distinguish 91% from 92%, a 500-row test set can't do it,
however carefully you sampled it. With millions of rows, 1% is plenty; with a thousand, you need cross-validation.

## Cross-validation

**k-fold CV**: split the data into $k$ folds; for each fold, train on the other $k - 1$ and evaluate on it; average the $k$ scores. Every example is used for
evaluation exactly once and for training $k - 1$ times.

What it estimates, precisely: the expected performance of **your training procedure** on a dataset of size $\frac{k-1}{k}n$, not the performance of any particular
fitted model. (That's why, after CV, you refit on all the data: the final model is trained on slightly more data, so the CV estimate is slightly pessimistic,
which is the good direction to be wrong in.)

Choices:

- **k = 5 or 10** is the standard. Larger $k$ means less pessimistic bias (bigger training sets) and more compute; leave-one-out ($k = n$) is nearly unbiased but high
  variance and expensive except for models with closed-form shortcuts (ridge, 17.2).
- **Stratify** for classification (`StratifiedKFold`): keep class proportions equal in every fold, especially with rare classes.
- **Repeat** it with different shuffles (`RepeatedStratifiedKFold`) when the dataset is small, to average out the luck of the split.
- **Report the spread**, not only the mean. The standard deviation of the fold scores tells you how much the estimate would move with different data. The
  naive standard error $s/\sqrt{k}$ is too small, because the folds share training data and aren't independent; treat it as a lower bound.

## Splits must respect the structure of the data

Plain k-fold assumes the rows are i.i.d. When they aren't, random splitting puts near-copies of test rows into training, and the score measures memory
rather than generalization.

**Grouped data.** Several rows per patient, customer, device, user session, photographed object, document. If rows from the same group appear in training and
test, the model can recognize the group instead of learning the pattern, and the score is inflated, sometimes enormously. Split **by group**
(`GroupKFold`, `StratifiedGroupKFold`), so every group is entirely in training or entirely in test. The question to ask: "in production, will the model see new
patients/customers/devices, or only new rows from ones it has seen?" Split to match the answer. (Medical imaging has a long, embarrassing history of
papers with patient-level leakage. Don't add to it.)

**Temporal data.** In production you always predict the future from the past. Random splits let the model train on next month and test on last month, and use
future information it will never have. Split by time:

- A **forward-chaining** split (`TimeSeriesSplit`): train on everything before $t$, test on the window after it, move $t$ forward, repeat. Expanding window (all history) or
  sliding window (the last $N$ periods, if old data is stale).
- A **gap** (embargo) between training and test when features or labels span time. If the label is "churned within 30 days", the last 30 days of training labels
  overlap the test period. Leave a gap at least as long as the label horizon (`TimeSeriesSplit(gap=...)`). Finance calls this purging and embargo.
- The final test set is the **most recent** period. Always.

Temporal splits usually give worse numbers than random ones. That's not a problem with the split; that's the truth arriving early. Time series gets its own
treatment in 23.1.

**Other structure**: spatial data (nearby locations are correlated, so split by region), near-duplicates (dedupe before splitting, or at least group them;
10.3), multiple rows from the same underlying event. Same principle every time: find the unit that will be new at deployment and split on it.

## Everything learned from data goes inside the fold

Scaling, imputation, target encoding, feature selection, PCA, oversampling: anything that looks at the data to learn something must be fit **only on the training
fold**, then applied to the validation fold. Fit them on the full dataset before CV and the validation folds have influenced the training. The fix in
scikit-learn is to put everything in a `Pipeline` (22.1) and cross-validate the pipeline, never a pre-transformed matrix.

The canonical demonstration, which the lab reproduces: 100 samples, 10,000 pure-noise features, random labels. Select the 20 features most correlated with the label
on all the data, then cross-validate a classifier on them. CV reports far above chance. On noise. With the selection inside the pipeline, CV reports 50%, which is
the truth. This exact mistake appeared in published gene-expression papers (Ambroise and McLachlan, 2002, wrote the paper pointing it out).

18.4 catalogs the other forms of leakage. This one deserves its own section because it's the easiest to commit with good intentions.

## Nested cross-validation

If you use CV to choose hyperparameters and report the best CV score, that score is optimistic: it's the maximum of many noisy estimates (16.3 again). For
an honest estimate of the whole procedure *including tuning*, nest it:

- **Outer loop**: k-fold split for evaluation.
- **Inner loop**: on each outer training set, run the full hyperparameter search with its own CV, pick the best configuration, refit on the outer training set,
  and score on the outer test fold.

The outer scores estimate how well "tune then train" works on new data. It's expensive ($k_{\text{outer}} \times k_{\text{inner}} \times$ configurations fits). With a big
enough dataset, a simple train/validation/test split does the same job for much less.

## Comparing two models

"Model B beat model A by 0.4 points in CV" is a statement about noise until shown otherwise.

- Use the **same folds** for both models and look at the **paired differences** per fold. The fold-to-fold variation is mostly shared (hard folds are hard for
  both), so the paired comparison is much more sensitive.
- The folds aren't independent, so a plain paired t-test on $k$ differences is overconfident. The **corrected resampled t-test** (Nadeau and Bengio, 2003) inflates
  the variance by $\left(\frac{1}{k} + \frac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\right)$ instead of $\frac{1}{k}$. Use it with repeated CV.
- Or skip formal tests and look at effect sizes against the spread. If the difference is smaller than the fold standard deviation, don't build a story around it.

## Is your test set like production?

A clean split of historical data still estimates performance on *historical* data. If production data differs (new market, new product line, a changed
upstream system), the estimate is for the wrong distribution. Two practical checks:

- **Adversarial validation**: label training rows 0 and test (or recent production) rows 1, train a classifier to tell them apart, and look at its ROC-AUC. Near
  0.5: the sets are indistinguishable. Well above: they differ, and the classifier's feature importances tell you how. The lab does it.
- **Slice the evaluation** by time, segment, region and source. An average hides a model that's great on the old customers and bad on the new ones (18.2).
  Monitoring after deployment continues this job (51.1).

## Things that will bite you

- **Looking at the test set more than once.**
- **Random splits on grouped or temporal data.**
- **Preprocessing or feature selection fitted on all the data** before CV.
- **Reporting the best CV score from a hyperparameter search** as the expected performance.
- **Declaring a winner from a difference smaller than the fold-to-fold noise.**
- **A test set too small** to distinguish the models you're choosing between.
- **Deduplicating after splitting**, or not at all.

## Exercises

1. You have 3 years of daily sales for 500 stores and must forecast next month for existing stores. Design the split. What changes if the model will be used
   for stores that open next year?
2. Why is the average of 10-fold CV scores a slightly pessimistic estimate of the performance of the final model trained on all the data?
3. A model has 0.912 accuracy on a 2,000-example test set; another has 0.921. Compute the standard error of each estimate. Can you tell them apart? What would
   you do to compare them better?
4. A colleague standardizes the full dataset, then runs 5-fold CV. Is this leakage? How much does it matter, and when would it matter a lot?
5. Explain why the best score from a grid search over 500 configurations, evaluated by 5-fold CV, is optimistic even though CV was used.
