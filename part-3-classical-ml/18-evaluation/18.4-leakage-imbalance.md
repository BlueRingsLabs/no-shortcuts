# 18.4 Leakage, imbalance and other ways to fool yourself

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 18.1 to 18.3, 09.2 (slowly changing dimensions), 10.3. Lab: [`lab_18_4_leakage_imbalance.py`](labs/lab_18_4_leakage_imbalance.py).*

**Leakage** is when information that won't be available at prediction time sneaks into training or evaluation. The model learns to use it, the
offline score is spectacular, and in production the information isn't there and the model falls apart. It's the single most common reason
a model that "worked" doesn't, and it's insidious because nothing errors out. Every step is individually reasonable. The pipeline runs. The
number is just wrong.

I find it useful to think about leakage the way I think about security vulnerabilities: you won't find it by looking at the model, because
the model is doing exactly what it was trained to do. You find it by asking, for every piece of data that reaches the model, **"at the moment
this prediction is made, in production, would I actually have this value?"** That question, applied relentlessly, catches most of it. This lesson
covers the forms that survive casual inspection, then class imbalance (where the usual advice is half wrong), then a list of other ways to fool
yourself that I've collected over the years, most of them by committing them.

## The forms of leakage

**Target leakage: features that are consequences of the label.** The feature is computed from data recorded after, or because of, the outcome. Classic cases:
"number of calls to the retention team" predicting churn (people call because they're leaving), "treatment administered" predicting a diagnosis, "chargeback
flag" hiding inside a fraud feature set, a "days since last activity" computed at extraction time instead of at the prediction date. The symptom: one feature
with enormous importance and a score that's too good to be true. The fix: define the **prediction time** for every row, and build every feature using only data
from before it.

**Point-in-time errors in joins.** You join the customer table to the training events, and the customer table holds *today's* values: today's segment, today's
credit limit, today's address. For an event from last March, the model sees the future. This is where dimensional modeling pays off: with a type 2 slowly
changing dimension (09.2) you can join **as of** the event time, and pandas' `merge_asof`, DuckDB's `ASOF JOIN` and feature stores (22.1, 49.1) exist to do exactly this. If
your source systems overwrite history, you can't build honest training data for those features, and that's worth knowing before you promise a model.

**Train-test contamination.** The same (or nearly the same) example in both sets: duplicates in the raw data, oversampled copies made before splitting,
augmented versions of a test image in training, the same customer on both sides (18.1's group leakage). Deduplicate before splitting, split by the right unit, and
do all resampling and augmentation inside the training fold only.

**Preprocessing and encoding fitted on everything.** 18.1's feature selection example, and its most dangerous cousin, **target encoding**: replacing a category with
the mean label for that category. Computed on all the data, a high-cardinality feature (user ID, zip code, product ID) encodes each row's own label into its
feature. The lab shows naive target encoding of a purely random categorical getting a high CV score. The fix is out-of-fold encoding (scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder`
does cross-fitting internally) plus smoothing toward the global mean for rare categories.

**Leakage through artifacts and metadata.** The label is correlated with something about how the data was collected: positive cases came from one hospital's
scanner, fraud cases were exported in a separate batch with a different ID range, the file is sorted by label and a row index made it into the features, images
of one class were taken on a different day with different lighting. The model learns the artifact. Famous published cases include chest X-ray models learning
which hospital (and which portable scanner) the image came from. The defenses: drop IDs and row numbers, look at what the model relies on (22.3), and test on
data collected differently.

**Leakage through you.** Every time you look at test results and change something, information flows from the test set into the model (18.1). Also: features
engineered after exploring the whole dataset including the test part, hyperparameters "known to work" on this exact benchmark, and early stopping on the test set.

### How to catch it

- **Too good to be true** usually is. Compare with the best published or internal results, and with domain intuition. If predicting churn was that easy, someone
  would have done it.
- **One feature dominates** the importance ranking (permutation importance or SHAP, 22.3). Investigate it before celebrating.
- **Write down the prediction time** for every row and the available-at time for every feature. Make it a column. Assert it in the pipeline (50.1).
- **Test on a later time period**, collected by the real production path, before launch. Or run in **shadow mode** (50.2): score live traffic without acting,
  and compare with the offline estimate. The gap is your leakage (or drift) budget.
- **Adversarial validation** (18.1) between training data and live data.

## Class imbalance

Rare positives are normal: fraud, defects, churn, disease, clicks, security incidents (and yes, the intrusion-detection problem is imbalance plus an
adversary, which is its own kind of fun). The usual advice is "resample until it's balanced". Here's a more careful version.

**First, is it a problem?** Imbalance hurts when (a) you evaluate with a metric that hides it (accuracy, ROC-AUC alone; 18.2), (b) you use a 0.5 threshold (18.3), or
(c) there are so few positives in absolute terms that the model can't learn their pattern. (a) and (b) are evaluation and decision problems, not training
problems. Fix them with PR curves, cost-based thresholds and calibrated probabilities, and the model often needs no change at all. (c) is real, and the
real fix is more positive examples, better features, or a different framing (anomaly detection, 23.3).

**What the training-side tricks actually do:**

- **Class weights** (`class_weight="balanced"`) and **random oversampling/undersampling** change the effective prior the model trains under. For a well-specified
  probabilistic model they mostly shift the intercept (17.3): the **ranking** barely changes, the **probabilities** become miscalibrated. You could get the same
  decisions by moving the threshold on the unweighted model. The lab shows this: nearly identical PR-AUC, nearly identical minimum cost after tuning the threshold, much
  worse log loss.
- **Where they do help**: models with limited capacity or greedy fitting (trees and boosting on very skewed data can ignore a tiny class; weighting makes the splits
  pay attention), mini-batch training where most batches contain no positives, and undersampling for sheer **speed** on huge datasets (correct the intercept after).
- **SMOTE** and friends synthesize new positives by interpolating between neighbors. In my experience, and in several careful benchmarks, they rarely beat class weights
  or threshold moving with modern models, and they create one more way to leak (oversampling before the split puts synthetic copies of test points into training).
  Know it exists; don't reach for it first.
- **Whatever you do**, resample only the training fold, evaluate on the natural distribution, and recalibrate if anyone will read the probabilities.

## Other ways to fool yourself

A collection. Each one has happened in a real project I've seen or run.

- **Selection bias in the data.** You only have labels for cases that were acted on. Loans that were approved (you never learn whether rejected applicants would have
  repaid), transactions that weren't blocked, patients who were tested. The model learns about the selected population and is applied to everyone. Related:
  **feedback loops**, where the model's own decisions shape tomorrow's training data (the fraud model blocks a pattern, the pattern disappears from the labels, the
  next model forgets it). Keep a small random holdout that isn't acted on, if the business allows it; it's the only unbiased data you'll get.
- **Proxy labels.** "Clicked" isn't "was relevant", "was arrested" isn't "committed a crime", "was diagnosed" isn't "had the disease". The model optimizes the proxy,
  including its biases (54.1).
- **Label noise** in the test set caps the measurable accuracy and can reorder models. Audit a sample of test labels by hand, especially the model's confident "errors" (10.3).
- **Survivorship**: training only on customers, products or companies that still exist.
- **Simpson's paradox** across slices: a model better in every segment can look worse overall if the segment mix differs, and vice versa (05.4). Slice.
- **Seed variance.** Retrain the same model with a different random seed and the score moves. If your improvement is smaller than that spread, it isn't one. Report
  results over several seeds for anything stochastic (neural networks especially).
- **Benchmark overfitting.** A community reusing one test set for years collectively tunes to it. Recht et al. (2019) rebuilt the ImageNet test set from the same
  sources and every model lost several points. Your team's favorite internal benchmark ages the same way.
- **Cherry-picked examples** in the demo. Show random examples, and the worst ones.

> **Scar tissue.** The best model I ever built had an AUC of 0.97 at a hackathon and about 0.6 on live data. It predicted which issues in
> an open-source project would be closed within a week, and its most important feature was the number of comments, counted when the data
> was dumped, long after the issues were closed. Issues that got fixed had comments like "fixed in #123". The model had learned to read
> the ending of the story. We won the hackathon anyway, which taught me the wrong lesson for a while. Any score that's too good is a bug
> report until proven otherwise.

## Things that will bite you

- **Features without a timestamp** you can check against the prediction time.
- **Joining current dimension values** to historical events.
- **Target encoding, oversampling or feature selection before the split.**
- **Row IDs, file names or batch identifiers** in the feature set.
- **Resampling to fix a problem that was really a threshold or a metric.**
- **Evaluating on the selected population** and deploying on everyone.

## Exercises

1. A hospital readmission model uses "discharge disposition" (home, nursing facility, deceased) and "length of stay". The prediction is made at admission. Which of
   those is leakage? What if the prediction were made at discharge?
2. You're building a churn model on a customer table that's overwritten nightly. Which features can you build honestly for historical training rows, and what would
   you ask the data team for?
3. Explain why naive target encoding of a high-cardinality ID feature can give near-perfect training and CV scores while carrying no real information.
4. A colleague reports that class weights improved their fraud model's ROC-AUC from 0.912 to 0.914 and plans to deploy the weighted model's probabilities to the
   pricing team. What would you check, and what would you recommend?
5. Your credit model is trained on approved loans only. Describe the bias this introduces, and one way to get less biased data going forward.

1. At admission, both are leakage: discharge disposition and length of stay are only known at discharge (and "deceased" trivially predicts no readmission). At
   discharge time, both are legitimate features. Leakage is always relative to the prediction time, which is why you write it down.

2. Only attributes that don't change (or whose change history you can reconstruct from event logs: transactions, logins, tickets with timestamps). Current-state
   attributes (plan, segment, address, balance) are today's values and would leak for past rows. Ask for daily snapshots or a type 2 SCD going forward (09.2), or
   change-data-capture history (14.1), and meanwhile build features from timestamped event tables with an as-of cutoff.

3. With many categories and few rows each, the category mean is dominated by the row's own label: for a category with one row, the encoding *is* the label. The model
   learns "encoding high → positive". Inside CV it still works if the encoding was computed on all rows (including the validation fold). On new data with new or
   rare IDs, the encoding carries nothing. Out-of-fold encoding breaks the self-reference.

4. The 0.002 difference is likely within noise (bootstrap it, 18.2). More importantly, weighted training miscalibrates the probabilities (they'll be inflated toward the
   positive class), which matters for pricing. Recommend the unweighted model (or recalibrate the weighted one on natural-prevalence held-out data), check a reliability
   diagram, and choose any decision threshold from costs.

5. Selective labels: you only observe outcomes for applicants the old policy approved, who differ systematically from those it rejected. The model can't learn about the
   rejected region, and its error there is unknown (possibly large). Going forward: approve a small random sample of would-be rejections (a controlled, budgeted
   exploration), or at least vary the approval threshold over time, to observe outcomes across the whole population. Reject inference methods exist but can't create
   information that isn't in the data.
