# 18.2 Classification and regression metrics

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 18.1, 04.2 (conditional probability, Bayes), 05.1 (bootstrap), 06.1 (cross-entropy). Lab: [`lab_18_2_metrics.py`](labs/lab_18_2_metrics.py).*

A metric is a compression of your model's behavior into one number, and every compression throws something away. The choice of metric decides what
gets thrown away, which means it decides what your model gets optimized for and what the people reading your report will believe. Pick the wrong one
and you can have a model with 99.9% accuracy that never catches a single fraud, or a forecast with a great MAPE that systematically under-orders stock.
Both of those are real patterns I've seen more than once, and in both cases the metric was reported correctly. It just answered the wrong question.

This lesson is a tour of the metrics you'll use, what each one actually measures, and which ones lie in which situations.

## Classification: the confusion matrix

For a binary classifier at a fixed threshold, everything starts from four counts: true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), false negatives (FN).

| Metric | Formula | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | $\frac{TP + TN}{n}$ | What fraction is right? |
| Precision (PPV) | $\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$ | When it says positive, how often is it right? |
| Recall (sensitivity, TPR) | $\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$ | Of the actual positives, how many does it catch? |
| Specificity (TNR) | $\frac{TN}{TN + FP}$ | Of the actual negatives, how many does it leave alone? |
| False positive rate | $\frac{FP}{FP + TN} = 1 - \text{specificity}$ | How often does it bother a negative? |
| F1 | $\frac{2 \cdot P \cdot R}{P + R}$ | Harmonic mean of precision and recall |

**Accuracy fails with imbalance.** With 0.5% fraud, "never fraud" is 99.5% accurate. Always compare with the majority-class baseline (16.1), and with rare
positives don't lead with accuracy at all.

**Precision depends on the base rate; recall and specificity don't.** Recall and specificity are properties of the classifier on each class. Precision mixes them
with the prevalence $\pi$:

$$\text{precision} = \frac{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi}{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi + \text{FPR}\cdot(1 - \pi)}$$

That's Bayes' rule (04.2), and it's the base-rate fallacy in metric form. A test with 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity, applied where 0.1% are positive, has a
precision of about 9%: 91% of its alarms are false. Move the same model to a population with a different prevalence and its precision changes, even
though nothing about the model did. Remember this when someone compares precision across datasets.

**F1** is a single number when you care about both precision and recall and the positive class is rare. It ignores true negatives entirely (fine when negatives
are abundant and uninteresting), and weights precision and recall equally, which is a business decision nobody made consciously. $F_\beta$ weights recall $\beta$
times as much. Better still, when you know the costs of each error, compute the cost directly (18.3).

## Threshold-free metrics

The confusion matrix depends on a threshold. Scores-based metrics look at all thresholds at once.

**ROC curve and ROC-AUC.** Plot TPR against FPR as the threshold sweeps from high to low. The area under it has a clean interpretation: **ROC-AUC is the probability that a
randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative** (the Mann-Whitney U statistic, which the lab verifies). 0.5 is random, 1.0 is
perfect ranking. It depends only on the ranking, not on calibration, and it's insensitive to class balance, because TPR and FPR are each computed within one class.

That insensitivity is also its weakness. The module's self-check question: *why can ROC-AUC look great with 0.1% positives while the model is useless?* Because
FPR is divided by the huge number of negatives. With 1,000,000 negatives, an FPR of 1% is 10,000 false alarms, a tiny move along the ROC curve's x-axis. If there
are 1,000 positives and the model catches 800 of them at that threshold, the ROC point (0.01, 0.8) looks excellent, and precision is 800 / 10,800 = 7.4%. The ROC curve
spends most of its area in regions of FPR that no one would ever operate at, because the false positives would bury the investigators.

**Precision-recall curve and average precision (AP).** Plot precision against recall across thresholds. Precision uses the absolute number of false positives, so
it exposes the problem immediately. The baseline of a random classifier is a horizontal line at the prevalence $\pi$ (not 0.5), so an AP of 0.3 might be terrible or
excellent depending on $\pi$. For rare positives, where what you care about is the quality of the top of the ranking, prefer PR curves and AP, and report the
prevalence next to them.

**Partial measures.** Often only one operating region matters: recall at a fixed precision ("how much fraud do we catch while keeping 90% of alerts real?"),
precision at the top $k$ ("the investigators can review 200 cases per day"), or TPR at a fixed low FPR. These are usually the numbers that map to the business.

## Probabilistic metrics: proper scoring rules

If downstream decisions use the probabilities (expected-cost decisions, pricing, risk, anything combining predictions), you need to evaluate the probabilities,
not only the ranking.

- **Log loss** (cross-entropy, 06.1): $-\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \log \hat p_i(y_i)$. Heavily punishes confident mistakes: a 0.001 probability on the true class costs 6.9 nats.
- **Brier score**: $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(\hat p_i - y_i)^2$. Mean squared error on probabilities; bounded, less brutal with confident mistakes.

Both are **strictly proper scoring rules**: their expected value is optimized only by reporting your true belief. Accuracy and F1 aren't: they only care which side
of the threshold you are on, so a model can improve its accuracy by being more reckless with its probabilities, and a model that's badly miscalibrated
can have excellent accuracy and AUC. Calibration gets its own lesson (18.3). Rule of thumb: rank with AUC/AP, decide with costs, and check probabilities with log
loss or Brier plus a reliability diagram.

## Multiclass averaging

Precision, recall and F1 are per class. To get one number:

- **Macro**: average the per-class scores. Every class counts equally, so rare classes matter as much as common ones.
- **Micro**: pool all TP, FP, FN across classes first. For single-label multiclass, micro-F1 = accuracy.
- **Weighted**: average weighted by class support. Mostly reflects the big classes.

If the rare classes are the point (they usually are), report macro, and the per-class table. Always look at the confusion matrix for multiclass problems; the
pattern of confusions (which classes get mixed up) tells you more than any average.

## Regression metrics

The key idea: **each loss is minimized by a different summary of the conditional distribution**, so the metric decides what your model predicts.

| Metric | Formula | Optimal constant prediction | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| MSE / RMSE | $\frac{1}{n}\sum(y - \hat y)^2$ | mean | Punishes large errors hard; outliers dominate. RMSE is in the target's units. |
| MAE | $\frac{1}{n}\sum\lvert y - \hat y\rvert$ | median | Robust to outliers. |
| Pinball (quantile) loss at $\tau$ | $\sum \max(\tau(y - \hat y), (\tau - 1)(y - \hat y))$ | $\tau$-quantile | For prediction intervals and asymmetric costs. |
| MAPE | $\frac{1}{n}\sum\left\lvert\frac{y - \hat y}{y}\right\rvert$ | a low quantile-ish value | Undefined at $y = 0$; punishes over-forecasts more than under-forecasts. |
| $R^2$ | $1 - \frac{\sum(y - \hat y)^2}{\sum(y - \bar y)^2}$ | mean | Relative to predicting the mean; negative when worse than that. |
| Poisson / Gamma / Tweedie deviance | 17.3 | mean, with a variance model | For counts and costs; compare GLMs with these. |

Consequences:

- Train with MSE and evaluate with MAE (or the reverse) and you're optimizing one target and grading another. Sometimes that's fine; know you're doing it.
- **MAPE rewards under-forecasting**: an under-forecast can be off by at most 100%, an over-forecast by any amount. Optimizing MAPE biases forecasts low, which in
  inventory means stockouts. It's still the most requested metric in forecasting because business people understand percentages. Report it if you must;
  don't optimize it without thinking. Weighted alternatives (WAPE: $\sum|y - \hat y| / \sum|y|$) behave better.
- $R^2$ on a test set can be negative, and $R^2$ on a *different* test set isn't comparable, because the denominator depends on the variance of $y$ in that set.
  Prefer RMSE or MAE in real units for comparisons, and use $R^2$ as a description.
- Scale-free comparisons across series (forecasting many products) need scaled errors, like MASE (23.1).

## Uncertainty and slices

A metric computed on a test set is an estimate (18.1). Report its uncertainty: for simple proportions use the binomial standard error; for everything else (AUC, F1,
AP, RMSE) use the **bootstrap** (05.1): resample the test set with replacement, recompute, take percentiles. For comparing two models on the same test set, bootstrap
the *difference*, pairing the resamples.

And **slice**: the same metric by segment, region, device, time period, data source, and any group that fairness or the business cares about (54.1). A model
whose aggregate AUC improved while it got worse for the newest customers hasn't improved in any way that matters.

## The metric is not the goal

Every offline metric is a proxy for something the business cares about: fraud losses prevented, revenue, hours saved, patients correctly referred. Before
choosing a metric, write down the decision the model will support and what each kind of error costs (18.3 does this formally). Then pick the metric that best
tracks that, check it against an online outcome when you can (05.3, 50.2), and be suspicious whenever the offline and online numbers disagree. Goodhart's law
applies with full force: once the metric becomes the target, people (and optimizers) find ways to improve it that don't improve the thing it was measuring.

## Things that will bite you

- **Accuracy on imbalanced data.**
- **Comparing precision across populations with different base rates.**
- **ROC-AUC as the headline metric** for rare-positive problems.
- **Average precision reported without the prevalence.**
- **Optimizing MAPE** and wondering why forecasts run low.
- **Macro vs micro confusion**, especially in papers and leaderboards.
- **A single number without an interval, or without slices.**

## Exercises

1. A disease has prevalence 1 in 500. A test has sensitivity 95% and specificity 98%. What fraction of positive tests are true positives?
2. Prove that the constant minimizing MAE is the median, and the one minimizing MSE is the mean.
3. A fraud model has ROC-AUC 0.97 on a dataset with 0.2% fraud. The fraud team can investigate 500 alerts per day out of 1,000,000 transactions (2,000 of which are
   fraud). What would you compute to tell them whether the model is useful?
4. Why is micro-averaged F1 equal to accuracy in single-label multiclass classification?
5. Tomorrow's demand is 50 or 150 with equal probability. Which constant forecast minimizes the expected MSE, the expected MAE, and the expected MAPE?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Per 100,000 people: 200 sick, 190 detected; 99,800 healthy, 2% = 1,996 false positives. Precision = 190 / 2,186 ≈ 8.7%. Over 90% of positive results are false.

2. MAE: $\frac{d}{dc}\sum|y_i - c| = -\sum \text{sign}(y_i - c)$, which is zero (or changes sign) when as many points are above $c$ as below: the median. MSE: $\frac{d}{dc}\sum(y_i - c)^2 = -2\sum(y_i - c) = 0 \Rightarrow c = \bar y$.

3. Precision among the top 500 scores per day (how many alerts are real fraud), and recall at that cutoff (what fraction of the 2,000 daily frauds those 500
   alerts catch; at most 25% by construction), ideally in money: fraud value caught per day vs investigation cost. Plus a bootstrap interval. ROC-AUC says
   nothing about the top 0.05% of the ranking, which is the only part they'll ever use.

4. In single-label multiclass, every error is simultaneously a false positive (for the predicted class) and a false negative (for the true class). So total
   FP = total FN = number of errors, micro-precision = micro-recall = correct / total = accuracy, and their harmonic mean is the same number.

5. MSE: the mean, 100. MAE: any value between 50 and 150 (every median works). MAPE: for $c \in [50, 150]$ the expected error is
   $\frac{1}{2}\frac{c - 50}{50} + \frac{1}{2}\frac{150 - c}{150}$, whose slope $\frac{1}{100} - \frac{1}{300}$ is positive, so the minimum is at $c = 50$: MAPE forecasts the *low* outcome, because
   the same absolute miss costs three times as much percentage when the actual is 50. Optimize MAPE and you systematically under-forecast.
