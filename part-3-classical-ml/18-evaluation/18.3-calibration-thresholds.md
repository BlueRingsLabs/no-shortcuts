# 18.3 Calibration, thresholds and costs

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 18.2, 17.3, 04.2 (Bayes' rule). Lab: [`lab_18_3_calibration.py`](labs/lab_18_3_calibration.py).*

A classifier gives you a score. A business needs a decision: block the transaction or not, call the customer or not, send the case to a
doctor or not. Between the two sit two questions that most ML courses skip, and most of the broken models I've been asked to look at got at
least one of them wrong: **can the score be read as a probability?** and **where should the threshold be?**

The default answers, "sure" and "0.5", are wrong so often that you should assume they're wrong until you've checked.

## Calibration

A classifier is **calibrated** if, among all the cases where it says 0.8, about 80% are positive. Formally, $P(y = 1 \mid \hat p = p) = p$ for every $p$.

Calibration is separate from discrimination. A model that ranks perfectly can be badly calibrated (18.2's "reckless" model), and a perfectly calibrated
model can be useless (predict the base rate for everyone: calibrated, zero discrimination). You want both, and they're fixed by different means.

**Why it matters**: any time probabilities are used as numbers rather than only for ranking. Expected-cost decisions (below), expected revenue
(probability × value), risk scores shown to doctors or loan officers, combining the outputs of several models, and thresholds that have to survive
retraining. If the numbers mean nothing, all of those are wrong in ways that no ranking metric will reveal.

### Measuring it

- **Reliability diagram**: bin the predictions (say 10 bins by predicted probability, or equal-count bins), and plot the mean predicted probability against the
  observed positive rate in each bin. Calibrated = on the diagonal. Below the diagonal: overconfident at high scores. Show the bin counts too; a bin with 12
  examples tells you nothing.
- **Expected calibration error (ECE)**: the count-weighted average gap between predicted and observed in each bin. Popular, and flawed: it depends on the binning,
  it's biased upward for small samples, and a model can game it. Use it for tracking, not as a target.
- **Proper scoring rules** (18.2) measure calibration and discrimination together. The Brier score decomposes exactly (Murphy, 1973) into
  $\text{reliability} - \text{resolution} + \text{uncertainty}$: the calibration error, minus how much the predictions separate the outcomes, plus the base-rate
  variance. The lab verifies the decomposition.
- **Calibration-in-the-large**: mean prediction vs observed rate. The first thing to check after any deployment; a gap here means the base rate moved.

### Which models are calibrated?

- **Logistic regression**: calibrated by construction on average (17.3), usually well calibrated overall when the model is reasonably specified.
- **Naive Bayes** (19.2): badly overconfident. Its independence assumption multiplies correlated evidence as if it were independent, pushing everything to 0 and 1.
- **SVMs** (19.3): produce margins, not probabilities. Wrap them in `CalibratedClassifierCV` (scikit-learn 1.9 deprecated the old `SVC(probability=True)`,
  which did the same thing internally).
- **Random forests** (20.2): underconfident at the extremes. Averaging trees rarely produces exactly 0 or 1, so predictions get pulled toward the middle.
- **Gradient boosting** (20.3): with log loss, often decent; varies with regularization and early stopping.
- **Modern neural networks**: overconfident, famously (Guo et al., 2017), and more so the bigger they are. Temperature scaling fixes most of it.
- **Anything trained with class weights, resampling, or focal loss**: miscalibrated by design, because the training distribution isn't the real one (17.3's
  intercept shift is the simplest case).

### Fixing it: recalibration

Fit a monotone map from the model's score to a calibrated probability, **on held-out data** (a calibration set, or cross-validation; fitting it on the training
data just learns the training set's overconfidence):

- **Platt scaling**: logistic regression on the score (or the logit), two parameters. Works with little data; assumes the distortion is sigmoid-shaped.
  Feed it log-odds rather than probabilities that are already squashed against 0 and 1, or it can only half-fix them (the lab shows this).
- **Isotonic regression**: the best monotone step function. Flexible, needs more data (thousands of examples), can overfit, and creates ties that slightly change the
  ranking.
- **Temperature scaling** for multiclass: divide all logits by a single $T$ fitted on validation log loss (17.4). One parameter; doesn't change the argmax, so
  accuracy is untouched.

In scikit-learn, `CalibratedClassifierCV(model, method="sigmoid" | "isotonic", cv=5)` does it with cross-validation, and `CalibrationDisplay` draws the diagram.

## Thresholds and costs

Suppose the probability is calibrated. When should you act? Put numbers on the outcomes: a false positive costs $C_{FP}$ (an investigation, an annoyed customer),
a false negative costs $C_{FN}$ (the fraud gets through, the disease goes untreated). Correct decisions cost nothing (or fold their benefits into the costs). For a
case with probability $p$:

- Act: expected cost $(1 - p)\,C_{FP}$.
- Don't act: expected cost $p\,C_{FN}$.

Act when $p\,C_{FN} > (1 - p)\,C_{FP}$, i.e. when

$$p > t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

That's the whole theory. If a missed fraud costs 20 times more than a false alarm, $t^* = 1/21 \approx 0.048$, nowhere near 0.5. **The 0.5 threshold is optimal only when the
two errors cost the same**, which almost never happens. And the formula needs **calibrated** probabilities: it's the reason calibration matters for
decisions.

Practical versions:

- **Costs that vary per example** (fraud amount, customer value): the rule becomes "act if $p \cdot \text{loss if missed} > \text{cost of acting}$", evaluated per case.
- **Capacity constraints** ("we can review 300 cases a day"): the threshold is whatever score gives 300 cases; the metric that matters is precision in the top 300 (18.2).
- **Choosing empirically**: when costs are known, sweep the threshold on a *validation* set and pick the one minimizing total cost. Never choose it on the test set.
  When costs are unknown, draw the precision-recall or cost curve for the stakeholders and let them pick the trade-off. Writing down their choice is worth
  more than any metric.
- **A reject option**: when $p$ is close to the threshold, send the case to a human. Coverage falls, accuracy on the automated cases rises. For high-stakes decisions
  this is often the right product design.

### When the base rate changes

A model trained where 10% are positive and deployed where 2% are positive will overestimate every probability. If only the prior changed (the class-conditional
distributions $p(x \mid y)$ are the same, called **prior shift** or label shift), Bayes' rule gives the exact correction:

$$\text{odds}_{\text{new}}(x) = \text{odds}_{\text{old}}(x) \cdot \frac{\pi_{\text{new}} / (1 - \pi_{\text{new}})}{\pi_{\text{old}} / (1 - \pi_{\text{old}})}$$

A shift in log-odds by a constant: 17.3's undersampling correction is the same formula. If you don't know the new prior, it can be estimated from the model's
predictions on the new data with an EM procedure (Saerens et al., 2002). If $p(x \mid y)$ changed too, no formula saves you; you need new labels (51.1).

## Things that will bite you

- **Using 0.5** because it's the default.
- **Treating scores as probabilities** without a reliability diagram.
- **Calibrating on the training set**, or choosing the threshold on the test set.
- **Class weights or resampling** during training, then serving the raw probabilities.
- **A threshold that silently changes meaning** when the model is retrained and its score distribution moves. Calibrate every version, or pin the threshold to a
  precision or volume target instead of a raw score.
- **Deploying into a population with a different base rate** without adjusting.

## Exercises

1. A missed defect costs 500 in warranty claims; an unnecessary inspection costs 20. What's the optimal threshold on a calibrated defect probability?
2. Explain why a random forest's probabilities are rarely below 0.05 or above 0.95 even for easy cases, and what recalibration does about it.
3. You trained a churn model on balanced data (50% churners, obtained by undersampling). In reality 8% churn. The model outputs 0.6 for a customer. What's the corrected
   probability?
4. Why doesn't temperature scaling change a classifier's accuracy? What does it change?
5. A colleague picks the threshold that maximizes F1 on the test set and reports the resulting F1. What's wrong, and how should it be done?

1. $t^* = 20 / (20 + 500) \approx 0.038$. Inspect anything with more than a 3.8% defect probability.
