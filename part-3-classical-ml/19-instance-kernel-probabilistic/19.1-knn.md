# 19.1 k-Nearest neighbors and distances

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 02.1 (norms, distances), 07.1 (KD-trees), 16.2, 16.3 (curse of dimensionality), 18.1. Lab: [`lab_19_1_knn.py`](labs/lab_19_1_knn.py).*

k-nearest neighbors is the simplest learning algorithm that actually works: to predict for a new point, find the $k$ training points closest to
it and let them vote (classification) or average (regression). There's no training step. The model *is* the data. It sounds too naive to take
seriously, and for tabular prediction it's usually beaten by the models in the rest of this part. But the idea underneath, "similar inputs have
similar outputs, and similarity is a distance", is everywhere in modern ML: every semantic search engine, every recommendation carousel, every RAG
system (Module 41) is a nearest-neighbor lookup over learned embeddings. So learn k-NN properly, because you'll be running it at scale long after you've
stopped using it as a classifier.

## The algorithm

Given a query $x$, a distance $d$, and training set $\{(x_i, y_i)\}$:

1. Compute $d(x, x_i)$ for all $i$ (or use an index to avoid that, below).
2. Take the $k$ nearest: $N_k(x)$.
3. Classification: majority vote, $\hat y = \arg\max_c \sum_{i \in N_k(x)} [y_i = c]$. The class fractions are crude probability estimates (multiples of $1/k$).
   Regression: $\hat y = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(x)} y_i$.
4. Optionally **weight** by inverse distance, so closer neighbors count more (`weights="distance"`).

**The bias-variance knob is $k$** (16.2): $k = 1$ memorizes the training set (zero training error, high variance, jagged boundaries); large $k$ smooths toward the
global majority (high bias). Choose it by cross-validation (18.1), usually on a log-ish grid, and prefer odd $k$ for binary problems to avoid ties.

A piece of theory worth knowing: as $n \to \infty$, the error of 1-NN is at most **twice the Bayes error** (the best possible error for the problem; Cover and Hart,
1967), and $k$-NN is **consistent** (converges to the Bayes error) if $k \to \infty$ and $k/n \to 0$. A nonparametric method with no assumptions about the shape of the
boundary, that provably gets there with enough data. The catch is in "enough data", and it's the next section.

## Distances are the model

k-NN makes exactly one modeling decision: what "close" means. Get that wrong and nothing else matters.

**Scale your features.** Euclidean distance sums squared differences across features, so a feature measured in thousands (income) completely dominates one
measured in units (number of children). Standardize, or scale to a range, and do it inside the CV pipeline (18.1). Then ask whether equal weight per feature is
right; often it isn't, and weighting features (or learning a metric) helps.

Common distances (02.1):

| Distance | Formula | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Euclidean ($L_2$) | $\sqrt{\sum_j (a_j - b_j)^2}$ | continuous, scaled features; the default |
| Manhattan ($L_1$) | $\sum_j \lvert a_j - b_j\rvert$ | more robust to single large differences; grid-like spaces |
| Minkowski ($L_p$) | $(\sum_j \lvert a_j - b_j\rvert^p)^{1/p}$ | the family containing both |
| Cosine | $1 - \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert b\rVert}$ | direction matters, magnitude doesn't: text vectors, embeddings |
| Mahalanobis | $\sqrt{(a - b)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(a - b)}$ | correlated features; accounts for their covariance |
| Hamming / Jaccard | fraction of differing positions / $1 - \frac{\lvert A \cap B\rvert}{\lvert A \cup B\rvert}$ | binary vectors, sets (tags, purchased items) |
| Gower | per-feature distances, averaged | mixed numeric and categorical data |

For normalized vectors, cosine distance and squared Euclidean distance are equivalent ($\|a - b\|^2 = 2 - 2\cos(a, b)$), which is why vector databases normalize
embeddings and use whichever is faster (41.1).

The modern answer to "which distance?" is often "learn one": train an embedding (a neural network, 27.3, 41.1) such that Euclidean or cosine distance in the
embedding space means what you want. That's metric learning, and it's what turns k-NN from a toy into the retrieval backbone of LLM applications.

## Why it fails in high dimensions

16.3 showed the geometry: in high dimensions, the neighborhood that holds a fixed fraction of the data spans most of the range of every feature, and the distance
to the nearest point approaches the distance to the farthest. When "nearest" and "farthest" are nearly the same distance away, "nearest" stops meaning "similar".

Two practical consequences:

- **Irrelevant features destroy k-NN.** Each one adds noise to every distance. With 5 informative features and 50 random ones, the informative signal is drowned. Trees
  and linear models can ignore a feature; k-NN can't, because every feature enters every distance. The lab adds noise dimensions one batch at a time and watches
  accuracy fall. Feature selection or a learned embedding is the fix.
- **The data you have lies on a lower-dimensional manifold** (16.3's manifold hypothesis), which is why k-NN works at all on images or text embeddings with hundreds of
  dimensions: the *intrinsic* dimension is much lower than the ambient one.

(A related oddity worth recognizing: **hubness**. In high dimensions, a few points become the nearest neighbor of a disproportionate number of queries. If your
recommendation system keeps suggesting the same five items to everyone, check this before blaming the users.)

## Making it fast

Brute force costs $O(nd)$ per query: fine for thousands of points, painful for millions, impossible for billions at interactive latency.

- **Vectorize** the brute force: $\|a - b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 - 2a^\top b + \|b\|^2$ turns all pairwise distances into one matrix multiplication (01.3, 07.3). This is what
  scikit-learn's brute-force path and GPU libraries do, and it's surprisingly competitive up to a few hundred thousand points.
- **Space-partitioning trees** (07.1): KD-trees split on one coordinate at a time, ball trees on hyperspheres. Exact, and dramatically faster in low dimensions (say,
  under 20). In high dimensions, they degrade to brute force (the curse again: the query ball intersects almost every cell).
- **Approximate nearest neighbors (ANN)**: give up exactness for speed. Graph-based (HNSW), clustering-based (IVF: search only the few clusters nearest the query),
  quantization (PQ: compress vectors to bytes) and hashing (LSH). Measured by **recall@k** (fraction of the true neighbors found) against queries per second. The
  lab builds a tiny IVF index from k-means (21.1) and shows the trade-off; 41.2 goes deep into the production versions (FAISS, HNSW in vector databases).

## Where k-NN earns its keep

- **Retrieval** over embeddings: semantic search, RAG, deduplication, "similar items". Here it's not a classifier; it's the product.
- **Baselines**: k-NN on good features is a strong, assumption-free baseline, and it tells you whether your features carry signal locally.
- **Anomaly detection** (23.3): the distance to the $k$-th nearest neighbor is a simple, effective outlier score.
- **Imputation**: fill missing values from similar rows (`KNNImputer`), with the leakage rules of 18.1.
- **Small data with good distances**: few examples, meaningful metric, boundary too irregular for a linear model.

What it's bad at: many irrelevant features, mixed and messy tabular data, huge training sets needing fast predictions without an ANN index, calibrated
probabilities, and interpretability beyond "here are the similar cases" (which, to be fair, is sometimes exactly the explanation a domain expert wants).

> **Scar tissue.** A "similar listings" feature I built for a friend's second-hand marketplace recommended the same handful of items to
> almost everyone. Price was one of the features, in pesos, next to features like condition from 1 to 5. Euclidean distance was, in
> practice, a distance on price alone, and a used motherboard's nearest neighbor was whatever else cost about the same, including a
> bicycle. Standardizing the features took one line. Distances have units whether or not you think about them.

## Things that will bite you

- **Unscaled features.** The single most common k-NN bug.
- **Irrelevant features** diluting every distance.
- **Scaling fitted on all the data** before cross-validation.
- **Using k-NN probabilities** as calibrated probabilities (they're multiples of $1/k$).
- **Duplicate rows across train and test**: 1-NN will find them and look brilliant (18.4).
- **Trusting a KD-tree** to be fast in 300 dimensions.

## Exercises

2. What's the leave-one-out error of 1-NN on the training set if you *forget* to leave the point out?
3. You have features `age` (years), `income` (dollars) and `num_children`. Without scaling, which feature determines the neighbors? What changes after standardization,
   and why might you still not want equal weights?
4. For unit-norm vectors, show that ranking by cosine similarity and ranking by Euclidean distance give the same neighbors.
5. Why do KD-trees lose their advantage in high dimensions?

1. Expand $(a - b)^\top(a - b)$. For query matrix $Q$ and training matrix $X$: $D^2 = \text{rownorms}(Q)^2\,\mathbf{1}^\top - 2QX^\top + \mathbf{1}\,\text{rownorms}(X)^2{}^\top$, where the middle term is one
   matrix product and the others are broadcasts. Catastrophic cancellation (07.2): for nearby points with large norms, the difference of large numbers can come out
   slightly negative. Clip at 0 before taking square roots, and center the data if the norms are large relative to the distances.

2. Zero: every point is its own nearest neighbor at distance 0. That's why resubstitution error is meaningless for 1-NN, and why duplicates across splits are so
   damaging.

3. Income, completely: differences of thousands of dollars swamp differences of a few years or children. After standardization, each contributes comparably, one
   standard deviation each. But equal weight is itself an assumption: maybe one child matters more for your target than one standard deviation of income. Weight
   features by domain knowledge, feature selection, or a learned metric.

4. With $\|a\| = \|b\| = 1$: $\|a - b\|^2 = 2 - 2\,a^\top b = 2 - 2\cos(a, b)$. It's a decreasing function of cosine similarity, so the ordering is identical.

5. A KD-tree prunes a cell when the query's current $k$-th-neighbor ball doesn't intersect it. In high dimensions, distances concentrate: the ball's radius is nearly
   as large as the distance to most cells, so it intersects almost all of them, nothing gets pruned, and you pay tree overhead on top of a brute-force scan.
