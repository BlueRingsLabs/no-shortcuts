# 19.3 Support vector machines and kernels

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 02.1 (inner products), 03.2 (constrained optimization, Lagrange multipliers), 03.3 (convexity), 17.2, 17.3. Lab: [`lab_19_3_svm_kernels.py`](labs/lab_19_3_svm_kernels.py).*

For about fifteen years, from the late 1990s until deep learning took over around 2012, support vector machines were *the* serious classifier. If
you read an ML paper from 2005, the baseline to beat was an SVM with an RBF kernel, and it was hard to beat. They've since been pushed out of most
large-scale work, but the ideas they brought are still everywhere: margins, the hinge loss, convex duality, and above all **kernels**, the trick of
working in an enormous (even infinite) feature space without ever computing a single feature. You'll see kernels again in Gaussian processes (23.4),
and the attention mechanism in transformers can be read as a learned kernel smoother (29.1). This lesson derives the SVM properly, because the
derivation is where the insight is.

## Maximum margin

Take linearly separable data with labels $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$. Infinitely many hyperplanes $w^\top x + b = 0$ separate it. Which one? The SVM answer: the one farthest
from the nearest points of either class, the **maximum-margin** hyperplane. It's the most robust to small perturbations of the data, and it has good
generalization guarantees (the margin, not the dimension, controls the capacity; 16.3).

The distance from a point to the hyperplane is $|w^\top x + b| / \|w\|$. Rescale $(w, b)$ so that the closest points satisfy $y_i(w^\top x_i + b) = 1$; then the margin (the gap
between the two classes) is $2/\|w\|$. Maximizing it is minimizing $\|w\|$:

$$\min_{w, b}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i(w^\top x_i + b) \ge 1 \ \ \forall i$$

A convex quadratic program: one global optimum, found reliably.

## Soft margin and the hinge loss

Real data isn't separable, and even when it is, one mislabeled point shouldn't dictate the boundary. Allow violations with slack variables $\xi_i \ge 0$:

$$\min_{w, b, \xi}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i \quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i(w^\top x_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0$$

At the optimum $\xi_i = \max(0, 1 - y_i f(x_i))$, so this is exactly

$$\min_{w, b}\ \sum_i \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(w^\top x_i + b)\big) + \frac{1}{2C}\|w\|^2$$

**hinge loss plus L2 regularization**. Seen this way, the SVM is a linear model like logistic regression (17.3), with a different loss:

- **Logistic loss** $\log(1 + e^{-yf})$: always positive, every point contributes, gives probabilities.
- **Hinge loss** $\max(0, 1 - yf)$: exactly zero for points beyond the margin on the right side. Those points don't affect the solution at all. The solution depends only
  on the points on or inside the margin: the **support vectors**.

$C$ is the inverse regularization strength (like scikit-learn's `C` for logistic regression): large $C$ = few violations tolerated, narrow margin, high variance;
small $C$ = wide margin, more violations, high bias.

## The dual

Form the Lagrangian of the soft-margin problem, minimize over $w, b, \xi$ (03.2), and you get the **dual problem**:

$$\max_\alpha\ \sum_i \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j\, x_i^\top x_j \quad\text{s.t.}\quad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C,\ \ \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$$

with $w = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i$. The complementary slackness conditions (KKT) tell you who matters:

- $\alpha_i = 0$: the point is outside the margin, correctly classified. Irrelevant.
- $0 < \alpha_i < C$: exactly on the margin. A support vector. $b$ is computed from these.
- $\alpha_i = C$: inside the margin or misclassified. Also a support vector.

The prediction is $f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, x_i^\top x + b$, a weighted vote of the support vectors. The lab solves this dual with a general-purpose optimizer on a small
problem and recovers exactly scikit-learn's solution.

Now look at the dual again. **The data appears only through inner products $x_i^\top x_j$.** That's the door the kernel trick walks through.

## The kernel trick

To make a linear model nonlinear, map the inputs through features $\phi(x)$ and work linearly in feature space (17.4's first way out). If $\phi$ has $D$ dimensions, each inner
product costs $O(D)$, and $D$ explodes: all monomials of degree up to 3 in 100 variables is about 177,000 features.

But the dual only needs $\phi(x_i)^\top\phi(x_j)$. For many feature maps, that inner product has a closed form that's cheap to compute in the original space. That function is a
**kernel**, $k(x, z) = \phi(x)^\top\phi(z)$:

- **Polynomial**: $k(x, z) = (x^\top z + c)^p$. Implicitly, all monomials up to degree $p$, with specific weights. Cost $O(d)$ instead of $O(d^p)$.
- **RBF (Gaussian)**: $k(x, z) = \exp(-\gamma\|x - z\|^2)$. Its feature space is **infinite-dimensional** (expand the exponential as a power series), so you couldn't
  compute $\phi$ even if you wanted to. It's also a similarity: 1 for identical points, decaying to 0 with distance, at a rate set by $\gamma$.
- **Others**: string kernels for sequences, graph kernels, kernels on sets and distributions. Anything that's a valid similarity can be used.

A function is a valid kernel (it corresponds to *some* feature space) iff every Gram matrix $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$ it produces is symmetric positive semidefinite
(Mercer's condition). Sums, products and positive scalings of kernels are kernels, so you can build them from parts.

Replace $x_i^\top x_j$ with $k(x_i, x_j)$ everywhere in the dual, and the prediction becomes

$$f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, k(x_i, x) + b$$

That's the self-check question answered: **what the kernel trick buys you computationally is the ability to fit a linear model in a huge or infinite feature space at the
cost of evaluating $k$ on pairs of original inputs**, $O(d)$ each, never constructing the features. The price moves elsewhere: you need the $n \times n$ Gram matrix, so training costs
between $O(n^2)$ and $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory, and prediction costs $O(n_{\text{SV}}\cdot d)$ per point. Kernel methods are cheap in $D$ and expensive in $n$. Linear models on explicit
features are the opposite. That trade decides which to use.

(The **representer theorem** generalizes the form of $f$: for any loss plus an L2 penalty in the kernel's feature space, the optimum is a combination $\sum_i \beta_i k(x_i, \cdot)$ of kernels
centered on the training points. Kernel ridge regression and Gaussian processes (23.4) are the same idea with squared loss.)

## Tuning an RBF SVM

Two knobs, both on log scales, both critical, and they interact:

- **$\gamma$**: the kernel width. Large $\gamma$ means each support vector influences only a tiny neighborhood: wiggly boundaries, eventually memorizing the training set (like 1-NN, 19.1).
  Small $\gamma$ means very smooth, nearly linear boundaries. scikit-learn's default `gamma="scale"` uses $1/(d \cdot \operatorname{Var}(X))$, a reasonable center for a search.
- **$C$**: as above, the penalty on margin violations.

Search a grid like $C \in \{10^{-2}, \dots, 10^3\}$, $\gamma \in \{10^{-4}, \dots, 10^1\}$ with cross-validation (18.1, and nested CV if you report the score). The good region is usually a
diagonal band: larger $C$ pairs with smaller $\gamma$. **Standardize features first**: the RBF kernel uses Euclidean distance, with all of 19.1's scaling problems.

## Scaling up, and where SVMs stand

Kernel SVMs are practical up to roughly $10^4$ to $10^5$ examples. Beyond that:

- **Linear SVM** (`LinearSVC`, liblinear, or SGD with hinge loss): scales to millions of rows and features, and on high-dimensional sparse data (text) it's as good as the
  kernel version. A classic strong text classifier.
- **Kernel approximations**: build an explicit, finite feature map whose inner products approximate the kernel, then train a linear model on it. **Random Fourier features**
  (Rahimi and Recht, 2007) approximate the RBF kernel with $\cos(\omega^\top x + b)$ for random $\omega$; **Nyström** uses a subset of training points. Linear cost in $n$, with a
  tunable accuracy. The lab shows random features matching an RBF SVM.

Today, for tabular data gradient boosting (20.3, 20.4) usually wins; for images, text and audio, deep networks win (Part IV). SVMs remain good on small to medium
datasets with a meaningful kernel (bioinformatics with sequence kernels, some scientific problems), as a strong baseline, and in variants: **SVR** (regression with an
$\epsilon$-insensitive loss) and the **one-class SVM** for novelty detection (23.3). The margin and kernel ideas outlived the algorithm.

## Things that will bite you

- **Unscaled features** with an RBF kernel.
- **Tuning $C$ and $\gamma$ separately**, or on a linear grid.
- **Kernel SVMs on 500,000 rows**: the Gram matrix alone won't fit. Use a linear SVM or a kernel approximation.
- **Treating `decision_function` outputs as probabilities.** They're margins. Calibrate (18.3).
- **Class imbalance**: the hinge loss treats every violation equally. Use `class_weight` or adjust the threshold on the margin.

2. For fixed $(w, b)$, each $\xi_i$ appears only in $C\xi_i$ (minimized by making it as small as possible) and in the constraints $\xi_i \ge 1 - y_i f(x_i)$ and $\xi_i \ge 0$. So $\xi_i = \max(0, 1 - y_i f(x_i))$.
   Substitute and divide the objective by $C$.

3. $L = \frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 + C\sum\xi_i - \sum\alpha_i[y_i(w^\top x_i + b) - 1 + \xi_i] - \sum\mu_i\xi_i$ with $\alpha, \mu \ge 0$. $\partial L/\partial w = 0 \Rightarrow w = \sum\alpha_i y_i x_i$. $\partial L/\partial b = 0 \Rightarrow \sum\alpha_i y_i = 0$.
   $\partial L/\partial\xi_i = 0 \Rightarrow C - \alpha_i - \mu_i = 0$, and $\mu_i \ge 0$ gives $\alpha_i \le C$. Substituting back eliminates $w, b, \xi$ and leaves the dual objective.

4. $(x^\top z + 1)^2 = (x_1z_1 + x_2z_2 + 1)^2$. Expanding: $\phi(x) = (x_1^2,\ x_2^2,\ \sqrt{2}x_1x_2,\ \sqrt{2}x_1,\ \sqrt{2}x_2,\ 1)$. Six features for $d = 2$, and $\binom{d+2}{2}$ in general, while the kernel costs one
   inner product.

5. Each training point becomes its own tiny bump; with large $C$ every point is fit: 100% training accuracy, like 1-NN. On new data, points far from all training points get
   $f(x) \approx b$, and the boundary is a patchwork: severe overfitting, poor generalization.
