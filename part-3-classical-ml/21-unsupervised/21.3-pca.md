# 21.3 PCA and linear dimensionality reduction

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 02.4 (eigendecomposition, SVD), 03.2 (Lagrange multipliers), 04.1 (covariance), 17.2 (ridge). Lab: [`lab_21_3_pca.py`](labs/lab_21_3_pca.py).*

You met PCA in 02.4 as "the SVD of the centered data matrix", and you already know how to compute it stably. This lesson is about everything around
that computation: *why* the principal components are the right directions (two derivations that land on the same answer), how many to keep, what
scaling does to them, when PCA as a preprocessing step quietly throws away exactly the signal you needed, and the other linear methods you'll
reach for when PCA's assumptions don't fit.

PCA is one of the most used and most misused tools in data science. Used well, it compresses, denoises, decorrelates and visualizes. Used on autopilot,
it's a step in the pipeline that nobody can justify and that makes the model worse.

## Two derivations, one answer

Center the data ($X$ is $n \times d$, columns with zero mean), with covariance $S = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X$.

**Maximum variance.** Find the unit direction $w$ along which the projected data $Xw$ has the largest variance: maximize $w^\top Sw$ subject to $\|w\| = 1$. With a Lagrange
multiplier (03.2): $Sw = \lambda w$. So $w$ is an eigenvector of $S$, and the variance it captures is $w^\top Sw = \lambda$: take the eigenvector with the **largest eigenvalue**. The second component
maximizes variance subject to being orthogonal to the first, and is the second eigenvector, and so on. (The quantity $w^\top Sw / w^\top w$ is the Rayleigh quotient; its maximum is the top eigenvalue.)

**Minimum reconstruction error.** Find the $k$-dimensional subspace such that projecting the data onto it loses the least, in squared error: minimize $\sum_i\|x_i - WW^\top x_i\|^2$
over $d \times k$ matrices $W$ with orthonormal columns. The answer is the span of the top $k$ eigenvectors, and the minimum error is $n$ times the **sum of the discarded eigenvalues**.
(This is the Eckart-Young theorem from 02.4, seen from the data's side.)

Both views agree because the total variance is fixed ($\text{tr}(S) = \sum_j \lambda_j$): whatever variance the subspace captures, the rest is exactly the reconstruction error. Keeping the
most variance *is* losing the least.

Vocabulary: the eigenvectors are the **principal axes** (or **loadings**, when scaled by $\sqrt{\lambda}$); the projections $XW$ are the **scores** or **components**; $\lambda_j / \sum\lambda$ is the
**explained variance ratio**.

## How many components?

- **Cumulative explained variance**: keep enough to reach 90%, 95%, 99%. Simple and arbitrary.
- **The scree plot**: eigenvalues in decreasing order; look for the elbow where they flatten into noise. Same caveat as k-means' elbow (21.1).
- **Downstream performance**: treat $k$ as a hyperparameter and cross-validate the whole pipeline (18.1). The only criterion that answers "does this help my model?".
- **Noise-aware rules**: for data that's signal plus isotropic noise, eigenvalues of pure noise follow a known distribution (Marchenko-Pastur); components above its upper edge are signal. Useful in
  genomics and finance, where $d$ is comparable to $n$ and random data produces impressive-looking eigenvalues.

## Scaling decides everything

PCA maximizes variance, and variance depends on units. Measure one feature in grams and another in kilograms and the grams feature "explains" a million times more variance. Two options:

- **Covariance PCA** (center only): when all features share units and their variances are meaningful (pixel intensities, the same sensor type, returns of similar assets).
- **Correlation PCA** (center and standardize): when features have different units, which is most tabular data.

The lab shows the first component of unscaled data being just the feature with the biggest numbers. Fit the scaler and the PCA inside the pipeline, on training folds only (18.1).

## PCA as preprocessing: when it helps and when it hurts

PCA before a supervised model is common: fewer features, decorrelated inputs, less noise. It helps when the predictive signal lives in the high-variance directions: images, where
fine pixel noise is low-variance; many correlated measurements of a few underlying factors.

It hurts when it doesn't. **PCA never looks at the target.** A direction can have tiny variance and be the only one that predicts $y$: a small, precise difference between two
nearly identical measurements, say. PCA ranks that direction last and **principal component regression** (PCR, regression on the top $k$ scores) throws it away. The lab builds exactly this
case: PCR fails, while ridge regression (17.2), which also shrinks low-variance directions but only softly, and in proportion to what the data supports, keeps it. Ridge is the soft,
target-aware cousin of PCR (look at 17.2's shrinkage factors: PCR uses 1 or 0 where ridge uses $\sigma_j^2/(\sigma_j^2 + \lambda)$). **Partial least squares** (PLS) picks directions with high *covariance with
the target*, a supervised alternative. Better than PCR, not a cure: covariance is variance times association, so a tiny-variance predictive direction still ranks low, and the lab's PLS
needs four components to find what ridge finds immediately. And for tree ensembles, PCA mostly destroys the axis-aligned structure trees like; it rarely helps them.

**Whitening**: scale each component to unit variance ($XW\Lambda^{-1/2}$). The result has identity covariance. Sometimes useful before methods that assume it (ICA, some clustering), and it
amplifies the noise directions, so combine with truncation.

## Beyond plain PCA

- **Probabilistic PCA** (Tipping and Bishop, 1999): PCA as a latent-variable model, $x = Wz + \mu + \epsilon$ with Gaussian $z$ and isotropic noise, fitted by maximum likelihood (or EM, 21.2). Gives a
  likelihood (for model comparison and anomaly scores) and handles missing values. **Factor analysis** is the same with per-feature noise variances.
- **Incremental PCA** for data that doesn't fit in memory; **randomized PCA** (02.4) for large, low-rank data; **truncated SVD** for sparse matrices (without centering, which would make them
  dense), known in text analysis as latent semantic analysis.
- **Kernel PCA**: PCA in a kernel's feature space (19.3), for nonlinear structure. The lab untangles concentric circles with it.
- **ICA** (independent component analysis): finds directions that are statistically *independent*, not just uncorrelated, by maximizing non-Gaussianity. The classic use is separating mixed signals
  (the "cocktail party problem").
- **NMF** (non-negative matrix factorization): for non-negative data (counts, spectra, images), finds additive parts, which are often more interpretable than PCA's signed loadings. Topics in text,
  components in spectra.
- **Random projections**: multiply by a random Gaussian matrix to $k = O(\log n / \varepsilon^2)$ dimensions and all pairwise distances are preserved within a factor $1 \pm \varepsilon$ (the
  Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma). No fitting at all. Surprisingly useful as a fast first step, and the lab checks the distortion.
- **Autoencoders** (31.1): nonlinear PCA with neural networks. A linear autoencoder with squared loss learns exactly the PCA subspace.

## Things that will bite you

- **Unscaled features** with different units.
- **Fitting PCA on all the data** before cross-validation.
- **PCR throwing away** a low-variance predictive direction.
- **Interpreting loadings** as if components were real-world factors. They're directions of variance, orthogonal by construction; nature isn't obliged to be orthogonal.
- **Sign flips** between runs or library versions (02.4).
- **Centering a sparse matrix** and running out of memory. Use `TruncatedSVD`.
- **PCA in front of a tree ensemble** by habit.

## Exercises

1. Derive the first principal component by maximizing $w^\top Sw$ subject to $\|w\| = 1$.
2. Show that the reconstruction error of projecting onto the top $k$ principal components is $n\sum_{j > k}\lambda_j$.
3. Explain the relationship between the PCA of $X$ and its SVD $X = U\Sigma V^\top$. What are the eigenvalues of $S$ in terms of $\Sigma$? What are the scores?
4. Construct a two-feature dataset where the first principal component is useless for predicting $y$ and the second is perfect.
5. Why does a linear autoencoder trained with squared error recover the PCA subspace but not necessarily the individual principal axes?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\mathcal{L} = w^\top Sw - \lambda(w^\top w - 1)$; $\nabla_w = 2Sw - 2\lambda w = 0 \Rightarrow Sw = \lambda w$. The objective at an eigenvector is $w^\top Sw = \lambda$, so the maximum is the largest eigenvalue's eigenvector.

2. With orthonormal eigenvectors $v_j$, each centered $x_i = \sum_j (v_j^\top x_i)v_j$, and projection keeps the first $k$ terms, so the error is $\sum_i\sum_{j>k}(v_j^\top x_i)^2 = \sum_{j>k} v_j^\top\big(\sum_i x_ix_i^\top\big)v_j = \sum_{j>k} v_j^\top(nS)v_j = n\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j$.

3. $S = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X = \frac{1}{n}V\Sigma^2V^\top$, so the principal axes are the columns of $V$ and $\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/n$. The scores $XV = U\Sigma$: the left singular vectors scaled by the singular values.

4. $x_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 100)$ (big variance, unrelated to $y$), $x_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.01)$, $y = x_2$. The first component is $x_1$'s axis and carries no information about $y$; the second is $x_2$ and predicts it perfectly.
   PCR with $k = 1$ gets $R^2 = 0$.

5. The squared reconstruction error depends only on the subspace spanned by the encoder/decoder weights: any invertible transformation of the code, undone by the decoder, gives the same reconstructions.
   So the optimum is any basis of the top-$k$ principal subspace; nothing forces it to be the orthogonal, variance-ordered one.
