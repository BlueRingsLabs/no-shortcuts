# 21.4 Manifold learning: t-SNE and UMAP

*About 6 hours. Prerequisites: 21.3, 06.1 (KL divergence, entropy), 19.1 (nearest neighbors), 16.3 (the manifold hypothesis). Lab: [`lab_21_4_manifold.py`](labs/lab_21_4_manifold.py).*

At some point in every project someone makes a scatter plot of embeddings, colored by label, with beautiful separated islands, and puts it in a slide
titled "the model has learned meaningful structure". Sometimes it has. The plot doesn't prove it. t-SNE and UMAP are extraordinary visualization tools,
and they're also the easiest way I know to generate convincing pictures of things that aren't there.

This lesson explains how they work, which is the only defense: once you know that t-SNE sets a separate distance scale for every point, you stop
reading anything into cluster sizes. Once you know what perplexity does, you stop trusting a single plot.

## Why linear projections aren't enough

PCA (21.3) finds the flat subspace with the most variance. Real data often lies near a **curved** low-dimensional manifold (16.3): images of a rotating object,
a swiss roll. Projecting a swiss roll onto its top two principal components squashes the layers on top of each other; points far apart *along* the roll land next to each other.
Manifold learning tries to preserve the structure along the manifold instead: which points are neighbors of which.

The classical methods, briefly: **MDS** preserves pairwise distances (as well as possible in 2D); **Isomap** replaces Euclidean distances by geodesic ones (shortest paths through a
nearest-neighbor graph) and then runs MDS, which unrolls a swiss roll beautifully and breaks when the graph has shortcuts; **LLE** reconstructs each point from its neighbors and preserves
those local weights. All are worth knowing by name; for visualization in practice, t-SNE and UMAP have taken over.

## t-SNE

**In high dimension**, turn distances into neighbor probabilities. For point $i$, put a Gaussian on it and define

$$p_{j \mid i} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \neq i}\exp(-\|x_i - x_k\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}$$

The detail that matters: **each point gets its own $\sigma_i$**, chosen so the distribution has a fixed **perplexity** $2^{H(p_{\cdot \mid i})}$, roughly the effective number of neighbors (06.1's entropy).
Points in dense regions get a small $\sigma_i$; points in sparse regions get a large one. Symmetrize: $p_{ij} = (p_{j \mid i} + p_{i \mid j}) / 2n$.

**In 2D**, define similarities with a **Student-t** kernel (one degree of freedom):

$$q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l}(1 + \|y_k - y_l\|^2)^{-1}}$$

and move the 2D points $y_i$ by gradient descent to minimize $\text{KL}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_{ij} p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}$.

Why the heavy-tailed Student-t? The **crowding problem**: a 10-dimensional neighborhood has room for far more equidistant neighbors than a 2D disc, so squeezing it into 2D forces moderately
distant points too close. The heavy tail lets moderately distant points sit much farther away in 2D at little cost, which opens up gaps between groups. That's where t-SNE's crisp
islands come from. The KL divergence is asymmetric in a way that matters too: putting true neighbors far apart ($p$ large, $q$ small) is expensive; putting non-neighbors close ($p$ small,
$q$ large) is cheap. **t-SNE preserves local neighborhoods and cares very little about anything else.**

Practicalities: **early exaggeration** (multiply $P$ by ~12 for the first iterations so clusters form before settling), Barnes-Hut or FFT approximations for $O(n\log n)$, and **PCA
initialization** instead of random (much more stable global layout; the default in recent scikit-learn).

### What a t-SNE plot does and doesn't tell you

The self-check question, answered from the mechanics:

- **Cluster sizes mean almost nothing.** Every point's $\sigma_i$ is tuned to see the same effective number of neighbors, so a tight cluster and a diffuse one are both normalized to "about
  `perplexity` neighbors each" and come out looking similar in size. The lab builds two clusters whose spreads differ by a factor of 10 and gets similar-sized blobs.
- **Distances between clusters mean little.** Beyond the neighborhood scale, the objective barely constrains positions (the cheap direction of the KL), and the heavy tail stretches gaps. Two
  clusters next to each other on the plot need not be close in the data; the lab shows the ordering of inter-cluster distances getting scrambled.
- **Perplexity changes the picture**, sometimes completely. Low perplexity shatters data into small clumps, and **pure noise isn't safe at any setting**: the lab runs t-SNE on a structureless
  Gaussian blob and a clustering algorithm finds several "clusters" in the plot, at both a low and the default perplexity, where the same algorithm finds none in the original data. Always
  look at several perplexities (5, 30, 100) and several seeds.
- **What it does preserve**: who is near whom, locally. If points of a class are consistently near each other across settings, that's real information about local neighborhoods.

## UMAP

UMAP (McInnes, Healy and Melville, 2018) builds a weighted $k$-nearest-neighbor graph in high dimension (with per-point distance scaling, like t-SNE's $\sigma_i$), builds a similar graph in low
dimension with a smooth kernel, and minimizes a **cross-entropy** between the two graphs' edge weights. The cross-entropy includes a term that pushes non-neighbors apart, which is part of why
UMAP is often said to preserve more **global structure** than t-SNE. That claim is weaker than its reputation: much of the difference comes from initialization (UMAP uses a spectral embedding
by default; t-SNE with PCA init closes most of the gap, per Kobak and Linderman, 2021).

Practical differences: UMAP is **faster** on large data, can **embed new points** into an existing map (`transform`), works for more than 2 output dimensions (sometimes used as a preprocessing step
for clustering), and has two main knobs: `n_neighbors` (local vs global emphasis, like perplexity) and `min_dist` (how tightly points pack). The same warnings about sizes, distances and
parameters apply.

## Using them responsibly

- **Explore, don't prove.** A t-SNE plot is a hypothesis generator. Evidence comes from something quantitative: a classifier's accuracy, a clustering's stability (21.1), a statistical test.
- **Several settings and seeds**, always. Report the ones you didn't like too.
- **Measure what you claim.** "Neighborhoods are preserved" can be quantified: the fraction of each point's $k$ nearest neighbors that remain neighbors in 2D, or **trustworthiness**. The lab computes both.
- **Don't cluster on t-SNE coordinates** and report the clusters as findings: you'd be clustering the algorithm's artifacts (sizes and gaps it invented). Cluster in the original or a PCA-reduced space;
  use the plot to look at the result.
- **Color by everything**: labels, but also batch, date, data source, sequence length. The most common "discovery" in an embedding plot is a data-collection artifact (18.4).
- **Reduce with PCA first** (to ~50 dimensions) for speed and denoising; it's the standard pipeline.

## Things that will bite you

- **Reading cluster sizes or inter-cluster distances** off a t-SNE plot.
- **One perplexity, one seed**, one conclusion.
- **Clusters in noise**, at any perplexity.
- **Clustering in the embedding** and treating the result as structure.
- **Believing UMAP's global layout** more than it deserves.
- **Fitting t-SNE on train and test together** and then evaluating anything on it (t-SNE has no `transform`; there's no honest out-of-sample map).

2. Why does the Student-t kernel in 2D help with the crowding problem?
3. Using the asymmetry of $\text{KL}(P\,\|\,Q)$, explain why t-SNE preserves local structure but not global structure.
4. A colleague shows a t-SNE plot where fraud cases form a small, tight island far from everything else and concludes fraud is "clearly separable". What would you check?
5. Why can't you use a t-SNE embedding fitted on your training data to embed new production data, and what would you use instead?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The perplexity is $2^{H}$ of each point's neighbor distribution: the effective number of neighbors it "sees". $\sigma_i$ is found by binary search so every point hits the target perplexity. In dense
   regions, a small $\sigma_i$ already covers enough neighbors; in sparse regions it must be large. The effect is to normalize local density, which is exactly why cluster sizes stop meaning anything.

2. In 2D there isn't room to place all of a high-dimensional point's moderately distant neighbors at their true relative distances. With a Gaussian kernel in 2D, moderately distant points would have to
   be placed too close to get small enough $q_{ij}$. The heavy-tailed Student-t assigns non-negligible similarity even at larger 2D distances, so moderately distant points can be placed far away while
   keeping $q_{ij}$ matched to $p_{ij}$. Neighbors stay close; the rest spreads out, forming gaps.

3. The cost of each pair is weighted by $p_{ij}$. When $p_{ij}$ is large (true neighbors) and $q_{ij}$ is small (placed far), the term $p_{ij}\log(p_{ij}/q_{ij})$ is large: heavily penalized. When $p_{ij}$ is tiny (far
   apart in the data), the term is tiny whatever $q_{ij}$ is: where non-neighbors land barely matters. So the optimization enforces neighborhoods and leaves global arrangement nearly free.

4. Whether the separation holds in the original space: train a classifier and measure precision/recall on held-out data (18.2). Try other perplexities and seeds. Color the plot by other variables
   (the fraud cases may come from one source, time window or merchant, an artifact, 18.4). Check the island's tightness isn't just t-SNE's density normalization. The plot alone shows only
   that fraud cases are each other's nearest neighbors, which might be true for a boring reason.

5. t-SNE optimizes coordinates for the specific points it was given; there's no function from input space to 2D, so no way to place a new point consistently (re-running changes everything). Use
   a method with a learned mapping: PCA, UMAP's `transform`, parametric t-SNE/UMAP (a neural network trained to produce the embedding), or train a small regression model from inputs to the
   2D coordinates, knowing the result is only approximate.
