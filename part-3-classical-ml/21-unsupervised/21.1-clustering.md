*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 02.1 (distances), 19.1 (scaling, the curse of dimensionality), 03.3. Lab: [`lab_21_1_clustering.py`](labs/lab_21_1_clustering.py).*

Clustering is where machine learning meets wishful thinking. You hand an algorithm a table with no labels and ask it to find the "natural groups", and
it always obliges: k-means will return exactly as many clusters as you ask for, on any data, including data with no structure at all. The algorithm
doesn't know whether the groups are real. Neither, until you check, do you.

That's not an argument against clustering. Customer segmentation, deduplication, compressing a million colors into 256, grouping log messages,
finding the modes of a sensor's behavior, building the index of a vector database (19.1's IVF): all clustering, all useful. It's an argument for
knowing what each algorithm assumes, because the answer you get is mostly a consequence of those assumptions.

## There is no single right answer

Supervised learning has a target to be right about. Clustering doesn't. "Group these points" is ill-posed until you say what makes a good group: compact
and round (k-means), connected through dense regions (DBSCAN), nested at several scales (hierarchical), generated by a probability distribution (21.2).
Different definitions give different answers on the same data, and each is correct by its own definition. Kleinberg (2002) proved that no clustering
function can satisfy three reasonable-sounding properties at once, which is a formal way of saying the same thing.

So the practical questions are: **what notion of similarity makes sense for my problem** (features, scaling, distance: 19.1 again), **what shape do I expect
the groups to have**, and **how will I know whether the result is useful** (usually: does it help a downstream decision, and is it stable?).

## k-means

Choose $k$ centers $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k$ to minimize the within-cluster sum of squares (the **inertia**):

$$J = \sum_{i=1}^n \min_j \|x_i - \mu_j\|^2$$

**Lloyd's algorithm** alternates two steps, each of which can only decrease $J$:

1. **Assign** each point to its nearest center.
2. **Update** each center to the mean of its assigned points (the mean minimizes squared distance, 18.2).

It converges (finitely many assignments, $J$ decreasing), fast, to a **local** minimum. Finding the global minimum is NP-hard, and bad local minima are common with
random initialization: two centers start in one true cluster, none in another, and Lloyd never fixes it.

**k-means++** (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007) fixes the initialization: pick the first center uniformly at random, then each next center with probability proportional to
its squared distance from the nearest center already chosen. Spread-out starts, with a proven $O(\log k)$ approximation guarantee in expectation. It's scikit-learn's default,
combined with several restarts (`n_init`), keeping the best $J$. The lab implements both and shows random initialization getting stuck.

Cost: $O(nkd)$ per iteration. **Mini-batch k-means** updates centers from small random batches and handles millions of points.

What k-means assumes, whether you like it or not:

- Clusters are **convex and roughly spherical** in the feature space you gave it (it partitions space into Voronoi cells).
- They have **similar spread** (one shared, isotropic "radius").
- **Euclidean distance** is meaningful, so the features must be scaled (19.1), and it's sensitive to outliers (squared distances again).

On two interleaved crescents, or a ring around a blob, k-means cuts straight lines through them. That's not a bug; you asked for compact groups.

### Choosing k

- The **elbow**: plot $J$ against $k$ and look for a bend. Often there's no clear bend, and people see one anyway.
- The **silhouette** score: for each point, $s = \frac{b - a}{\max(a, b)}$ where $a$ is the mean distance to its own cluster and $b$ to the nearest other cluster; average it. Between −1 and 1, higher is better.
- The **gap statistic** (Tibshirani et al., 2001): compare $J$ with its value on uniform random data with the same range. Useful precisely because it can say "no clusters here".
- The **downstream** criterion: the number of segments the marketing team can act on, the compression rate you need. Often the honest answer.

The lab runs k-means on uniform noise and gets a perfectly respectable-looking silhouette for $k = 4$. Validate against something external when you can.

## Hierarchical clustering

**Agglomerative** clustering starts with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until one remains. The merge history is a tree
(the **dendrogram**); cutting it at a height gives a flat clustering, and you can inspect every level of granularity at once.

"Closest clusters" needs a **linkage**:

- **Single**: distance between the closest pair of points. Finds elongated, irregular shapes; suffers **chaining** (a thin bridge of points merges two big clusters).
- **Complete**: farthest pair. Compact clusters; sensitive to outliers.
- **Average**: mean pairwise distance. A compromise.
- **Ward**: the merge that increases total within-cluster variance least. Like k-means, hierarchically; the usual default.

Cost is $O(n^2)$ memory for the distance matrix and $O(n^2)$ to $O(n^3)$ time, so it's for thousands of points, not millions. It works with any distance, including ones that aren't
Euclidean, which k-means can't.

## Density-based clustering: DBSCAN and HDBSCAN

**DBSCAN** (Ester et al., 1996) defines clusters as dense regions separated by sparse ones. With a radius $\varepsilon$ and a count `min_samples`: a **core point** has at least `min_samples`
neighbors within $\varepsilon$; clusters are connected sets of core points plus the **border points** within reach; everything else is **noise**. Strengths: arbitrary shapes, no $k$ to choose,
and an explicit notion of outliers. Weakness: one global $\varepsilon$, so clusters of **different densities** can't all be found (a radius that separates the dense ones merges or
dissolves the sparse ones). Choosing $\varepsilon$: plot each point's distance to its $k$-th neighbor, sorted, and look for the knee.

**HDBSCAN** (Campello et al., 2013) runs DBSCAN at all radii at once, builds the hierarchy of density-connected clusters, and extracts the most stable ones. It handles varying
densities, needs mainly `min_cluster_size`, and is in scikit-learn. It's the density-based method I'd start with today.

## Evaluating a clustering

- **Internal** metrics use only the data: silhouette, Davies-Bouldin, Calinski-Harabasz. Each rewards its own notion of a good cluster (silhouette and CH favor convex blobs), so they're
  biased toward algorithms with the same assumptions. Useful for comparing $k$ within one method, weak for comparing methods.
- **External** metrics compare with known labels, when you have some (a labeled sample, a synthetic benchmark): the **adjusted Rand index** (ARI; 0 for random, 1 for perfect) and
  **normalized mutual information** (06.2). Invariant to how the clusters are numbered.
- **Stability**: re-cluster bootstrap samples and measure agreement (ARI between runs on the shared points). Clusters that change with every resample aren't structure; they're
  noise with a name.
- **Usefulness**: do the segments behave differently on something you didn't cluster on (churn rate, response to a campaign)? That's the test that matters for a business.

> **Scar tissue.** The student union once asked me to segment the answers to their annual survey, and I delivered five clusters from
> k-means. They named them, wrote a persona for each and printed posters. A month later I realized I'd clustered unscaled features, and
> the segments were bands of one numeric answer (hours worked per week) with a bit of noise. With scaled features the structure was
> different and much weaker. By then the personas had cartoon mascots. Unsupervised results are the easiest to fall in love with, because
> nothing ever tells you they're wrong.

## Things that will bite you

- **Unscaled features.** One feature in large units decides every cluster.
- **k-means on non-convex or unequal-density data.**
- **Believing the clusters exist** because the algorithm returned them. Check against noise, check stability.
- **Naming clusters** ("the bargain hunters") and then treating the names as findings.
- **Silhouette as a universal referee** between different algorithms.
- **Single linkage** on data with a few bridging points.
- **One global $\varepsilon$** for data with regions of different density.

4. Explain why DBSCAN can't find a dense cluster and a sparse cluster with one choice of $\varepsilon$. How does HDBSCAN avoid this?
