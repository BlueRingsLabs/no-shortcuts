# 21.2 Gaussian mixtures and EM

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 21.1, 04.1 (multivariate Gaussian), 04.4 (MLE), 06.1 (KL divergence), 19.2 (generative models). Lab: [`lab_21_2_gmm_em.py`](labs/lab_21_2_gmm_em.py).*

k-means gives every point exactly one cluster and assumes every cluster is a round ball of the same size. A **Gaussian mixture model** relaxes both:
each cluster is a Gaussian with its own shape, orientation and weight, and each point gets a *probability* of belonging to each cluster. It's also
a proper generative model of the data (19.2): it assigns a density $p(x)$ to every point, which means you can use it for anomaly detection, sample
new data from it, and compare models by likelihood.

The algorithm that fits it, **expectation-maximization**, deserves as much attention as the model. EM is the general recipe for maximum likelihood
when some variables are missing or hidden, and its derivation (a lower bound on the likelihood, tightened and maximized in turn) is the same
derivation that gives you variational autoencoders in 31.1. Learn it here, where everything is small enough to check by hand.

## The model

$$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k), \qquad \pi_k \ge 0,\ \sum_k \pi_k = 1$$

Generatively: pick a component $z \sim \text{Categorical}(\pi)$, then draw $x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_z, \Sigma_z)$. The component $z$ is a **latent** (hidden) variable: we see $x$, never $z$.

Given a point, Bayes' rule gives the posterior probability of each component, the **responsibility**:

$$\gamma_{ik} = P(z_i = k \mid x_i) = \frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_j \pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}$$

That's the soft cluster assignment. A point halfway between two components gets about 0.5 for each, which is more honest than k-means' coin flip.

## Why direct maximum likelihood is awkward

The log-likelihood is $\sum_i \log\sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$: a log of a sum. No closed form for the maximizer, and the gradients couple all the components. But notice: **if we knew the
$z_i$**, the problem would be trivial. Each component's parameters would be the mean and covariance of its own points, and $\pi_k$ the fraction of points it owns. EM exploits exactly that.

## EM

Alternate two steps:

- **E-step**: with the current parameters, compute the responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$ (the expected, soft assignments).
- **M-step**: re-estimate the parameters as if the soft assignments were data, i.e. weighted MLE with $N_k = \sum_i \gamma_{ik}$:

$$\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{n}, \qquad \mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i \gamma_{ik}x_i, \qquad \Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i \gamma_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)(x_i - \mu_k)^\top$$

Repeat until the log-likelihood stops improving. Compare with Lloyd's algorithm (21.1): E-step = soft "assign", M-step = weighted "update". k-means is EM for a mixture with shared
spherical covariance $\sigma^2 I$ in the limit $\sigma \to 0$, where the responsibilities become 0 or 1 (the lab verifies it).

### Why it works: the lower bound

For any distribution $q(z)$ over the latent variable, Jensen's inequality (06.1) gives, for one point,

$$\log p(x \mid \theta) = \log\sum_z q(z)\frac{p(x, z \mid \theta)}{q(z)} \ \ge\ \sum_z q(z)\log\frac{p(x, z \mid \theta)}{q(z)} =: \mathcal{L}(q, \theta)$$

and the gap is exactly $\text{KL}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z \mid x, \theta)\big) \ge 0$. $\mathcal{L}$ is the **evidence lower bound (ELBO)**. EM is coordinate ascent on it:

- **E-step**: maximize $\mathcal{L}$ over $q$ with $\theta$ fixed. The KL gap is zero when $q(z) = p(z \mid x, \theta)$: the responsibilities. The bound now touches the log-likelihood.
- **M-step**: maximize $\mathcal{L}$ over $\theta$ with $q$ fixed. The bound goes up, so the log-likelihood (which is above it) goes up at least as much.

So **the log-likelihood never decreases**, and EM converges to a local maximum (or saddle). Local, as with k-means: initialize well (k-means++ centers are the usual choice) and restart
several times. The lab checks at every iteration that the ELBO is below the log-likelihood and touches it after the E-step.

When the posterior $p(z \mid x)$ is intractable (as with a neural network decoder), you can't do the exact E-step; you restrict $q$ to a family and maximize the ELBO over it by
gradient descent. That's variational inference, and a VAE is exactly that (31.1).

## Things that go wrong

**Singularities.** Put one component's mean exactly on a data point and shrink its covariance: the density at that point goes to infinity, and so does the likelihood. The MLE of a
GMM doesn't exist; EM can find these degenerate spikes, especially with duplicated points or more components than needed. Fixes: add a small **ridge to the covariances**
(`reg_covar` in scikit-learn), put a prior on them (MAP, 04.4), or restart when a component collapses. The lab provokes a collapse.

**Too many parameters.** A full covariance in $d$ dimensions has $d(d+1)/2$ parameters per component. The **covariance type** trades flexibility for parameters:

| Type | Each component's covariance | Parameters per component (covariance only) |
|---|---|---|
| `full` | any | $d(d+1)/2$ |
| `tied` | one shared full matrix | $d(d+1)/2$ total |
| `diag` | axis-aligned ellipse | $d$ |
| `spherical` | $\sigma_k^2 I$ | 1 |

**Choosing $K$ and the covariance type**: because a GMM has a likelihood, use an information criterion: **BIC** $= -2\log L + p\log n$ penalizes parameters and usually recovers the
right $K$ on data that really is a mixture. (Real data rarely is a Gaussian mixture, so treat it as a guide.) Bayesian GMMs with a Dirichlet-process prior
(`BayesianGaussianMixture`) can switch off unneeded components automatically.

## Uses

- **Soft clustering** with elliptical clusters of different sizes.
- **Density estimation**: a smooth, parametric $p(x)$ for anomaly detection (points with low log-likelihood are unusual, 23.3), or for classification with one GMM per class (19.2).
- **Generation**: sample new points (the lab does), or use a GMM as a prior in a bigger model.
- **Speaker and sensor models**, where GMMs were the standard before deep learning.
- **EM generally**: missing data (treat the missing values as latent), hidden Markov models (Baum-Welch is EM), semi-supervised learning, mixture-of-experts training in its original form.

## Things that will bite you

- **Likelihood going to infinity**: a collapsed component, not a great model. Use `reg_covar`.
- **Local maxima**: a single run from a bad start. Use several initializations.
- **Full covariances in high dimensions** with few points per component: singular or near-singular estimates. Use `diag` or `tied`, or reduce dimension first (21.3).
- **Unscaled features**, as always, though less damaging than for k-means (full covariances can absorb scale differences).
- **Reading BIC's choice as the number of "real" groups** in data that isn't a mixture of Gaussians.

## Exercises

1. Derive the M-step update for $\mu_k$ by maximizing $\sum_i\sum_k \gamma_{ik}\log\big(\pi_k\mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)\big)$.
2. Derive the update for $\pi_k$ (use a Lagrange multiplier for $\sum_k\pi_k = 1$).
3. Show that the gap between the log-likelihood and the ELBO is $\text{KL}(q\,\|\,p(z \mid x))$.
4. Show that with $\Sigma_k = \sigma^2 I$ for all $k$ and $\sigma \to 0$, the responsibilities become hard assignments to the nearest mean.
5. Why does the likelihood of a GMM have no maximum, and why doesn't this happen with a single Gaussian (fitted to more than one distinct point)?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The terms involving $\mu_k$ are $-\frac{1}{2}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_i - \mu_k)$. Gradient: $\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_i - \mu_k) = 0 \Rightarrow \mu_k = \sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i / \sum_i\gamma_{ik}$.

2. Maximize $\sum_k N_k\log\pi_k$ subject to $\sum_k\pi_k = 1$: $\frac{N_k}{\pi_k} = \lambda$, so $\pi_k = N_k/\lambda$; summing, $\lambda = \sum_k N_k = n$.

3. $\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}(q) = \sum_z q(z)\log p(x) - \sum_z q(z)\log\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)} = \sum_z q(z)\log\frac{q(z)\,p(x)}{p(x, z)} = \sum_z q(z)\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z \mid x)} = \text{KL}(q\,\|\,p(z \mid x))$.

4. $\gamma_{ik} \propto \pi_k\exp(-\|x_i - \mu_k\|^2 / 2\sigma^2)$. Dividing numerator and denominator by the term with the smallest distance, every other term has a factor $\exp(-(\|x_i - \mu_k\|^2 - d_{\min}^2)/2\sigma^2) \to 0$
   as $\sigma \to 0$ (for a strictly larger distance), so the responsibility of the nearest mean tends to 1 (the $\pi_k$ don't matter in the limit).

5. Set $\mu_1 = x_1$ and let $\Sigma_1 = \epsilon I \to 0$: the first component's density at $x_1$ grows like $\epsilon^{-d/2}$, so that point's likelihood term $\to \infty$, while the other components keep every other
   point's density bounded away from zero. The product is unbounded. A single Gaussian has no "other component" to cover the remaining points: shrinking its covariance makes their density go to zero
   faster than the one point's goes up, so the likelihood is bounded and maximized at the sample mean and covariance.
