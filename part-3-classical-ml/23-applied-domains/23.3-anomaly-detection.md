# 23.3 Anomaly detection

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 05.1 (robust statistics), 18.2 (precision at low prevalence), 19.1 (k-NN distances), 21.2 (GMM densities), 21.3 (PCA reconstruction), 23.1. Lab: [`lab_23_3_anomaly_detection.py`](labs/lab_23_3_anomaly_detection.py).*

Anomaly detection is the part of ML I've spent the most time on outside of "normal" prediction work, because it's what security monitoring, fraud
detection, infrastructure alerting and data quality checks (15.3) have in common: you're looking for the rare thing that doesn't fit, you usually
don't have labels for it, and the cost of getting it wrong is either a missed incident or an on-call engineer who has learned to ignore your alerts.
The second failure is more common and, in practice, just as bad. A detector nobody trusts is a detector that doesn't exist.

So this lesson is about methods, but also about base rates, alert budgets and context, which decide whether a detector is useful far more than
the choice between an isolation forest and a local outlier factor.

## What kind of anomaly?

- **Point anomalies**: one observation far from the rest (a $40,000 purchase on a card that usually spends $40).
- **Contextual anomalies**: normal in general, abnormal in context (daytime-level traffic at 3 a.m.; a login from a country that's normal for the company but not for this user). Most real ones are
  contextual, and most naive detectors miss them.
- **Collective anomalies**: individually unremarkable points that together aren't normal (a slow, steady exfiltration where each transfer is small; a sensor drifting by 0.1% a day).

And the setting: **outlier detection** (the training data itself contains anomalies, unlabeled), **novelty detection** (train on clean normal data, flag departures later), or **supervised** (you have labeled
anomalies: then it's imbalanced classification, 18.4, and usually better than any unsupervised method, if the future anomalies look like the past ones). Many systems combine them: unsupervised
detectors for the unknown, a supervised model for the known patterns, and rules for the things the business is sure about.

## Statistical methods: start here

- **z-score**: flag $|x - \bar x| / s > 3$. Breaks under contamination: the anomalies inflate $s$ and pull $\bar x$, **masking** themselves. Use **robust** estimates: the median and the MAD (median absolute
  deviation, scaled by 1.4826 to match $\sigma$ under normality). The lab shows the classical z-score missing an entire batch of bad readings (10% of the data, 5 standard deviations
out) that the robust one catches.
- **Multivariate**: a point can be ordinary in every feature and anomalous in combination (tall and light, high spend and zero sessions). The **Mahalanobis distance** $\sqrt{(x - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)}$
  measures distance in units of the data's own covariance, and catches points that break the correlation structure. Estimate $\mu, \Sigma$ robustly (the **minimum covariance determinant**,
  `MinCovDet`), for the same masking reason.
- **Streams**: for a metric over time, a **control chart** flags points outside $\pm 3\sigma$; **EWMA** and **CUSUM** charts accumulate small deviations and detect a small, *persistent* shift much sooner than a
  per-point threshold ever will. CUSUM: $S_t = \max(0, S_{t-1} + (x_t - \mu_0) - k)$, alarm when $S_t > h$. Cheap, well understood, and the right tool for "the error rate crept up by 20%".
- **Seasonal and contextual baselines**: compare each value with what's normal for *its context* (the same hour last weeks, a 23.1 forecast, the user's own history), and detect on the **residual**. This one idea
  turns most contextual anomalies into point anomalies.

## Machine-learning detectors

- **Distance-based**: distance to the $k$-th nearest neighbor (19.1). Simple and strong.
- **Local outlier factor** (LOF): compares a point's local density with its neighbors' densities, so it finds points that are sparse *relative to their neighborhood*: a point slightly off a very dense cluster can
  be anomalous even if it's closer to the data than points in a sparse cluster are.
- **Isolation forest** (Liu, Ting and Zhou, 2008): build random trees that split on random features at random thresholds. Anomalies are few and different, so they get **isolated in fewer splits**; the average
  path length to isolate a point is the score. No distances, no densities, linear time, handles many features. Often the best default. Its `contamination` parameter only sets the threshold; it doesn't change the scores.
- **Density models**: a GMM (21.2) or a kernel density estimate; score by low log-likelihood.
- **Reconstruction error**: fit a model of normal data that compresses and reconstructs it (PCA with a few components, 21.3; an autoencoder, 31.1); anomalies reconstruct badly. Works well when normal data lies near a
  low-dimensional subspace (sensor arrays, network traffic features).
- **One-class SVM** (19.3): learns a boundary around the normal data in kernel space. Sensitive to its parameters; less used now.

No method dominates. Each has a notion of "normal" built in, and each misses anomalies of a type its notion doesn't cover. The lab plants three types (global outliers, local outliers beside a dense
cluster, and correlation breakers) and shows every method winning on some and losing on others. In practice: understand what your anomalies look like, use features that expose them, and
ensemble a couple of detectors with different assumptions.

## Evaluation without many labels

- **Precision at the alert budget** (18.2): if the team can review 50 alerts a day, the metric is precision among the top 50 scores. ROC-AUC at 0.01% prevalence is decoration.
- **Injected anomalies**: plant synthetic anomalies of the types you care about and measure recall; honest about what it can't tell you (the anomalies you didn't think to inject).
- **Labeled incidents from the past**, however few, as a sanity check: would the detector have fired?
- **Analyst feedback** as labels over time, which turns an unsupervised detector into a semi-supervised one (and is the best source of improvement you'll have).

## Running a detector for real

- **Budget the alerts.** Set thresholds from the review capacity and the cost of misses, not from a percentile someone picked (18.3). Track alert volume as a first-class metric.
- **Explain every alert**: which features were unusual, compared with what baseline. An analyst who can triage an alert in 30 seconds will keep looking at them.
- **Normal drifts.** Retrain or re-baseline on a schedule, and keep known anomalies out of the "normal" data used for refitting, or the detector learns to accept them.
- **Adversaries adapt.** In fraud and security, the anomalies are generated by people who are trying not to look anomalous: they stay under thresholds, spread activity over time, and imitate normal behavior. That's
  an argument for contextual baselines per entity, collective and sequence-level features, and for never relying on one detector. It's also why I always test a detector against the evasions I'd
  try myself if I were on the other side, in a lab, before trusting it in production.

> **Scar tissue.** I trained an anomaly detector on three months of metrics from my home server, and it missed the next memory leak
> completely. The three months had included the previous one, a container slowly eating RAM over days. The detector had learned that a
> slowly climbing memory curve was normal. "Train on normal data" assumes you know which data was normal, and the incidents you never
> labeled are exactly the ones that teach your detector to ignore the next one.

## Things that will bite you

- **Classical mean and standard deviation** on contaminated data.
- **Global thresholds on contextual data** (seasonality, per-user behavior).
- **Unscaled features** in distance-based detectors.
- **ROC-AUC as the headline metric** at tiny prevalence.
- **Too many alerts.** The detector will be muted within a month.
- **Refitting on data that includes the anomalies** you should have caught.
- **One detector** with one notion of normal.

## Exercises

1. Why does the classical z-score fail when 5% of the data are large outliers? What does the MAD-based version do differently?
2. Give an example of a point that's unremarkable in every feature but anomalous jointly. Which of the methods in this lesson would catch it, and which wouldn't?
3. Explain why isolation forests need fewer random splits to isolate an anomaly than a normal point.
4. A metric's mean shifts by 0.5 standard deviations and stays there. Why will a 3-sigma control chart take a long time to notice, and why does CUSUM notice faster?
5. Your detector flags 2,000 events a day; the security team can review 40. What do you change first?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The outliers inflate the standard deviation and pull the mean toward themselves, so each outlier's z-score shrinks: they mask each other (and can push normal points over the line on the other side). The median
   and MAD have a 50% breakdown point: the outliers barely move them, so the scale stays that of the normal data and the outliers stand out.

2. Height 1.95 m and weight 55 kg: each is plausible alone, the combination is rare. Mahalanobis distance (with a covariance capturing the height-weight correlation), GMM density, PCA reconstruction error
   (the point is off the main correlation axis) and k-NN/LOF (it has no close neighbors) would catch it; per-feature z-scores and thresholds wouldn't. Isolation forests catch it only partially, since
   axis-aligned splits isolate correlation-breakers less efficiently.

3. A random split at a uniform threshold is more likely to separate a point that lies in a sparse, extreme region from the rest (there's a wide range of thresholds between it and the bulk), and anomalies
   are few, so each split that separates one also leaves it with few companions. Normal points sit in dense regions and need many splits before they're alone. Averaged over many random trees, the
   expected path length is short for anomalies and long for normal points.

4. Each observation is still mostly within $\pm 3\sigma$ (a 0.5$\sigma$ shift raises the per-point alarm probability only from about 0.27% to about 0.64%), so it takes about 150 points on average to fire. CUSUM
   adds up the small deviations above a reference value; a persistent shift makes the sum grow steadily and cross the threshold after a handful of observations, while noise alone keeps resetting it to zero.

5. Rank alerts by score and set the threshold to about 40 a day (the budget), then measure precision among those 40 with the team's feedback. Then improve precision: add context (per-entity baselines,
   seasonality), suppress known benign patterns, group related alerts into one incident, and add explanations. More alerts than capacity isn't more detection; it's less.
