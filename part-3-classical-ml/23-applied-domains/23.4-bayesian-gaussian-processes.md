# 23.4 Bayesian methods and Gaussian processes

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 04.2 (Bayes' rule), 04.4 (MLE and MAP), 05.1 (confidence intervals), 17.1 to 17.3, 19.3 (kernels), 21.2 (the ELBO), 22.2. Lab: [`lab_23_4_bayes_gp_conformal.py`](labs/lab_23_4_bayes_gp_conformal.py).*

Almost every model in this part produces a number. Decisions need to know how much to trust it. A demand forecast of 120 units means one thing if the
plausible range is 110 to 130 and something else if it's 20 to 400; a model that says "85% malignant" should be more careful on a patient unlike any
it was trained on. This lesson is about quantifying uncertainty, with two different philosophies and one pragmatic tool:

- **Bayesian inference**: treat unknown parameters as random variables, describe your uncertainty with a probability distribution, and update it with data. Principled, flexible, and computationally
  demanding. Gaussian processes are its most elegant special case: a distribution over *functions*.
- **Conformal prediction**: make almost no assumptions about the model, and get prediction intervals with a guaranteed coverage rate from a held-out calibration set. Blunt, cheap, and
  remarkably useful. It's what I reach for first in production when someone asks for intervals around a black-box model.

## The Bayesian recipe

Prior $p(\theta)$, likelihood $p(D \mid \theta)$, and Bayes' rule:

$$p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(D)}, \qquad p(y^* \mid x^*, D) = \int p(y^* \mid x^*, \theta)\,p(\theta \mid D)\,d\theta$$

The posterior is the full answer about $\theta$; the **posterior predictive** averages predictions over it, so parameter uncertainty flows into the prediction. MAP (04.4) keeps only the posterior's mode and
throws the rest away; ridge regression is MAP with a Gaussian prior (17.2), and it doesn't know how unsure it is.

**Conjugate priors** make the posterior the same family as the prior, in closed form. The one you'll use constantly: a conversion rate with a **Beta** prior and binomial data. Prior $\text{Beta}(a, b)$, observe $s$
successes in $n$ trials, posterior $\text{Beta}(a + s, b + n - s)$. The lab uses it for a Bayesian A/B test (05.3): the probability that B beats A, and the expected loss of choosing each, straight from posterior
samples, which is often an easier conversation with stakeholders than a p-value.

**Credible vs confidence intervals**: a 95% credible interval contains the parameter with 95% posterior probability, given the prior and the data. A 95% confidence interval (05.1) is produced by a procedure that
covers the true parameter in 95% of repeated experiments. With lots of data and weak priors they're numerically similar; their meanings aren't, and a credible interval is what most people wrongly
think a confidence interval is.

## Partial pooling

Estimate the conversion rate of 50 stores, some with thousands of visitors and some with twelve. Per-store estimates (**no pooling**) are wildly noisy for the small stores: the store with 3 conversions in 12
visits "converts at 25%". One global rate (**complete pooling**) ignores real differences. A **hierarchical model** assumes each store's rate comes from a shared distribution whose parameters are also learned;
each store's estimate is **shrunk** toward the group mean, more strongly when it has less data. This is partial pooling, and it's one of the most useful ideas in applied statistics: small-sample groups
borrow strength from the others. The lab fits it by empirical Bayes (estimating the shared Beta prior from all stores) and shows it beating both extremes on known true rates.

## Bayesian linear regression

With a Gaussian prior $w \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2 I)$ and Gaussian noise, the posterior over the weights is Gaussian in closed form:

$$\Sigma_{\text{post}} = \Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}X^\top X + \frac{1}{\tau^2}I\Big)^{-1}, \qquad \mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\Sigma_{\text{post}}X^\top y$$

(the posterior mean is the ridge solution with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$). The predictive variance at $x^*$ is $\sigma^2 + x^{*\top}\Sigma_{\text{post}}x^*$: **noise** (aleatoric uncertainty, irreducible) plus **parameter uncertainty**
(epistemic, shrinks with data). The second term grows as $x^*$ moves away from the training data, which is exactly the behavior you want and a point estimate can't give. `BayesianRidge` in scikit-learn
also estimates $\sigma$ and $\tau$ from the data.

## When there's no closed form

Most posteriors (logistic regression, hierarchical models, anything nonlinear) have no closed form. Three families of approximation:

- **MCMC** (Markov chain Monte Carlo): build a random walk whose stationary distribution is the posterior, run it, and use the visited points as samples. **Metropolis-Hastings** proposes a move and accepts it with
  probability $\min(1, p(\theta')/p(\theta))$ (only ratios, so the normalizing constant $p(D)$ is never needed). **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** and its adaptive version **NUTS** use gradients to make long, efficient moves,
  and are what Stan and PyMC run. Check convergence: several chains from different starts, the $\hat R$ statistic near 1, enough effective samples. The lab implements Metropolis for a logistic regression and compares
  the posterior with 17.3's MLE and Wald intervals.
- **Variational inference**: pick a tractable family $q$ and maximize the ELBO (21.2) over it. Fast and scalable, tends to underestimate posterior variance. VAEs (31.1) are amortized variational inference.
- **Laplace approximation**: a Gaussian centered at the MAP with covariance from the inverse Hessian. That's exactly what 17.3's Wald standard errors are.

For deep networks, approximate Bayesian inference (MC dropout, deep ensembles, Laplace on the last layer) is an active area; **deep ensembles** (train several networks from different seeds, use their
spread) are the strong practical baseline.

## Gaussian processes

A GP is a prior over functions: any finite set of function values $f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n)$ is jointly Gaussian, with mean $m(x)$ (usually 0) and covariance given by a **kernel** $k(x, x')$ (19.3). The kernel encodes
assumptions: the RBF kernel says nearby inputs have similar outputs, with the length scale saying how near is near; periodic, linear and Matérn kernels encode other shapes, and sums and products
combine them.

With Gaussian noise $\sigma^2$, the posterior at test points $X_*$ is Gaussian with

$$\mu_* = K_{*}^\top(K + \sigma^2I)^{-1}y, \qquad \Sigma_* = K_{**} - K_{*}^\top(K + \sigma^2I)^{-1}K_{*}$$

where $K$ is the kernel matrix of the training inputs and $K_*$ between training and test inputs. The mean is a kernel-weighted combination of the training targets (kernel ridge regression, 19.3's
representer theorem); the variance is small near data and grows back to the prior far from it. **Hyperparameters** (length scale, signal variance, noise) are fitted by maximizing the **marginal likelihood**
$p(y \mid X)$, which trades fit against complexity automatically. Compute with a Cholesky factorization, never an explicit inverse (02.3).

The cost is $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory: GPs are for up to a few thousand points (sparse and inducing-point approximations go further). Within that range they're superb: small-data regression with
honest uncertainty, the surrogate model in Bayesian optimization (22.2), emulators for expensive simulations, spatial statistics (where they're called kriging). The lab builds one from scratch and checks
it against scikit-learn.

## Conformal prediction

All of the above is only as good as its assumptions: a wrong prior, a misspecified likelihood or a bad kernel gives confidently wrong intervals. **Conformal prediction** gives a coverage guarantee that
holds for *any* model, under one assumption: the calibration data and the future data are **exchangeable** (roughly, i.i.d.).

**Split conformal regression**, in full:

1. Train any model on a training set.
2. On a separate **calibration** set of $n$ points, compute the scores $s_i = |y_i - \hat f(x_i)|$.
3. Let $\hat q$ be the $\lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha)\rceil / n$ empirical quantile of the scores.
4. For a new $x$, predict the interval $[\hat f(x) - \hat q,\ \hat f(x) + \hat q]$.

Then $P(y_{\text{new}} \in \text{interval}) \ge 1 - \alpha$, exactly, in finite samples, whatever the model. The proof is a symmetry argument: the new point's score is equally likely to rank anywhere among the
calibration scores.

Refinements: **normalized scores** ($|y - \hat f(x)| / \hat\sigma(x)$ with a model of the spread) or **conformalized quantile regression** (CQR: start from quantile regression intervals, 20.3, and conformalize
them) give intervals that adapt to heteroskedasticity instead of a constant width. For classification, conformal methods produce **prediction sets** (the set of labels whose score clears the calibrated
threshold), which grow when the model is unsure.

What the guarantee is and isn't: it's **marginal** coverage (averaged over all $x$), not coverage for every subgroup; and it's void when exchangeability fails, as under distribution shift or in time series
(23.1: use rolling or weighted variants and check coverage in the backtest). The lab shows exact coverage on i.i.d. data and the failure under shift.

## Things that will bite you

- **MAP presented as Bayesian**: a point estimate with a prior isn't a posterior.
- **Unchecked MCMC**: one chain, no $\hat R$, no effective sample size.
- **Priors nobody wrote down** or that dominate small data invisibly. Do a prior predictive check: simulate from the prior and see if the data it implies is plausible.
- **GPs on 100,000 points** without approximations.
- **Conformal intervals under drift**, or read as conditional (per-subgroup) guarantees.
- **Model-based intervals trusted without measuring their coverage** on held-out data.

## Exercises

1. Derive the Beta-Binomial posterior. What prior corresponds to "I've seen 2 successes and 8 failures before"?
2. Show that the posterior mean of Bayesian linear regression is the ridge solution with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$.
3. In Metropolis-Hastings with a symmetric proposal, why is the normalizing constant $p(D)$ not needed?
4. A GP with an RBF kernel is asked to predict far from all training points. What are its predictive mean and variance there, and why?
5. Prove the split conformal coverage guarantee in the case where all scores are distinct, using exchangeability.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $p(\theta \mid s) \propto \theta^s(1 - \theta)^{n-s} \cdot \theta^{a-1}(1 - \theta)^{b-1} = \theta^{a+s-1}(1 - \theta)^{b+n-s-1}$: a $\text{Beta}(a + s, b + n - s)$. "2 successes and 8 failures" is roughly $\text{Beta}(2, 8)$ (or $\text{Beta}(3, 9)$ if you start from a uniform
   $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$): the parameters act as pseudo-counts.

2. The log posterior is $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|y - Xw\|^2 - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|w\|^2 + \text{const}$. Multiply by $-2\sigma^2$: minimize $\|y - Xw\|^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\|w\|^2$, ridge with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$. For a Gaussian posterior, the mean equals the mode.

3. The acceptance probability uses the ratio $p(\theta' \mid D)/p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid \theta')p(\theta')}{p(D \mid \theta)p(\theta)}$; $p(D)$ appears in both numerator and denominator and cancels. (With an asymmetric proposal $q$, the ratio
   $q(\theta \mid \theta')/q(\theta' \mid \theta)$ is included too, still without $p(D)$.)

4. Far from the data, $k(x_*, x_i) \approx 0$ for all $i$, so $K_* \approx 0$: the mean reverts to the prior mean (0, or whatever $m(x)$ is) and the variance to the prior variance $k(x_*, x_*)$ (plus noise). The model says "I have
   no information here", which is the right answer; a point-estimate model would extrapolate with unwarranted confidence.

5. The calibration scores $s_1, \dots, s_n$ and the test score $s_{n+1}$ are exchangeable, so the rank of $s_{n+1}$ among all $n + 1$ is uniformly distributed on $\{1, \dots, n+1\}$. The test point is covered iff $s_{n+1} \le \hat q$, where $\hat q$
   is the $k$-th smallest calibration score with $k = \lceil(n + 1)(1 - \alpha)\rceil$, i.e. iff $s_{n+1}$'s rank is at most $k$. That has probability $k/(n + 1) \ge 1 - \alpha$.
