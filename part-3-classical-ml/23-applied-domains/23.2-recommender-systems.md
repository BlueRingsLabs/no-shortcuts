# 23.2 Recommender systems

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 19.1 (nearest neighbors, ANN), 02.4 (low-rank factorization), 17.2 (regularization), 18.2 (ranking metrics), 05.3 (A/B tests). Lab: [`lab_23_2_recommenders.py`](labs/lab_23_2_recommenders.py).*

Recommenders are probably the most economically important ML systems in existence: what you watch, buy, read and listen to next is largely chosen by one.
They're also a good lesson in how a clean textbook problem ("fill in the missing entries of a ratings matrix") differs from the real one ("rank a
catalog of ten million items for this user, in 50 milliseconds, without recommending the same bestsellers to everyone, and prove it made money").

This lesson builds the core methods (neighborhood methods, matrix factorization for implicit feedback, two-tower retrieval) on data where we know
the ground truth, and spends real time on evaluation, because recommender evaluation is where the published literature has the most embarrassing
results.

## The problem, as it really is

- **Feedback is mostly implicit.** Clicks, views, purchases, listens, dwell time. Explicit star ratings are rare and biased (people rate what they loved or hated). Implicit feedback has no negatives:
  a user who didn't click an item may not have seen it. Absence isn't dislike.
- **The output is a ranking**: the top $k$ items for a user, from a huge catalog. Accuracy of predicted ratings barely matters; the quality of the top of the list does.
- **Cold start**: new users and new items have no interactions. Content features (item attributes, user context) are the only way in.
- **Popularity bias and feedback loops**: popular items get recommended, get more interactions, get recommended more (18.4's feedback loop). A system that only recommends bestsellers can look good
  offline and be useless (the user would have found them anyway).
- **Scale and latency** force a **multi-stage architecture**: a cheap **candidate generation** stage retrieves hundreds of items from millions (two-tower embeddings plus approximate nearest neighbors,
  19.1, 41.2); a **ranking** stage scores them with a richer model (often gradient boosting or a deep network with many features, 20.4); a **re-ranking** stage applies diversity, freshness and business
  rules.

## Baselines

- **Most popular** items (globally, or recently, or per segment). Shockingly competitive on many offline benchmarks, which tells you more about the benchmarks than about the method.
- **Item-to-item co-occurrence** ("people who bought this also bought"): count how often items are consumed by the same users, normalize (cosine similarity between item columns of the interaction
  matrix), and recommend items similar to the user's history. Simple, explainable, scales, and was Amazon's workhorse for years (Linden, Smith and York, 2003).

## Matrix factorization

Model the user-item matrix $R$ ($m$ users × $n$ items) as low-rank: each user $u$ and item $i$ get a vector in $\mathbb{R}^k$, and the affinity is $\hat r_{ui} = p_u^\top q_i$ (plus biases for users and items, in the explicit
case). It's 02.4's low-rank approximation with most entries missing, and the same idea as PCA (21.3) applied to a matrix with holes.

**Explicit ratings**: minimize squared error over the *observed* entries only, with L2 regularization (17.2):

$$\min_{P, Q} \sum_{(u,i) \in \text{observed}}(r_{ui} - p_u^\top q_i)^2 + \lambda(\|p_u\|^2 + \|q_i\|^2)$$

by SGD or by **alternating least squares** (fix $Q$, and each $p_u$ is a ridge regression with a closed form; then fix $P$ and solve for each $q_i$; repeat). This is the Netflix Prize model (Koren, Bell and
Volinsky, 2009).

**Implicit feedback** (Hu, Koren and Volinsky, 2008): treat *every* user-item pair as an observation. The preference is $p_{ui} = 1$ if there was an interaction, else 0; the **confidence** is
$c_{ui} = 1 + \alpha\,r_{ui}$ (more interactions, more confidence). Minimize $\sum_{u,i} c_{ui}(p_{ui} - x_u^\top y_i)^2 + \lambda(\ldots)$. It sums over all $mn$ pairs, which sounds infeasible, but the ALS update for user $u$ is

$$x_u = (Y^\top C^u Y + \lambda I)^{-1}Y^\top C^u p(u), \qquad Y^\top C^u Y = Y^\top Y + Y^\top(C^u - I)Y$$

and $Y^\top Y$ is shared by all users, while $C^u - I$ is nonzero only on the user's few interacted items. So each update costs time proportional to the user's history, not the catalog. The lab implements it
in about twenty lines.

**Ranking losses**: instead of regressing on 0/1, optimize the ranking directly. **BPR** (Rendle et al., 2009) samples triples (user, an item they interacted with, an item they didn't) and maximizes
$\log\sigma(\hat r_{ui} - \hat r_{uj})$: "rank the positive above the negative". The negative sampling strategy matters a lot.

## Two-tower models

Generalize the factorization: the user vector is the output of a **user tower** (a neural network over the user's ID, history, context, demographics) and the item vector is the output of an
**item tower** (over the item's ID, category, text, image embeddings). The score is still a dot product, which is what makes **retrieval** possible: precompute all item vectors, index them for approximate
nearest-neighbor search (19.1's IVF, 41.2's HNSW), and at request time compute one user vector and look up its neighbors.

Training: treat each observed interaction as a positive and use the **other items in the same mini-batch as negatives** (in-batch softmax). Cheap and effective, with a subtle bias: popular items appear as
negatives more often and get pushed down, which is corrected by subtracting each item's log-popularity from its logit (the "logQ" correction, Yi et al., 2019). In the lab
that one line more than doubles recall.

Because the item tower can use content features, **a brand-new item gets a sensible vector from its attributes alone**: the cold-start fix. The lab trains a small two-tower model in PyTorch with a category
feature and recommends items that had zero training interactions.

## Evaluation, and how the literature fooled itself

**Offline metrics** (18.2) for top-$k$ lists: **recall@k** / **hit rate@k** (did the held-out items appear in the top $k$?), **precision@k**, **MAP**, and **NDCG@k** (discounted cumulative gain: hits near the top count more,
normalized by the best possible ordering). Plus **catalog coverage** (what fraction of items ever gets recommended) and **diversity** and **novelty**, to catch the bestseller trap.

The split matters (18.1): use a **temporal** split (train on interactions before a date, test on after) or leave-last-out per user. Random splits leak the future and inflate everything.

Two cautionary results worth knowing:

- **Sampled metrics** (rank each held-out item against 100 random negatives instead of the full catalog, to save compute) are inconsistent with the full metrics: they can **reverse the ranking of models**
  (Krichene and Rendle, 2020). The lab shows it.
- **Weak baselines**: a replication study of neural recommendation papers (Ferrari Dacrema et al., 2019) found that most couldn't be reproduced, and most of the rest were beaten by well-tuned simple
  neighborhood methods. Tune your baselines as seriously as your model (22.2).

**Offline isn't online.** Offline metrics reward predicting what users did under the *old* system; the new system changes what they see. The only real test is an online experiment (05.3, 50.2) on the
metric the business cares about (retention, revenue, long-term engagement), with guardrails for the effects you can't see in clicks.

## Things that will bite you

- **Treating non-interactions as dislikes.**
- **Random train/test splits.**
- **Sampled evaluation metrics** used to compare models.
- **Untuned baselines** (popularity and item-item are strong).
- **Popularity collapse**: great offline numbers, the same 50 items for everyone. Measure coverage.
- **Cold start** with an ID-only model.
- **Feedback loops**: training only on what the last model chose to show.

## Exercises

1. Why can't you treat a missing interaction as a rating of 0 in implicit feedback? How does the Hu et al. model handle this?
2. Derive the ALS update for one user in the explicit model: minimize $\sum_{i \in I_u}(r_{ui} - p_u^\top q_i)^2 + \lambda\|p_u\|^2$ over $p_u$.
3. Explain why the implicit ALS update can be computed in time proportional to the user's number of interactions rather than the catalog size.
4. A new model beats popularity on hit rate@10 with 100 sampled negatives, but loses with full ranking. How is that possible?
5. Design the candidate-generation and ranking stages for a news recommender where articles live for about 24 hours. What does the short lifetime imply for the item representation?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Most non-interactions are items the user never saw, not items they rejected; treating them as ratings of 0 with full weight would teach the model that users dislike most of the catalog. Hu et al. keep every pair
   but give observed interactions high confidence and non-interactions a low baseline confidence (1), so non-interactions count as weak evidence of low preference, overridden where there's data.

2. Gradient: $-2\sum_{i \in I_u}(r_{ui} - p_u^\top q_i)q_i + 2\lambda p_u = 0 \Rightarrow \big(\sum_{i \in I_u}q_iq_i^\top + \lambda I\big)p_u = \sum_{i \in I_u}r_{ui}q_i$, i.e. $p_u = (Q_u^\top Q_u + \lambda I)^{-1}Q_u^\top r_u$: a ridge regression of the user's ratings on the
   item vectors (17.2).

3. $Y^\top C^uY = Y^\top Y + Y^\top(C^u - I)Y$. The first term is the same for all users (compute once per sweep, $O(nk^2)$). $C^u - I$ is diagonal with nonzeros only on the user's $n_u$ interacted items, so the second term costs
   $O(n_uk^2)$. Likewise $Y^\top C^up(u)$ only involves those items ($p(u)$ is zero elsewhere). Then solve a $k \times k$ system: $O(k^3)$.

4. With 100 random negatives, most negatives are obscure items that any reasonable model ranks low; the test only checks whether the positive beats *those*. A model that ranks the positive above
   obscure items but below many popular ones scores well sampled and badly on the full catalog, where the popular items are the real competition. Popularity is hurt less by the full ranking
   because it already puts popular items first. The two metrics measure different things, and the sampled one can reverse the ordering.

5. Candidate generation can't rely on item IDs (each article has hours to accumulate interactions): represent articles by their content (text embeddings of title and body, topic, source, entities) so the
   item tower produces vectors immediately at publication, plus a recency filter. Users: embeddings of recently read articles, topics followed, time of day. Ranking: a model with freshness, source,
   predicted click and dwell, the user's topic affinities, trending signals. Retrain or refresh item embeddings continuously, and re-rank for diversity so one story doesn't fill the page.
