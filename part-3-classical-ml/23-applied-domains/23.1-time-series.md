# 23.1 Time series forecasting

*About 11 hours. Prerequisites: 18.1 (temporal splits), 18.2 (MAPE and friends), 20.1 (trees don't extrapolate), 20.4, 22.1, 05.1. Lab: [`lab_23_1_time_series.py`](labs/lab_23_1_time_series.py).*

Forecasting is where machine learning gets humbled most reliably. In the M4 forecasting competition (100,000 series), most of the sophisticated
machine-learning entries lost to simple statistical methods, and some lost to a *seasonal naive* forecast ("next Monday will look like last Monday").
Things changed in M5 (Walmart sales), where gradient-boosted trees trained across thousands of series won. Both results are instructive, and this
lesson teaches you both toolkits: the statistical models that are hard to beat on a single series, and the machine-learning reformulation that wins
when you have many related series and good covariates. Plus the thing that matters more than either: evaluating forecasts honestly.

## What makes time series different

Rows are ordered and **dependent**: today resembles yesterday (autocorrelation), and the series has structure in time: **trend** (long-run movement), **seasonality** (patterns with a fixed
period: hour of day, day of week, month of year), **holidays and events**, **level shifts** (a new store opens), and noise. And the future isn't drawn from the same distribution as the past:
the series is often **nonstationary**.

That breaks the i.i.d. assumption behind 18.1's default cross-validation, and answers this module's self-check: **with random k-fold, the model trains on points after the ones it's tested on**.
Neighboring days are highly correlated, so a random validation day's neighbors (yesterday and tomorrow) are in training, and the model interpolates between them instead of forecasting. The score
measures interpolation, and production requires extrapolation. The lab measures the gap. Every evaluation in this lesson is **backtesting**: train on the past, test on the future, roll forward.

## Baselines first

These are embarrassingly simple and embarrassingly hard to beat. Always compute them:

- **Naive**: $\hat y_{t+h} = y_t$.
- **Seasonal naive**: $\hat y_{t+h} = y_{t+h-m}$ with season length $m$ (7 for daily data with a weekly pattern).
- **Mean** and **drift** (the naive forecast plus the average historical change per step).

A scale-free metric built on them is **MASE** (mean absolute scaled error): the forecast's MAE divided by the in-sample MAE of the seasonal naive forecast. MASE < 1 means you beat seasonal naive;
MASE > 1 means your model is worse than "same as last week", a sentence I've had to say to more people than I'd like, myself included. Unlike MAPE (18.2), it's defined at zero and symmetric.

## Decomposition and stationarity

**STL** (seasonal-trend decomposition using Loess) splits a series into trend, seasonal and remainder components; plotting it is the best first look at any series. **Stationarity** (constant mean,
variance and autocorrelation over time) is what classical models assume; you get closer to it by **differencing** ($y_t - y_{t-1}$ removes a trend; $y_t - y_{t-m}$ removes seasonality) and by variance-stabilizing
transforms (log, Box-Cox, 17.1). The **autocorrelation function** (ACF: correlation of the series with its own lags) and the **partial autocorrelation** (PACF: the correlation at lag $k$ after accounting
for shorter lags) show what memory structure is left.

## Exponential smoothing

A forecast as a weighted average of past observations, with weights decaying exponentially into the past:

- **Simple exponential smoothing**: level only, $\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha)\ell_{t-1}$, forecast $\hat y_{t+h} = \ell_t$.
- **Holt**: add a trend component (optionally **damped**, which almost always forecasts better over long horizons, because trends don't continue forever).
- **Holt-Winters**: add a seasonal component, additive or multiplicative.

Written as state-space models (**ETS**: error, trend, seasonal), they have a likelihood, so parameters are fitted by maximum likelihood, model forms are chosen by AIC, and prediction intervals come out
naturally. Fast, robust, and the thing to beat on a single series.

## ARIMA

**AR($p$)**: regress $y_t$ on its own last $p$ values. **MA($q$)**: regress on the last $q$ forecast errors. **I($d$)**: difference $d$ times first. **SARIMA** adds the same at the seasonal lag. Choose orders
from the ACF/PACF (the Box-Jenkins method) or by searching AIC (auto-ARIMA). Covariates enter as **ARIMAX** / regression with ARIMA errors. ARIMA and ETS overlap a lot; neither dominates, and averaging
their forecasts is a well-known cheap improvement.

## Forecasting as supervised learning

Reframe: each row is a (series, time) pair; the target is the value $h$ steps ahead; the features are what's known at forecast time:

- **Lags** of the target ($y_{t}, y_{t-1}, y_{t-7}, \dots$) and **rolling statistics** (mean and standard deviation over the last 7, 28 days).
- **Calendar**: day of week, month, holidays, paydays (22.1).
- **Known future covariates**: planned promotions, prices, scheduled events.
- **Series identifiers and static attributes** (store size, product category) for **global models** trained on many series at once, which share information across series. That's how M5 was won.

Pitfalls specific to this setting:

- **Every feature must be available at forecast time for the horizon you're predicting.** A lag-1 feature is fine for a 1-day-ahead forecast and a leak for a 7-day-ahead one. Either build
  **direct** models (one per horizon, using only lags $\ge h$) or forecast **recursively** (predict tomorrow, feed it back as a lag, predict the day after; errors compound).
- **Trees don't extrapolate** (20.1). A trending series leaves the training range, and a GBDT forecasts a flat line at the last leaf's value. Model the differences or the ratio to a recent
  level instead of the raw value, or detrend first. The lab shows the failure and the fix.
- **Nonstationarity** means old data may hurt: weight recent data more or use a sliding window.

## Evaluating forecasts

- **Rolling-origin backtesting** (time series cross-validation): several forecast origins, each training on everything before it and forecasting the next $H$ steps, with a gap if features need it
  (18.1's `TimeSeriesSplit`). Report error **by horizon**: day-1 and day-28 forecasts are different products.
- **Metrics**: MAE and RMSE in units, MASE for comparisons across series, WAPE for aggregated business reporting, and pinball loss (18.2) for quantile forecasts. Be wary of MAPE (it
  rewards under-forecasting and explodes near zero).
- **Probabilistic forecasts**: decisions (inventory, staffing, capacity) need intervals, not points. ETS and ARIMA give model-based intervals (often too narrow, because they ignore model
  uncertainty); quantile regression (20.3) gives them directly; **conformal prediction** (23.4) calibrates any of them using backtest residuals. Check the empirical coverage in the backtest.

## Beyond this lesson

**Hierarchical forecasting** (store → region → country forecasts that add up, via reconciliation methods like MinT), **intermittent demand** (mostly zeros: Croston's method and its variants), and **deep
learning** models (N-BEATS, DeepAR, temporal fusion transformers, and pretrained "foundation" forecasters). The deep models can win with many series and rich covariates; on a single short
series, a well-tuned ETS usually still wins. Benchmark against seasonal naive and ETS before believing any of them.

## Things that will bite you

- **Random k-fold on time series.**
- **Not beating seasonal naive**, and not checking.
- **Lag features unavailable at the forecast horizon.**
- **Trees on a trending level.**
- **MAPE on series with small values.**
- **Point forecasts** where the decision needs a quantile.
- **One backtest origin** (one lucky or unlucky period decides everything).

## Exercises

1. Why can't you use random K-fold cross-validation on a time series? Describe what the model gets to see that it won't in production.
2. Compute MASE for a forecast with MAE 12 on a series whose in-sample seasonal naive MAE is 15. What does it mean?
3. You need a 14-day-ahead daily forecast. Which lag features can a direct model for horizon 14 use? What changes for a recursive approach?
4. Why does damping the trend in Holt's method usually improve long-horizon forecasts?
5. A GBDT forecast of a growing product's weekly sales is flat for the next 3 months. What happened and what are two fixes?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Random folds put the neighbors of each validation point (the previous and next days) in the training set. Because the series is autocorrelated and trending, the model predicts validation points by
   interpolating between known neighbors, and it sees future values (after the validation point) during training. In production it only ever has the past and must extrapolate forward. The random-fold
   score is therefore optimistic, sometimes wildly. Use expanding- or sliding-window backtests.

2. MASE = 12 / 15 = 0.8: the forecast's error is 80% of what "same as last season" achieves in-sample. It beats the seasonal naive baseline by 20%.

3. Only lags of 14 days or more ($y_{t-14}, y_{t-15}, \dots$ relative to the target date) and rolling statistics ending at least 14 days before the target date, plus known-in-advance covariates (calendar,
   planned promotions). A recursive model uses its own predictions for days $t+1..t+13$ as lags, so it can use short lags, at the cost of compounding its errors.

4. Undamped Holt extrapolates the current trend linearly forever, and real trends flatten (markets saturate, growth slows). A damped trend converges to a constant level, which is a more
   conservative and, empirically, more accurate long-range assumption. Large forecasting competitions found damped trend methods consistently strong.

5. Trees predict constants per leaf and can't produce values outside the training range; a growing series leaves that range, so every future point lands in the "highest values seen" leaf. Fixes:
   predict the change from the last known value (or the ratio to a recent average) instead of the level, and add it back; detrend with a linear or ETS model and let the trees model the
   residual; or use a model that extrapolates (linear, ETS) for the trend component.
