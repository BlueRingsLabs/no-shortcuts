# 46.1 Bandits and exploration

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 04.1 and 04.2 (probability, Bayes), 05.3 (A/B testing). Lab: [`lab_46_1_bandits.py`](labs/lab_46_1_bandits.py).*

Reinforcement learning is learning from interaction: an agent acts, the world responds with a reward, and the agent adjusts. There are no
labels telling it what the right action was, only a number saying how that one went. The simplest case strips away everything except the
core dilemma. There's no state, no sequence of decisions, just a set of options with unknown payoffs, and every choice is both a way to
earn and a way to learn. That's the **multi-armed bandit**, named after a row of slot machines. It's also, unlike most of this module, a
thing you'll deploy directly: page variants, recommendations, ad creatives, pricing experiments, and which model or prompt a router
tries (45.1).

## The problem

$K$ arms; pulling arm $a$ gives a reward drawn from an unknown distribution with mean $\mu_a$. The goal is to maximize total reward over
$T$ pulls or, equivalently, to minimize **regret**, the reward lost compared with always pulling the best arm:

$$\text{Regret}(T) = T\mu^* - \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^T \mu_{a_t}\Big].$$

Pulling the arm that looks best so far (**exploiting**) earns now. Pulling others (**exploring**) earns information, which pays later.
Too little exploration and you lock onto a mediocre arm forever; too much and you keep paying for information you already have.

The lab has 10 arms with Bernoulli rewards, like 10 versions of a page with conversion rates between 2% and 12%: small and close
together, as real conversion rates are. It runs 400 independent problems of 10,000 pulls each.

## The algorithms

- **Greedy**: always pull the arm with the highest average so far. It locks onto whichever arm paid first.
- **$\varepsilon$-greedy**: greedy, except with probability $\varepsilon$ pull a random arm. Simple and robust, and it explores at the
  same rate forever.
- **Optimistic initial values**: start every estimate high, so untried arms look attractive until tried. It explores early and then
  stops, which is fine in a fixed world and bad in a changing one. (Implementation detail that bites: with plain sample averages the
  optimism vanishes after one pull, so give the prior some weight, as pseudo-counts.)
- **UCB1** (Auer et al., 2002): pull the arm with the highest *upper confidence bound*, $\hat\mu_a + \sqrt{2\ln t / N_a}$. Optimism in the
  face of uncertainty, done properly: arms are explored exactly as long as they might be the best.
- **KL-UCB** (Garivier and Cappé, 2011): the same idea, with a confidence bound that's tight for Bernoulli rewards.
- **Thompson sampling** (Thompson, 1933): keep a posterior over each arm's mean (a Beta distribution for Bernoulli rewards), draw one
  sample from each posterior, and pull the arm whose sample is highest. Arms are pulled with the probability that they're the best.

|---|---|---|---|
| greedy | 40.3 | 403.2 | 0.09 |
| $\varepsilon$-greedy, 0.01 | 35.4 | 179.6 | 0.39 |
| $\varepsilon$-greedy, 0.1 | 22.3 | 108.5 | 0.49 |
| optimistic initial values | 36.0 | 145.8 | 0.65 |
| UCB1 | 36.8 | 302.6 | 0.21 |
| KL-UCB | 28.2 | 135.4 | 0.54 |
| Thompson sampling | 27.5 | 101.9 | 0.61 |

Three lessons from one table:

1. **Greedy is terrible**, and it's what people build by default ("show the variant with the best rate so far").
2. **The theory is asymptotic.** UCB and Thompson have regret that grows like $\log T$, which is optimal (Lai and Robbins, 1985).
   $\varepsilon$-greedy's grows linearly, because it keeps exploring at a fixed rate. The lab shows this: the regret added between
   pulls 5,000 and 10,000, relative to that added between 1,000 and 5,000, is 0.92 for $\varepsilon$-greedy and 0.60 for Thompson. But at
   10,000 pulls with gaps this small, a well-tuned $\varepsilon$-greedy is still competitive. Over a long horizon the logarithm wins; over
   a short one, constants matter.
3. **Constants matter a lot.** UCB1's bonus assumes the worst-case variance of a reward in $[0, 1]$. For conversion rates near 5% the true
   variance is tiny, so UCB1 explores far too long, and it's the worst of the principled methods here. KL-UCB, with the right
   confidence bound for Bernoulli rewards, recovers most of the gap.

Thompson sampling is usually the one to deploy. It performs well, has no exploration parameter to tune, and extends naturally: informative
priors from past experiments, batched updates when feedback arrives in bulk, delayed rewards, and contextual versions (below).

## A changing world

Conversion rates drift with seasons, campaigns and novelty effects. Sample averages weight the first reward as much as the last, so they
track change slowly. A constant step size, $Q \leftarrow Q + \alpha(r - Q)$, forgets exponentially and tracks the drift: in the lab, with
rates doing a slow random walk, it cuts $\varepsilon$-greedy's regret from 577 to 352. Thompson sampling gets the same benefit from
discounting old observations in its posterior.

## Bandits vs A/B tests

A bandit and an A/B test (05.3) answer different questions:

- An **A/B test** asks "which version is better, and by how much?", with a pre-registered sample size, even allocation and valid
  confidence intervals. It pays for that with regret during the test: in the lab, an even split for 3,000 pulls followed by the winner
  has regret 163, against Thompson's 102.
- A **bandit** asks "how do I earn the most while learning?". It pays with its estimates: the arms it abandons early are those that had
  an unlucky start, and since it stops pulling them, their averages are never corrected. In the lab Thompson's estimate of each problem's
  worst arm is biased low by 0.006 (on rates around 0.05, about 10%), while the A/B test's estimates are unbiased. Adaptively collected
  data have this bias in general (Nie et al., 2018).

So: if you need a measured effect size for a decision, a report or a scientific claim, run the A/B test, or use estimators designed for
adaptive data. If you need conversions, run the bandit, and don't present its averages as measurements. Many teams do both: a test to
learn, then a bandit to earn, or a bandit with a floor on each arm's traffic.

## Contextual bandits

When the best arm depends on who's asking (a user's history, the time, the device), the problem becomes a **contextual bandit**: observe
a context $x$, choose an arm, get a reward. LinUCB and linear Thompson sampling model the reward as linear in features of $x$ and the arm;
neural versions use a network. This is the workhorse of recommendation and ad systems. It's also still a bandit: your action doesn't
change the next context. When it does (a user's future behavior depends on what you showed them), you've arrived at the full
reinforcement learning problem, which is the rest of this module.

## Things that will bite you

- **Greedy "show the best so far"** logic in production.
- **Trusting asymptotic regret guarantees** at your actual horizon, or UCB1's constants for low rates.
- **Sample averages** in a world that drifts.
- **Reporting a bandit's averages** as unbiased effect estimates.
- **Delayed rewards** (a conversion days after the impression) handled as if immediate: the bandit sees late arms as bad.
- **Novelty effects**: a new arm looks good because it's new.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. With probability $\varepsilon$ it pulls a uniformly random arm, whose expected regret is $\bar\Delta = \frac{1}{K}\sum_a (\mu^* - \mu_a) > 0$.
   Even after it has identified the best arm perfectly, each pull contributes at least $\varepsilon \bar\Delta$ expected regret, so
   $\text{Regret}(T) \ge \varepsilon \bar\Delta T$: linear. Decaying $\varepsilon$ over time fixes this if the schedule is right.

2. Posterior after $s$ successes and $f$ failures: Beta$(1 + s, 1 + f)$; each round, sample $\theta_a \sim$ Beta for each arm, pull
   $\arg\max_a \theta_a$, and add 1 to the arm's $s$ or $f$. A prior centered near 5% with modest confidence, such as Beta(1, 19)
   (mean 0.05, worth 20 observations), speeds up early decisions; too strong a prior slows learning when an arm really is different.

3. The bonus $\sqrt{2\ln t/N}$ is derived from Hoeffding's inequality, which holds for any reward in $[0, 1]$ and so assumes the worst-case
   variance, 1/4. A Bernoulli with $p = 0.05$ has variance 0.0475, five times smaller, so the confidence interval is several times wider
   than needed and arms stay "possibly best" for much longer than they should.

4. That the bandit's estimates are biased (arms with unlucky starts are underestimated and stop being sampled), so the lift will be
   overstated, and naive confidence intervals are invalid under adaptive allocation. For a reportable lift, run an A/B test, or use
   estimators designed for adaptive data (inverse propensity weighting with the logged allocation probabilities).

5. The bandit sees fewer conversions for recently pulled arms and undervalues whichever arm it favored most recently, oscillating.
   Options: update only on impressions old enough for their conversion window to have closed; model the delay distribution and
   correct; use a faster proxy reward (a click) that correlates with the conversion, validated; or batch updates daily.

6. When actions change future states: a recommendation changes what the user will want or do next, a price changes future demand, a
   treatment changes the patient's next state. Then maximizing immediate reward can hurt long-term reward, and you need to plan over
   sequences: an MDP (46.2).
