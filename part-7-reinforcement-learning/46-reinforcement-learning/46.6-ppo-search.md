# 46.6 PPO, search, and RL in the wild

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 46.5. Lab: [`lab_46_6_ppo_search.py`](labs/lab_46_6_ppo_search.py).*

Two ideas close this module. **PPO** is the policy-gradient method that became the default, in robotics, in games and in LLM
post-training, because it's stable enough to use without a research team babysitting it. **Search** is the other way to get good decisions:
think ahead at decision time instead of (or as well as) learning a reflex. AlphaGo combined the two, and reasoning models do something
similar with tokens. The lesson ends with what applying RL to real problems looks like, which is mostly about why people don't.

## The problem PPO solves

A batch of experience is expensive, and plain policy gradients (46.5) use it for one gradient step and throw it away. Taking many steps on
the same batch is the obvious fix and the obvious mistake: the gradient is only valid near the policy that collected the data. Move far
from it and the advantages describe a policy you no longer have, and a large bad step can collapse the policy, after which it collects
bad data, and learning never recovers.

**Importance sampling** lets you evaluate the new policy on the old policy's data: weight each action by the ratio
$r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) / \pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)$ and maximize $\mathbb{E}[r_t(\theta)\hat A_t]$. That objective
is accurate only while $r_t$ stays near 1. **TRPO** (Schulman et al., 2015) enforced a hard KL constraint with second-order optimization.
**PPO** (Schulman et al., 2017) gets most of the benefit with a first-order trick, the **clipped objective**:

$$L^{\text{clip}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big], \quad \epsilon \approx 0.2.$$

For a good action ($\hat A > 0$), the objective stops increasing once its probability has grown 20%; for a bad one, once it's fallen 20%.
The `min` makes it a pessimistic bound: the clipping never lets you claim improvement you haven't earned, and it doesn't stop you undoing a
move that made things worse. There's no gradient toward going further, so several epochs of minibatch SGD on the same batch are safe-ish.
Add GAE advantages, a value loss, an entropy bonus and gradient clipping, and that's PPO.

## What the lab measures

CartPole, 100,000 steps, batches of 2,000, three seeds, the same actor-critic with GAE in all three:

| method | after 20k | after 50k | after 100k | median KL between old and new policy per update |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| one update per batch (lr 10× larger) | 73 | 242 | 272 | 0.0007 |
| 10 epochs, no clipping | 205 | 384 | 310 | 0.167 |
| PPO (10 epochs, clipped) | 193 | 234 | 370 | 0.0044 |

- One update per batch is slow even with a learning rate ten times larger: it wastes most of each batch.
- Ten epochs without the clip learn fastest, then fall back, with each update moving the policy 40 times further than PPO's does. That's
  the usual shape of an unconstrained run: impressive for a while, then unstable.
- PPO is slower early and ends highest. The KL column is the mechanism: small, controlled steps.

The gap between the last two rows at 100k steps is suggestive with three seeds, not proven. The KL gap is not subtle. In practice, you
monitor the approximate KL per update in any PPO run; a sudden jump is the early warning of a collapse, and many implementations stop the
epoch loop when it passes a threshold.

## PPO in LLM post-training

RLHF (38.3) is PPO with: the LLM as the policy, a reward model's score at the end of each response, a value head (the critic), and a
**KL penalty to the reference model** (the SFT model) added to the reward, which keeps the policy from drifting into text that fools the
reward model (the over-optimization of 38.3). That's four large models in memory (policy, reference, reward, critic), which is why DPO
(which needs no sampling or reward model) and GRPO (which needs no critic, 46.5) became popular. The ideas remain: advantages, a baseline,
a limit on how far each update moves the policy.

## Search: thinking at decision time

A policy is a reflex: one forward pass per decision. When you have a model of the environment (the rules of a game, a simulator, a
verifier for a proof), you can instead **simulate** futures before choosing.

**Monte Carlo tree search** (Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006, as UCT) builds a search tree one simulation at a time, in four steps:

1. **Select**: from the root, walk down the tree choosing children by an upper confidence bound (46.1's UCB, now at every node):
   average result plus an exploration bonus that shrinks with visits.
2. **Expand**: at a node not yet in the tree, add it.
3. **Simulate**: play the game to the end from there with random moves (a *rollout*).
4. **Back up**: update the visit counts and results of every node on the path, from each mover's point of view.

After the simulations, play the most-visited move. The lab runs MCTS on tic-tac-toe, whose perfect-play value (by minimax) is a draw:

| simulations per move | vs a random player: win / draw / loss | vs perfect play: draw rate |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.90 / 0.02 / 0.08 | 0.03 |
| 100 | 0.98 / 0.02 / 0.00 | 0.57 |
| 1,000 | 0.98 / 0.02 / 0.00 | 0.93 |

No learning, no knowledge of the game beyond its rules, and at 1,000 simulations it draws 93% of games against perfect play (a draw is the
best possible result). The remaining losses are lines where random rollouts misjudge a position a careful opponent punishes.

**AlphaGo and AlphaZero** (Silver et al., 2016, 2017) replaced the random rollouts with a **value network** and biased the selection with
a **policy network**, both trained on the search's own games: the search produces better moves than the network, the network learns to
imitate them, and the better network makes the search stronger. Search and learning improving each other is the idea to remember.
**MuZero** learned the model too, so it could search in environments without known rules.

For LLMs, the analogues are **best-of-n** sampling with a verifier (38.3, 38.4), **tree search over reasoning steps** with a learned
value or process reward model, and **test-time compute**: spending more inference on hard problems. The same trade-off applies: search is
only as good as the evaluation at its leaves.

## RL in the wild

What works in production, and what to watch for:

- **Bandits** (46.1) are by far the most common RL in industry: recommendations, ad selection, pricing experiments. Contextual bandits
  cover most "RL" problems that have immediate feedback.
- **Offline RL**: learning a policy from logged decisions, without exploring. It needs the off-policy evaluation of 47.2 and conservative
  methods (CQL, IQL), because the policy will exploit any action the data never tried.
- **Simulators** make RL practical (robotics, chip placement, logistics), with the **sim-to-real gap** as the recurring problem: the
  policy exploits the simulator's inaccuracies. Domain randomization helps.
- **Reward hacking** is the default outcome, not the exception. An RL agent optimizes the reward you wrote, not the one you meant: the boat
  racing agent circling to collect points, the LLM that learns to sound confident for a reward model. Every reward is a proxy (38.3).
- **Safety during learning**: exploration in the real world has real costs. Constrained RL, human oversight and conservative policies.
- **Is RL even needed?** If you have labels for the right action, supervised learning is simpler, cheaper and more stable. RL is for when
  the only feedback is a delayed consequence of a sequence of decisions.

> **Scar tissue.** For a robotics course I built a small simulation where an RL agent was rewarded for each box that crossed a scanner
> line, and it learned to push the same box back and forth across the line. Each crossing counted as a scan. The reward curve was
> spectacular; boxes delivered, zero. The simulator did exactly what the reward said. Every reward function I've written since has had at
> least one exploit like that, and the agent always finds it before I do.

## Things that will bite you

- **No KL monitoring** in PPO: the collapse arrives without warning.
- **Too many epochs or too large a learning rate**, which clipping only partly protects against.
- **Search with a bad evaluator**: more simulations make it more confident, not more right.
- **Reward functions nobody has tried to hack**: try it yourself before the optimizer does.
- **RL where a bandit, a supervised model or a rule would do.**

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. With $\hat A > 0$, the objective is $\min(r\hat A, \text{clip}(r)\hat A)$: it grows with $r$ until $r = 1+\epsilon$, then is flat, so
   there's no incentive to raise the probability further. With $\hat A < 0$, it's flat below $r = 1-\epsilon$ (no incentive to lower the
   probability further), but if $r$ went *up* (the bad action became more likely), the unclipped term is the smaller one and the full
   penalty applies. The bound is pessimistic in both directions.

2. After the first update, the batch was generated by a different policy than the one being optimized. The policy gradient is an
   expectation under the current policy; the ratio $\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}$ reweights the old samples so the expectation is (nearly)
   right, as long as the policies stay close.

3. The approximate KL between old and new policy per update, the fraction of ratios clipped (for PPO), the policy's entropy, the value
   loss and the explained variance of the critic, alongside returns. A KL spike and an entropy crash usually come before the return drops.

4. The average value of a rarely visited move is a noisy estimate from a few rollouts; visit counts integrate the whole search, since UCB
   sends simulations to moves that keep looking good. The most-visited move is the more robust choice.

5. Self-play games are played with search; the policy network is trained to predict the search's visit distribution, and the value network
   the game's outcome. Better networks make better searches, which make better training targets. For reasoning LLMs: sample many solutions
   (search), keep those a verifier accepts, train on them (rejection sampling fine-tuning, or GRPO's rewards), repeat.

6. What's the reward (revenue, margin, long-term retention?), and what can be hacked (discount wars, gouging loyal customers); can you
