# 46.5 Policy gradients and actor-critic

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 46.3, 46.4, 03.1 (gradients), 04.3 (expectations). Lab: [`lab_46_5_policy_gradients.py`](labs/lab_46_5_policy_gradients.py).*

Everything so far learned values and derived a policy from them by taking the best action. Policy gradient methods skip the middleman:
parameterize the policy directly, $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$, and do gradient ascent on the expected return. They handle continuous actions
and stochastic policies naturally, they're the family PPO belongs to, and PPO is what trained the chat models of 38.3. They're also noisy
enough that most of this lesson is about variance, and how to beat it down.

## The policy gradient theorem

We want to maximize $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[G(\tau)]$, the expected return of trajectories $\tau$ generated by the
policy. The difficulty: the distribution we're taking the expectation over depends on $\theta$. The trick (the **score function** or
**log-derivative** trick, 04.3 and 03.1) is $\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau) = p_\theta(\tau) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)$. The
environment's transition probabilities don't depend on $\theta$, so they drop out of $\nabla \log p_\theta(\tau)$, and what's left is:

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\; G_t\Big],$$

with $G_t$ the return from step $t$ onward (actions can't affect rewards that came before them). You don't need a model of the world, and
you don't need to differentiate through it: sample trajectories, and push up the log-probability of each action in proportion to the
return that followed. That's **REINFORCE** (Williams, 1992). In code it's one line, the loss `-(log_prob * G).mean()`, and every
policy-gradient method you'll meet is that line with a better number in place of $G$.

## Baselines: same gradient, less noise

$G_t$ is a sum of many random rewards, so the estimate is very noisy. Worse, if all returns are positive (CartPole: +1 per step), every
action in every episode gets pushed up, the good ones slightly more. Subtract a **baseline** $b(s_t)$ that doesn't depend on the action:

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,\big(G_t - b(s_t)\big)\Big].$$

The expectation doesn't change, because $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[\nabla \log \pi(a \mid s)] = \nabla \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) = \nabla 1 = 0$.
The variance does. The lab measures it at a random initial policy, over 40 batches of 500 steps:

| gradient estimate | variance (sum over parameters) |
|---|---|
| REINFORCE, weights = return | 0.75 |
| REINFORCE, weights = return − mean return | 0.49 |

A constant baseline gets a factor of 1.5 here, and the factor is modest because a random policy's episodes are short and similar. The best
baseline is the state's value, $b(s) = V(s)$, learned by regression on the returns. Then $G_t - V(s_t)$ estimates the **advantage**
$A(s_t, a_t) = Q(s_t, a_t) - V(s_t)$: how much better this action was than what the policy usually does from here. Actions better than
usual go up, worse ones go down, whatever the absolute scale of the returns.

## Actor-critic and GAE

The value network is the **critic**, the policy the **actor**. Once you have a critic, you can bootstrap as TD does (46.3): the one-step
advantage estimate is the TD error $\delta_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$, low variance and biased by the critic's errors.
**Generalized advantage estimation** (Schulman et al., 2016) interpolates between the two ends with an exponentially weighted sum:

$$\hat A_t = \sum_{l \ge 0} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l},$$

$\lambda = 1$ giving the Monte Carlo advantage (unbiased, noisy), $\lambda = 0$ the one-step TD error (smooth, biased). It's 46.3's
TD($\lambda$) dial, applied to advantages. Typical values: $\gamma = 0.99$, $\lambda = 0.95$.

## What the lab measures

CartPole, 100,000 environment steps, batches of 2,000, three seeds, mean return of the last 20 episodes (maximum 500):

| method | after 20k | after 50k | after 100k | per seed at the end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| REINFORCE | 67 | 245 | 282 | 294, 163, 390 |
| REINFORCE + learned baseline | 74 | 225 | 485 | 484, 477, 494 |
| actor-critic (GAE, $\lambda = 0.95$) | 78 | 144 | 309 | 414, 350, 165 |

The baseline does what the theory says: every seed solves the task. Actor-critic with GAE, which the textbooks rank above it, keeps up for
20,000 steps and then falls behind, unevenly. I left it that way because it's the honest result, and it teaches the trade-off better than a
tuned run would: GAE's advantages lean on the critic, and a critic fitted for 20 gradient steps per batch is wrong in systematic ways. A
wrong critic biases *every* update in the same direction; Monte Carlo returns are only noisy, and noise averages out. At this budget, the
bias cost more than the variance saved. Actor-critic pays off when the critic is trained well (more steps, normalized targets) and when
episodes are long enough that Monte Carlo variance becomes unbearable, which is the situation PPO (46.6) was designed for.

And look at the per-seed column. Plain REINFORCE ends at 163 on one seed and 390 on another. With three seeds, a ranking between the first
and third rows would be a coin flip dressed as a result.

## Practical details that matter

- **Normalize the advantages** in each batch (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation). The lab does it for all methods except
  plain REINFORCE, which it only rescales, so that the baseline's effect stays visible.
- **Entropy bonus**: add a small multiple of the policy's entropy to the objective, so it doesn't collapse to deterministic too early.
- **Separate learning rates** for actor and critic, and more critic steps than actor steps.
- **On-policy only**: the gradient is an expectation under the *current* policy. Old data can't be reused directly (46.6 shows how PPO
  reuses it a little, safely), which makes policy gradients less sample-efficient than DQN-style methods.
- **Continuous actions**: output the mean and standard deviation of a Gaussian, sample, and use its log-density in the same formula.

## The LLM connection

Language model post-training is a policy gradient problem. The policy is the model, an action is a token (or a whole response), the reward
comes from a reward model or a verifier at the end, and the "environment" is trivial: appending the token. RLHF used PPO with a critic
the size of the model (38.3). **GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024) drops the critic and uses as baseline the mean reward of a group of responses
sampled for the same prompt: exactly this lesson's constant baseline, per prompt, normalized by the group's standard deviation. It works
because the group gives a decent estimate of $V(\text{prompt})$ without training a second large network, and it's the method behind the
reasoning models of 38.4.

## Things that will bite you

- **Treating the loss value as progress.** The policy-gradient "loss" isn't a quantity that should go down; watch returns, entropy and the
  size of each update.
- **A baseline that depends on the action**: then it's no longer unbiased.
- **Reusing old batches** for plain policy gradients: the gradient is for a policy you no longer have.
- **Entropy collapse**: a policy that's certain too early stops exploring.
- **Three seeds and a ranking.**

2. Show that subtracting a state-dependent baseline leaves the expected gradient unchanged.
3. Why does using $G_t$ (return from $t$ onward) instead of the whole episode's return reduce variance without adding bias?
4. In the lab, GAE lost to the Monte Carlo baseline. Name two changes you'd expect to reverse that, and how you'd test them.
5. What does GRPO use as a baseline, and what does it save compared with PPO for LLMs?
6. Why are policy-gradient methods less sample-efficient than DQN, and when is that acceptable?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $p_\theta(\tau) = p(s_0)\prod_t \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$. The log turns the product into a sum; the initial
   state and transition terms don't depend on $\theta$, so their gradient is zero. That's why no model of the environment is needed.

2. $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[\nabla \log \pi(a \mid s)\, b(s)] = b(s) \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \nabla \log \pi(a \mid s) = b(s) \sum_a \nabla \pi(a \mid s) = b(s)\nabla 1 = 0$.

3. Rewards before step $t$ can't be affected by $a_t$, so their terms have zero expected contribution to $a_t$'s gradient (same argument as
   the baseline), but they add noise. Dropping them removes the noise and keeps the expectation.

4. Train the critic more (more steps per batch, or normalized return targets) so its bias shrinks; raise $\lambda$ toward 1 to rely on it
   less. Test with at least 5 seeds, comparing final returns and the curve, with the Monte Carlo baseline run at the same budget.

5. The mean (and standard deviation) of the rewards of several responses sampled for the same prompt. It saves training and serving a value
   network as large as the policy, which halves the memory and removes the critic's bias problem at the cost of more samples per prompt.

6. They're on-policy: each batch is used for one (or, with PPO, a few) updates and thrown away, while DQN replays each transition many
   times. Acceptable when experience is cheap (simulators, LLM generation) and stability or continuous actions matter more than samples.
