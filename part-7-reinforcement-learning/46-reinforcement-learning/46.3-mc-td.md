# 46.3 Monte Carlo and temporal difference: SARSA and Q-learning

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 46.2. Lab: [`lab_46_3_mc_td.py`](labs/lab_46_3_mc_td.py).*

Dynamic programming (46.2) needs the model of the world. Here the agent gets none: it acts, observes what happens, and learns. That's
the actual reinforcement learning problem, and the two families of methods in this lesson are the foundation of nearly everything that
follows. Both estimate action values $q(s, a)$ from experience and act (mostly) greedily with respect to them. They differ in what they
use as the target of each update.

## Monte Carlo: wait for the return

**Monte Carlo** methods play an episode to the end, then use the actual discounted return $G_t$ that followed each state-action pair as a
sample of its value, and average: $Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \frac{1}{N(s,a)}\,(G_t - Q(s, a))$. The estimate is unbiased (the return
is a genuine sample of $q_\pi$), but its variance is high: a return is the sum of many random rewards and transitions. It needs episodes
that end, and it learns nothing until they do.

To improve the policy while evaluating it, act $\varepsilon$-greedily with respect to the current $Q$ (46.1's exploration problem, now in
every state): that's **Monte Carlo control**.

## Temporal difference: bootstrap

**Temporal-difference (TD)** learning updates after every step, using the reward it just got plus its *own current estimate* of what
comes next:

$$Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha\,\big[R_{t+1} + \gamma\, \text{(estimate of the value of } S_{t+1}) - Q(S_t, A_t)\big].$$

The bracket is the **TD error**: the difference between what the agent predicted and a slightly better-informed prediction one step later.
Using an estimate inside the target is called **bootstrapping**. It introduces bias (the estimate may be wrong), and it removes most of the
variance (one random reward and transition instead of a whole episode's). It also works without episodes ending, and learns from every
step.

What goes in "estimate of the value of $S_{t+1}$" defines the two classic algorithms:

- **SARSA** uses the action the agent will actually take next: $Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1})$. It learns the value of the policy it follows,
  exploration included: it's **on-policy**.
- **Q-learning** (Watkins, 1989) uses the best action: $\max_a Q(S_{t+1}, a)$. It learns the value of the greedy policy while following
  another one: it's **off-policy**. That's a sampled version of the Bellman optimality backup, and under mild conditions it converges to
  $q_*$.

## From experience alone

The lab runs Q-learning and Monte Carlo control on the slippery lake of 46.2, without access to its model, and compares the result with the
true optimal values computed there:

| episodes | method | max error in $Q$ vs $q_*$ | success of its greedy policy (optimal: 0.73) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,000 | Q-learning | 0.071 | 0.729 |
| 2,000 | Monte Carlo | 0.143 | 0.552 |
| 20,000 | Q-learning | 0.051 | 0.729 |
| 20,000 | Monte Carlo | 0.112 | 0.706 |

Both approach the optimum. Q-learning gets there much faster here, because every step updates it and its targets have low variance.

One detail in the lab matters more than it looks: the step size. With a constant $\alpha = 0.1$, Q-learning's estimates keep jittering
around $q_*$ (error stuck near 0.10). With per-pair step sizes that shrink like $1/N^{0.6}$ (the Robbins-Monro conditions: their sum
diverges and the sum of their squares converges), they settle. Constant step sizes are what you want in a changing world (46.1). Decaying
ones are what the convergence proofs assume.

## The cliff: on-policy vs off-policy

Sutton and Barto's cliff walk: a 4×12 grid, start bottom-left, goal bottom-right, the cells between them a cliff (−100 and back to start),
−1 per step. Both algorithms act $\varepsilon$-greedily with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ (20 runs of 500 episodes):

| method | return per episode while learning | greedy path | steps next to the cliff |
|---|---|---|---|
| SARSA | −28.4 | 17 steps | 2 |
| Q-learning | −50.1 | 13 steps (optimal) | 12 |

Q-learning learns the optimal path, straight along the edge. But it keeps exploring, and one random step on that path is a fall, so it
earns *less* while learning. SARSA learns values that include its own exploration, so it discovers the edge is dangerous *for an agent that
sometimes stumbles*, and takes the safer route. Which one is right depends on whether you care about the policy you'll deploy greedily
later (Q-learning) or about performance while learning (SARSA). A robot that's learning in the real world cares about the second.

Off-policy learning has a large practical advantage: it can learn from data generated by any behavior (older versions of itself, human
demonstrations, logged decisions of a production system). Experience replay, and therefore DQN (46.4), depends on it.

## Maximization bias

Q-learning's target takes a max over estimates, and the max of noisy estimates is biased upward: if ten actions are all worth −0.1 and your
estimates are noisy, the largest estimate is probably positive. The lab reproduces Sutton and Barto's example. From state A, "right" ends the
episode with 0, and "left" leads to B, where ten actions each give a reward drawn from N(−0.1, 1). Left is worse on average:

| method | share of "left", episodes 1-50 | episodes 250-300 | (exploration alone: 0.05) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q-learning | 0.86 | 0.13 | |
| double Q-learning | 0.28 | 0.06 | |

**Double Q-learning** (van Hasselt, 2010) keeps two estimates, chooses the best action with one and values it with the other. The noise in
the choice and the noise in the value are then independent, and the bias disappears. DQN overestimates for the same reason and is fixed
the same way (Double DQN, 46.4).

## n-step methods and eligibility traces

Monte Carlo and one-step TD are two ends of a spectrum. **n-step** methods use $n$ real rewards and then bootstrap:
$R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \dots + \gamma^{n-1}R_{t+n} + \gamma^n V(S_{t+n})$. **TD($\lambda$)** averages all n-step targets with
weights $(1-\lambda)\lambda^{n-1}$, and eligibility traces implement that efficiently. Intermediate $n$ or $\lambda$ usually beats both
extremes. The same trade-off returns in policy gradients as GAE (46.5), which is what PPO uses.

## Things that will bite you

- **Too little exploration**: a state-action pair never tried keeps its initial value forever.
- **Constant step sizes** when you need convergence, or decaying ones in a world that drifts.
- **Judging an off-policy learner** by its returns while exploring, or an on-policy one by its greedy policy.
- **Maximization bias** in any method that maxes over noisy estimates.
- **Truncation treated as termination**: an episode cut off by a time limit didn't end, so don't set the next value to zero (the lab's
  environments distinguish `terminated` from `truncated` for this reason).

## Exercises

1. Write the SARSA and Q-learning updates, and point to the one symbol that makes one on-policy and the other off-policy.
2. Why does Monte Carlo have higher variance and lower bias than TD?
3. In the cliff walk, what happens to SARSA's path as $\varepsilon \to 0$ over training? And to the gap in online returns?
4. Explain maximization bias with a numerical example: three actions, true values all 0, estimates with noise of standard deviation 1.
5. Why must experience replay use an off-policy method?
6. A game can go on forever and gives a reward only at the end. Which of Monte Carlo and TD can you use, and why?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. SARSA: $Q(S,A) \mathrel{+}= \alpha[R + \gamma Q(S', A') - Q(S,A)]$ with $A'$ the action actually taken next. Q-learning:
   $Q(S,A) \mathrel{+}= \alpha[R + \gamma \max_a Q(S', a) - Q(S,A)]$. The difference is $A'$ (drawn from the behavior policy) versus
   $\max_a$ (the greedy target policy).

2. The Monte Carlo target is the actual return, a sum of many random rewards and transitions: unbiased for $q_\pi$ but noisy. The TD target
   uses one random reward and transition plus the current estimate, which is much less noisy but biased while the estimate is wrong.

3. As $\varepsilon \to 0$, falling off the cliff becomes rarer, the values SARSA learns approach those of the greedy policy, and its path
   moves toward the edge: it converges to the same optimal path as Q-learning, and the gap in online returns closes.

4. The largest of three independent N(0, 1) estimates has expected value about 0.85. So $\max_a \hat Q(s, a) \approx 0.85$ while the true
   max is 0: every Q-learning target using that state is biased up by about 0.85, and the bias propagates backward to earlier states.

5. The transitions in the replay buffer were generated by older policies. An on-policy method's target must come from the current
   policy's actions, which these aren't. Q-learning's target ($\max_a Q(S', a)$) doesn't depend on which action the behavior policy took
   next, so any transition $(S, A, R, S')$ is valid training data.

6. TD. Monte Carlo needs episodes to end to compute returns; with episodes that may never end it can't update. TD bootstraps from its
   estimates after every step, so it learns during the episode (with $\gamma < 1$ keeping values finite).

</details>
