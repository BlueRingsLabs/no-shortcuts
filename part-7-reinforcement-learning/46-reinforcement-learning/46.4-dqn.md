# 46.4 Deep Q-networks

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 46.3, 26.1 (optimizers), 26.5 (training recipe). Lab: [`lab_46_4_dqn.py`](labs/lab_46_4_dqn.py).*

Tables work while states fit in a table. The moment the state is four real numbers (a cart's position and velocity, a pole's angle and
angular velocity), let alone the pixels of a screen, there's no table, and $Q(s, a)$ has to be a function that generalizes: a neural
network. That sounds like a small change. It's the change that made Q-learning go from "converges, provably" to "diverges, regularly",
and the two tricks that brought it back are the whole content of the 2015 Atari paper that restarted the field. This lesson is about those
tricks, why each one is needed, and the part the papers mention less: how unstable the result still is.

## Q-learning with a function approximator

Replace the table with a network $Q_\theta(s, \cdot)$ that outputs one value per action, and turn 46.3's update into a regression: for a
transition $(s, a, r, s')$, the target is

$$y = r + \gamma\,(1 - \text{done})\,\max_{a'} Q_\theta(s', a'),$$

and you take a gradient step on $(Q_\theta(s, a) - y)^2$ (in practice the Huber loss, which doesn't let one huge TD error blow up the
gradient). Written that way it looks like supervised learning. It isn't, in two ways that break it:

1. **The data are correlated and non-stationary.** Consecutive transitions come from the same episode and look alike, and as the policy
   changes, the distribution of states changes with it. SGD assumes roughly independent samples from a fixed distribution (26.1).
2. **The target moves with the parameters.** $y$ depends on $\theta$. Every step that raises $Q_\theta(s, a)$ also tends to raise
   $Q_\theta(s', \cdot)$ for similar states, which raises the target, which raises the estimate. The regression chases its own tail.

Sutton and Barto call the combination of function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy learning the **deadly triad**: any two are
fine, all three can diverge. Q-learning with a network has all three.

## The two fixes

**Experience replay** (Lin, 1992): store transitions in a buffer (tens of thousands to millions) and train on random minibatches drawn
from it. Random sampling breaks the correlation between consecutive samples; each transition gets used many times, which matters when
experience is expensive; and the minibatch mixes data from many past policies, which smooths the non-stationarity. Replay only works with
an off-policy method, because those transitions came from older policies (46.3, exercise 5).

**A target network**: compute $y$ with a copy of the network, $Q_{\theta^-}$, whose weights are refreshed from the online network only
every few thousand steps (or blended in slowly: $\theta^- \leftarrow \tau\theta + (1-\tau)\theta^-$). Between refreshes, the target is a
fixed function and the problem really is a regression. The price is slower propagation of new information.

Together with an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy that starts at 1 and decays, gradient clipping and the Huber loss, that's **DQN** (Mnih et
al., 2015).

## What the lab measures

CartPole: +1 per step the pole stays up, at most 500. Thirty thousand environment steps per run, two seeds per variant, greedy return
evaluated every 5,000 steps:

| variant | best evaluation (mean of 2 seeds) | final (20 episodes) |
|---|---|---|
| DQN (replay + target network) | 318 | 161 |
| Double DQN | 217 | 112 |
| no target network | 9 | 9 |
| no replay (each transition once, in order) | 165 | 89 |

Read it slowly, because every row teaches something:

- **No target network** is a catastrophe: 9 per episode, worse than pushing randomly (about 22). The values chase their own updates and
  the greedy policy collapses to always pushing one way.
- **No replay** learns in spikes: a seed reaches 170, then falls back to 12. Learning from a stream of correlated samples means the network
  keeps fitting whatever it's seeing right now, and forgetting the rest.
- **Full DQN learns, and then gets worse.** One seed reached 446 and ended at 133. Once the agent balances well, the buffer fills with
  "pole upright" states, the network forgets what to do when the pole falls, it starts failing, the buffer fills with failures, and around
  it goes. This is **catastrophic forgetting** inside a single training run, and it's normal.
- **Double DQN didn't help here.** It fixes an overestimation bias (next section) that barely matters on CartPole, where every reward is +1;
  two seeds can't separate the rest from noise. On Atari it helps clearly. Techniques don't transfer between problems just because a paper
  says they work.

The practical consequences: **evaluate periodically and keep the best checkpoint**, not the last one; decay the learning rate; use bigger
buffers or prioritized ones; and **always run several seeds**. The per-seed curves in the lab's output differ more than the averages of
some variants. A paper table with one seed per method is an anecdote.

## Refinements

The "Rainbow" paper (Hessel et al., 2018) combined six improvements; the ones worth knowing:

- **Double DQN** (van Hasselt et al., 2016): choose $a' = \arg\max_{a'} Q_\theta(s', a')$ with the online network, evaluate it with the
  target network. It's 46.3's double Q-learning for the maximization bias, at no extra cost.
- **Prioritized replay** (Schaul et al., 2016): sample transitions with large TD errors more often, with importance weights to correct the
  bias that introduces.
- **Dueling networks**: separate heads for $V(s)$ and the advantages $A(s, a)$; helps when many actions have similar values.
- **n-step returns**: $r_{t+1} + \dots + \gamma^{n-1} r_{t+n} + \gamma^n \max Q(s_{t+n}, \cdot)$, 46.3's middle of the spectrum.
- **Distributional RL** (C51, QR-DQN): learn the distribution of returns, not only its mean.

DQN-style methods need discrete actions (the max over actions). For continuous control (robot joints), the off-policy family continues as
DDPG, TD3 and SAC, which learn a policy network alongside the Q-network, and meet the policy gradients of 46.5.

## Things that will bite you

- **One seed.** In RL, the variance between runs is often larger than the difference between methods (Henderson et al., 2018).
- **Reporting the final checkpoint** of a run that peaked earlier, or the best checkpoint of a run chosen by its test score (that's
  selection on the test set, 18.1).
- **Truncation treated as termination**: the lab stores `done` only for real endings, so the time limit at 500 doesn't teach the agent that
  step 500 is a disaster.
- **Learning starts too early**: training on a buffer of a few hundred transitions from a random policy overfits to them. The lab waits for
  1,000.
- **Unscaled rewards and inputs**: Atari DQN clipped rewards to $[-1, 1]$; large rewards mean large TD errors and unstable updates.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: learning rate, buffer size, target refresh period and exploration schedule interact. Change one at a
  time, with several seeds.

## Exercises

1. Why can't you train on the most recent transition only, as online supervised learning would?
2. The target network makes the regression target fixed between refreshes. What goes wrong if the refresh period is very long? Very short?
3. Why does DQN need an off-policy algorithm? Could you use SARSA with a replay buffer?
4. In the lab, full DQN reached 446 on one seed and ended at 133. Propose two changes to reduce that drop, and how you'd verify them.
5. Explain Double DQN's target in one sentence, and why it reduces overestimation.
6. A colleague reports "our DQN variant beats DQN by 15%" from one run each. What do you ask for?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Consecutive transitions are strongly correlated, so each gradient step pushes the network toward whatever the agent is seeing now, and
   it forgets the rest (the lab's no-replay row: spikes and collapses). Each transition is also used once, which wastes experience.

2. Very long: targets stay stale, so value information propagates slowly and learning crawls. Very short (every step): you're back to the
   moving target, and the instability returns (the lab's no-target row is the extreme).

3. Replayed transitions come from older policies; an on-policy target needs the next action from the current policy, which the stored
   transition doesn't have. Q-learning's max doesn't care which action was taken next. SARSA from replay would learn a muddle of old
   policies' values (you can re-sample the next action from the current policy, which is what Expected SARSA-style targets do, and that
   makes it off-policy).

4. Keep the best checkpoint by periodic evaluation; decay the learning rate after the agent first reaches a threshold; enlarge the buffer or
   keep a reserve of early, diverse transitions; soft target updates. Verify with several seeds (5 or more), comparing the final evaluation
   and the drop from the peak, with intervals.

5. Pick the next action with the online network and evaluate it with the target network. The max of noisy estimates is biased up because
   the same noise chooses and evaluates; with two networks, the noise in the choice is independent of the noise in the value.

6. Several seeds per method (at least 5, ideally 10 or more), the same budget and tuning effort for both, intervals on the difference,
   learning curves rather than one number, and whether "15%" is on the final policy or the best checkpoint.
