# 50.2 Deployment strategies: shadow, canary, A/B, bandits

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 50.1, 05.3 (A/B testing), 46.1 (bandits), 48.1 (registries). Lab: [`lab_50_2_deployment.py`](labs/lab_50_2_deployment.py).*

Offline tests (50.1) tell you a model is probably fine. Production is where you find out, and the deployment strategy decides how many users
pay for it if "probably" was wrong. The strategies below trade speed against risk, and each answers a different question: does it crash, is
it better, for whom. Good teams use several in sequence.

The lab simulates three releases replacing a current model that serves 200,000 requests a day: one slightly better ("fine"), one that errors
on 3% of requests because a feature it expects is sometimes missing in production ("crashing"), and one that's better on average and much
worse for a segment that's 10% of traffic ("segment").

## Shadow mode

The new model receives a copy of real production traffic and its predictions are logged, never served. Users see only the current model.

| release | error rate | agreement with the current model | agreement within the segment |
|---|---|---|---|
| fine | 0.05% | 97.0% | 97.6% |
| crashing | 3.05% | 96.9% | 96.8% |
| segment | 0.04% | 93.3% | 59.8% |

Shadowing catches crashes, latency problems and training/serving skew with zero user risk, on real inputs. It can't measure outcomes,
because nobody acted on the new predictions. But disagreement is informative: the segment model disagrees with the current one on 40% of
the segment's requests, a clear hint of where to look. The costs are compute (you're running two models) and care with side effects: a
shadowed model must never send emails or write to production state.

## Canary releases

Route a small share of real traffic to the new version, watch it, and grow the share in stages if it's healthy: 1%, 5%, 25%, 100%. Each
stage has gates, checked automatically:

- **Guardrails**: error rate, latency percentiles, resource usage, compared with the current version over the same period.
- **Quality**: the business metric, with a statistical test (not just "looks similar"), where traffic allows.
- **Automatic rollback** when a gate fails: the registry alias moves back (48.1), in seconds, with no human in the loop.

| release | canary outcome | users who hit an error on the way |
|---|---|---|
| fine | promoted | 37 |
| crashing | rolled back at 1% (error rate 2.70%) | 54 |
| segment | promoted | 37 |

The crashing version reaches 1% of traffic, fails its error gate within a day and rolls back: a few dozen affected users instead of
thousands. That's the point of a canary: a limited blast radius. The segment regression sails through every gate, because every gate looks
at aggregates, and the aggregate improves.

## A/B tests

A canary asks "is it safe?"; an **A/B test** asks "is it better, and by how much?" with the statistical care of 05.3: a pre-registered
metric, a sample size computed in advance, no peeking without sequential methods, guardrail metrics alongside. To detect a lift from 5.0% to
5.2% with 80% power takes 186,200 requests per arm, about 1.9 days at full traffic split in two. Smaller effects take much longer, which is
why teams run several experiments in parallel on disjoint traffic and use variance reduction (CUPED).

The segment model in a proper A/B test:

| | current | new |
|---|---|---|
| overall success rate | 0.0505 | 0.0509 (+0.8%) |
| in the segment | 0.0509 | 0.0305 (−40%) |
| everywhere else | 0.0504 | 0.0531 |

The average improved. One user group in ten lost 40% of its conversions. That might be a new market, a device type, or a protected group
(54.1), and the aggregate result says "ship it". So pre-register the segments that matter and look at each in every test, with the
multiple-comparison caution of 05.2. And design the metric: a release that trades one group's outcomes for another's is a product decision,
to be made on purpose.

## Bandits and interleaving

- **Bandit rollouts** (46.1) shift traffic toward the better version while the test runs, losing fewer conversions than a fixed split,
  at the price of biased effect estimates (46.1) and more complex analysis. They suit many-variant, short-lived choices (headlines,
  creatives) better than model releases, where you want a clean, reportable comparison.
- **Interleaving** (47.2) compares rankers within a single result list and needs far less traffic than A/B tests for search and
  recommendation.

## Rollback

Rollback is the most important deployment feature, and it's tested least. Requirements:

- **One command, or none**: an alias move in the registry, a feature flag, a traffic weight. Not a rebuild.
- **The old version still running or instantly startable**: keep it warm during the canary, with its image by digest (49.2).
- **Compatible state**: if the new version changed a feature schema, a database, or a cache format, rolling back the model isn't enough.
  Make changes backward-compatible (expand, migrate, then contract).
- **Practiced**: roll back on purpose in a drill, so the first time isn't during an incident.

> **Scar tissue.** I help run the mailing list of a small security community, and once shadow-deployed a new "posts you might like" model
> for the weekly digest, the textbook safe way. A few hundred members got the digest twice. The shadow service ran the whole pipeline,
> including the step that queued emails, because the sender was called inside the scoring code rather than after it. "Shadow" means the
> candidate's outputs go nowhere, and that has to be enforced by the architecture, not by hoping every side effect was removed. The
> replies in the list were merciless, and deserved.

## Things that will bite you

- **Big-bang releases** to 100% of traffic.
- **Canary gates on aggregates only**, missing a broken segment.
- **Rollback that needs a rebuild**, or breaks because state changed.
- **A/B tests stopped when they first look significant** (05.3's peeking).
- **Shadow models with side effects.**
- **Novelty effects** read as lasting improvements: run tests long enough to see them fade.

## Exercises

1. Design the rollout for a new fraud model: which stages, which gates, which metrics can and can't be measured during a canary (think
   about delayed labels).
2. Why can't shadow mode measure the business effect of a recommendation model? What can it measure?
3. A canary at 5% shows the new version's p99 latency 30% higher and everything else equal. Promote?
4. Compute the sample size per arm to detect a 1% relative lift on a 3% conversion rate with 80% power. What would you do if that's
   more traffic than you have in a month?
5. How would you have caught the segment regression before the A/B test?
6. A model change also changed the format of cached features. The new model fails its canary. What happens when you roll back, and
   how should the change have been made?
