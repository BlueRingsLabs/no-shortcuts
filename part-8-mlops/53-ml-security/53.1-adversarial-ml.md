# 53.1 Adversarial machine learning

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 24.2 (backpropagation), 25.1 (tensors and autograd), 44.1 (security mindset), 39.1 (membership inference). Lab: [`lab_53_1_adversarial_ml.py`](labs/lab_53_1_adversarial_ml.py).*

A model is software that makes decisions, and anything that makes decisions attracts people who want different decisions. Module 44
covered attacks on LLM applications through their inputs. This lesson covers the older, more general field: attacks on the model itself,
at training time and at inference time. I approach it the way I'd approach any security review, from the defender's side: what could
go wrong, how do we measure our exposure, and which controls actually hold. The lab runs three audits on a digit classifier: robustness,
a backdoored training set, and privacy.

## The threat model first

Four questions frame every ML security review (NIST AI 100-2 is the standard taxonomy):

- **When** can the adversary act? At training time (poisoning the data or the model) or at inference time (crafting inputs, querying the
  model).
- **What do they know?** White-box (weights and code), gray-box (architecture, training data source), or black-box (only queries).
- **What do they want?** Wrong outputs in general, a specific wrong output (targeted), the training data (privacy), or the model itself
  (theft).
- **What can they touch?** Their own inputs, a data source you scrape, a vendor's dataset, a pretrained model from a hub, your API.

A fraud model faces adversaries who control their own transaction features. A content filter faces users crafting inputs. A model trained
on scraped web data faces anyone who can publish a web page. The answers decide which of the following matter.

## Evasion: robustness to worst-case inputs

Neural networks are sensitive to small, carefully chosen input changes: perturbations too small for a person to notice can change the
prediction (Szegedy et al., 2014). The standard way to *measure* this is to search for such perturbations within a budget:

- **FGSM** (Goodfellow et al., 2015): one step in the direction of the sign of the loss gradient, scaled by $\varepsilon$.
- **PGD** (Madry et al., 2018): many smaller steps, each projected back into the allowed $\varepsilon$-ball. A much stronger search, and the
  standard evaluation.

Robust accuracy under PGD, on the lab's digits (pixels in [0, 1]; $\varepsilon$ is the largest change allowed per pixel):

| $\varepsilon$ | standard model, FGSM | standard model, PGD | adversarially trained, PGD |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (clean) | 0.975 | 0.975 | 0.983 |
| 0.05 | 0.821 | 0.816 | 0.951 |
| 0.10 | 0.456 | 0.407 | 0.836 |
| 0.20 | 0.052 | 0.017 | 0.251 |

Changes of a tenth of the intensity range cut the standard model's accuracy to about 40%; a fifth takes it near zero. PGD is always at least as
strong a test as FGSM. For a real audit, use more steps, random restarts and a standard library (AutoAttack) so a weak search doesn't give a
false sense of safety.

**Adversarial training**, training on PGD examples generated on the fly, is the defense that holds up best: 84% at $\varepsilon = 0.1$. On
this small problem it didn't cost clean accuracy (it acted as regularization); on large image models it usually costs a few points of clean
accuracy and several times the training compute, and it protects only against the kind and size of perturbation it was trained on.
Robustness is a measured property relative to a threat model. Report the curve, not a single number.

When does this matter in practice? When adversaries control inputs and have an incentive: fraud, spam and abuse detection, malware
classification, content moderation, biometric systems. For most business models, the realistic "adversarial input" is a user gaming
features you exposed (47.1: don't let the model depend on features the user controls cheaply), not pixel-level noise.

## Poisoning and backdoors

If an adversary can influence training data, they can change the model. The most dangerous form is a **backdoor**: the model behaves
normally, except on inputs containing a trigger, where it does what the attacker chose. The lab's fixture: a training set "received from a
vendor" in which 9 of 1,200 images (0.75%) are sevens stamped with a small bright patch and relabeled as ones.

The result is sobering:

- the backdoored model's clean test accuracy is 0.972, indistinguishable from a clean model;
- sevens with the patch are classified as ones 94% of the time.

Three audits:

| audit | result |
|---|---|
| data: spectral signatures (Tran et al., 2018), flagging the 10% of each class furthest along the top singular direction of the representations | 0 of 9 poisoned images caught |
| labels: flag examples whose label disagrees with an out-of-fold prediction | 0 of 9 caught: models trained on the other folds learned the trigger too |
| model: stamp candidate patches on clean test images and look for patches that send many images to one class | the right patch and class: 38% of other images go to "1", against 16% for the same patch on a clean model |

Both data audits miss it. Published detection methods work in their papers' settings (more poisoned examples, larger models, particular
trigger types) and are easy to fall outside of. The model scan finds this backdoor, because it searched the right family of triggers. But a
clean model also has sensitive patches (a bright top-right corner looks like part of a 7, sending 25% of images there), so a scan produces
candidates for a person to inspect, not verdicts.

The defenses that don't depend on luck come before any statistics:

- **Provenance**: know where every training example came from, and who could change it.
- **Access control** on training data and pipelines, like production code (48.2, 52.1).
- **Review of new data sources**, and of large changes in existing ones (51.1's monitoring applies to training data too).
- **Reproducible pipelines** that record exactly which data trained which model, so a discovered poisoning can be traced and every
  affected model found (48.1).
- **Skepticism toward pretrained models** from unknown sources: a backdoor can arrive in the weights as easily as in the data (53.2).

## Model extraction

Anyone who can query a model can, with enough queries, train a copy that mimics it, stealing the investment and making white-box attacks
possible. Defenses are mostly about the API: authentication, rate limits and quotas per client (44.2), monitoring for query patterns unlike
normal use (systematic sweeps, many near-duplicate inputs), returning labels instead of full probability vectors where the product allows,
and contractual terms. None prevents extraction by a determined, patient adversary; they raise its cost and make it visible.

## Membership inference: a privacy audit

Does the model reveal which records it was trained on? That's a privacy question about the people in the data (54.2). The simplest audit:
models tend to have lower loss on their training examples, so check how well loss separates members from non-members drawn from the same
distribution (39.1 made the point about matching distributions). The lab trains on 300 records, 20% of them with wrong labels (records that
don't follow the pattern, which a model can only memorize):

| training | accuracy on clean test images | membership AUC (0.5 = no leak) |
|---|---|---|
| 300 epochs, no regularization | 0.757 | 0.727 |
| 60 epochs | 0.812 | 0.650 |
| 15 epochs with weight decay | 0.863 | 0.555 |

The more a model memorizes, the more it leaks, and what it memorizes are the unusual records, which in personal data are often the most
sensitive. Here less memorization was also better accuracy; that's not always so. Regularization and early stopping reduce the leak;
differential privacy bounds it (54.2). Run this audit before releasing a model trained on personal data, or exposing its scores.

## Things that will bite you

- **Robustness claims from weak attacks** (FGSM only, one restart), or no threat model at all.
- **Features the adversary controls cheaply** at the center of a model's decisions.
- **Training data from sources anyone can write to**, with no provenance.
- **Trusting a statistical poisoning detector** as the only control.
- **Full probability outputs** on public APIs without rate limits or monitoring.
- **Models trained on personal data** released without a membership audit.

3. The lab's backdoor needed 9 images out of 1,200. What does that imply for models trained on scraped web data?
4. You receive a pretrained model from a vendor. List what you'd check before deploying it.
5. How would you notice a model-extraction attempt against your API?
6. Why do membership-inference audits need non-members from the same distribution as members?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Adversaries: spammers, at inference time (crafting messages) and training time (reporting their own messages as "not spam", or good
   messages as spam, if user reports feed training). Knowledge: black-box, with unlimited queries via test accounts. Goals: get spam through

2. FGSM takes one step and can miss perturbations that a multi-step search finds (especially where the loss surface is curved); gradient
   masking can make FGSM fail while the model remains vulnerable. A robustness number from a weak attack is an upper bound on true
   robustness, sometimes a very loose one, and defenses evaluated that way have repeatedly been broken (Athalye et al., 2018).

3. That a small number of controlled documents can plant behavior in a model trained on a large corpus: whoever can publish content the
   crawler picks up has influence. Research has shown poisoning of web-scale datasets by buying expired domains that datasets reference.
   Controls: provenance and curation of sources, hashing snapshots so content can't change after collection, filtering, and evaluating
   for trigger-like behavior.

4. Its provenance (who trained it, on what data, signed?), its format (safetensors, not pickles, 53.2), its license, its behavior on your
   evaluation suite and on slices, behavioral tests including probes for trigger-like patterns, and performance compared with a model you
   trained or one from a trusted source. And whether you can retrain an equivalent model yourself if needed.

5. Per-client query volume and rate, input distributions unlike real traffic (grids, random inputs, many near-duplicates differing in one
   feature), unusually high coverage of the input space, and clients who request full probability outputs heavily. Alert on these and
