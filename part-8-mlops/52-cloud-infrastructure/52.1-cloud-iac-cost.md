# 52.1 Cloud, GPUs, infrastructure as code and cost

*About 15 hours. Prerequisites: 49.2 (containers), 48.2 (pipelines), 33.1 (GPU arithmetic). Lab: [`lab_52_1_cloud_costs.py`](labs/lab_52_1_cloud_costs.py).*

The cloud is somebody else's computer, with a bill, and the bill is the part people learn about too late. This lesson covers the
primitives every ML system on AWS, GCP or Azure is built from, how to define them as code so they can be reviewed and reproduced, how to
choose GPUs, and the arithmetic that keeps a training budget from becoming an incident. Provider names differ; the concepts map one to one,
and I'll give the three names where it helps.

## The primitives

- **Compute**: virtual machines (EC2, Compute Engine, Azure VMs), with GPU instance families; managed Kubernetes (EKS, GKE, AKS, 49.2);
  serverless functions and containers for light inference; batch services for jobs.
- **Object storage** (S3, GCS, Azure Blob): where datasets, checkpoints and models live. Cheap, durable, effectively unlimited, and
  accessed over the network, so throughput matters for training (33.3).
- **Identity and access management (IAM)**: who and what can do which actions on which resources. The security boundary of everything
  else.
- **Networking**: virtual private networks (VPCs), subnets, security groups, private endpoints, load balancers. Where data transfer
  costs come from.
- **Managed ML platforms** (SageMaker, Vertex AI, Azure ML): hosted notebooks, training jobs, registries, endpoints. They bundle the
  above with ML conveniences. They're useful, they lock you in, and they cost more per unit than the primitives underneath.

## IAM, from the defender's side

Most cloud security incidents in ML teams come from access configuration, not exotic attacks:

- **Least privilege**: a training job reads one bucket prefix and writes another, and that's all its role allows. No wildcard
  permissions on production accounts.
- **No long-lived keys**: use roles attached to compute (instance profiles, workload identity) and short-lived credentials. An access key
  in a notebook or a repository is the classic leak (44.2's secrets rules apply to infrastructure too).
- **No public buckets** for anything that isn't meant for the public. Block public access at the account level.
- **Separate accounts or projects** for development and production, with production data out of reach of experiments by default.
- **Audit logs** on, and alerts on unusual access.

## Infrastructure as code

Clicking resources into existence in a web console produces infrastructure nobody can review, reproduce or delete with confidence.
**Infrastructure as code** (Terraform or OpenTofu, Pulumi, CloudFormation) declares resources in files:

```hcl
resource "aws_s3_bucket" "training_data" {
  bucket = "acme-ml-training-data"
}
resource "aws_s3_bucket_public_access_block" "training_data" {
  bucket                  = aws_s3_bucket.training_data.id
  block_public_acls       = true
  block_public_policy     = true
  ignore_public_acls      = true
  restrict_public_buckets = true
}
```

`terraform plan` shows exactly what will change; the plan is reviewed in a pull request like code (50.1); `terraform apply` makes it so;
state records what exists. The benefits are the same as version control for code: review, history, reproducibility (48.2), and the ability
to create a staging environment identical to production, or tear everything down. Linters (Checkov, tfsec) catch public buckets and
wildcard IAM before they exist.

## GPUs

What matters when choosing (33.1):

- **Memory**: does the model, with optimizer state and activations, fit? That often decides everything else (37.3, 38.2).
- **Memory bandwidth** for inference, which is usually memory-bound (37.2, 45.2), and **compute** for training.
- **Interconnect** for multi-GPU training (NVLink within a node, InfiniBand between nodes, 33.2).
- **Availability**: the newest GPUs are scarce, and quotas must be requested in advance.

Decide by price per job, not price per hour. In the lab's illustrative example, a job that takes 30 hours on a small GPU at $0.80 an hour
costs $24.00; on a GPU four times more expensive per hour that's 4.5 times faster, $20.00; on one 7.5 times more expensive and 9 times
faster, $20.00 and done in 3.3 hours. The expensive GPU only wins if the job keeps it busy: large enough batches, mixed precision, and a data
pipeline that isn't the bottleneck (33.3). Measure throughput on your own job before choosing.

## The nightly job

The self-check question: what's the cheapest way to run a nightly training job that takes four GPU-hours?

**Spot (preemptible) instances** sell spare capacity at a large discount, and can be taken back at short notice. The lab simulates
2,000 nights on spot at $1.40 an hour against on-demand at $4.00 ($16.00 a night), with preemptions every 3 hours on average:

| checkpoint every | mean wall time | 95th percentile | mean cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| never (240 min) | 8.9 h | 21.2 h | $12.49 |
| 60 min | 5.2 h | 7.3 h | $7.33 |
| 30 min | 5.0 h | 6.2 h | $7.00 |
| 6 min | 6.4 h | 7.0 h | $8.98 |

Without checkpoints the job keeps starting over: cheaper on average, but one night in twenty it takes over 20 hours, and the model isn't
ready in the morning. With a checkpoint every 30 minutes it costs less than half of on-demand and reliably finishes in 5 to 6 hours.
Checkpoint too often and the checkpoints themselves cost more than they save. The optimum is close to Young and Daly's formula,
$\sqrt{2 \times \text{checkpoint cost} \times \text{mean time between failures}}$, here 33 minutes (simulated best: 30). The prerequisite is
code that can resume exactly: model, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, data loader position and random states, not just weights.

So the answer: spot instances, checkpointing to object storage at roughly the Young/Daly interval, run by a scheduler that retries on
preemption, in the region where the data lives, with a fallback to on-demand if the deadline is at risk.

## The quiet costs

- **Idle instances.** Six GPU development machines left on all month cost $17,280 in the lab's example, against $1,440 for the hours
  anyone used. Auto-stop idle machines. This is the most common bill surprise.
- **Data egress.** Moving data out of a cloud, or between regions, costs per gigabyte ($90 per terabyte in the example), every time. Keep
  compute next to data.
- **Storage that accumulates**: checkpoints of every experiment forever. Set lifecycle rules to delete or archive.
- **Managed service premiums**, and endpoints left running for demos.
- **Logging and monitoring volume**, which can rival compute costs for high-traffic services (43.2).

Tag every resource with an owner and a project, set budgets with alerts per team, and review the bill monthly by tag.

> **Scar tissue.** Before a long holiday weekend, I rented an eight-GPU cloud instance for a Kaggle competition, left it running with a
> notebook open, and meant to finish on Monday. I came back to an idle machine and a bill that would have bought me a second-hand 3090.
> Since then, every GPU machine I rent has an idle-shutdown script and a budget alert before I install anything else on it.

## Things that will bite you

- **Keys in code**, public buckets, wildcard IAM.
- **Console-created infrastructure** that nobody can reproduce or safely delete.
- **Spot without checkpoints**, or checkpoints that can't resume exactly.
- **Choosing GPUs by hourly price.**
- **Training in one region on data in another.**
- **No budgets, no tags, no auto-stop.**

## Exercises

1. Write the IAM permissions a nightly training job needs, and nothing more.
2. A checkpoint of a 7B model with optimizer state takes 4 minutes to write, and preemptions happen every 2 hours on average. What
   checkpoint interval does Young's formula suggest?
3. Your team's cloud bill doubled this month. How do you find out why, in order?
4. When is a managed ML platform worth its premium over running the primitives yourself?
5. Compare the monthly cost of serving a model on one always-on GPU instance at $2.50 an hour with a serverless GPU option at $0.0012 per
   second of use, for 50,000 requests a day of 0.4 s each. Where's the break-even?
6. Why is `terraform plan` part of code review, and what would you look for in it?

## Further reading

- Luiz André Barroso, Urs Hölzle, Parthasarathy Ranganathan, *The Datacenter as a Computer*, 3rd edition, Morgan & Claypool, 2018.
  How the machines you rent are built and what they cost to run.
- Your cloud provider's "Well-Architected" framework, especially the security and cost optimization pillars.
- Terraform (or OpenTofu) documentation, "Get Started" tutorials for your provider.
- John Young, "A first order approximation to the optimum checkpoint interval", *Communications of the ACM*, 1974; John Daly, "A higher
  order estimate of the optimum checkpoint interval for restart dumps", *Future Generation Computer Systems*, 2006.
- The FinOps Foundation's framework, for when the bill gets big enough to need a discipline.
