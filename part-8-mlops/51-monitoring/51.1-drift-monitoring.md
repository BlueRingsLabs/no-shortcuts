# 51.1 Drift, data quality and model monitoring

*About 12 hours. Prerequisites: 50.1, 43.2 (monitoring LLM systems), 05.2 (hypothesis tests), 18.3 (calibration). Lab: [`lab_51_1_drift.py`](labs/lab_51_1_drift.py).*

A model is trained on a snapshot of the world, and the world keeps moving. Customers change, products change, fraudsters adapt, an upstream
team changes a field's units, a pandemic happens. Models don't fail loudly when this happens: they keep returning predictions, with the same
confidence, and get quietly worse. Monitoring is how you find out before the customers, the auditors or the quarterly numbers do. It's also
where many teams drown in alerts that mean nothing, which is almost as bad as having none.

The lab monitors a credit model whose labels (did the loan default?) arrive 90 days after each prediction.

## What can change

- **Data quality problems**: a broken pipeline, a changed unit, a new category, nulls where there were none. These are bugs, and they're
  the most common cause of sudden model degradation. Catch them with the data tests of 50.1, run on every batch in production too.
- **Covariate shift**: the distribution of inputs $P(x)$ changes, but the relationship $P(y \mid x)$ doesn't. Younger customers, a new
  region.
- **Label (prior) shift**: the base rate $P(y)$ changes. A recession raises defaults across the board.
- **Concept drift**: $P(y \mid x)$ itself changes. The same inputs now mean something different: fraudsters adapted, a policy
  changed what "debt" measures, a competitor's launch changed customer behavior.

## Measuring input drift

Two common measures on each feature, comparing a current window with a reference (the training data or a stable recent period):

- **PSI** (population stability index): bin the reference into deciles, compare the share of the current data in each bin,
  $\text{PSI} = \sum (q_i - p_i)\ln(q_i/p_i)$. Rules of thumb: below 0.1 stable, 0.1 to 0.25 worth a look, above 0.25 significant.
- **Kolmogorov-Smirnov**: the largest gap between the two cumulative distributions, with a test of significance.

The lab's first table is the most important thing in this lesson:

| current window | n | PSI | KS p-value |
|---|---|---|---|
| same distribution | 50,000 | 0.000 | 0.25 |
| mean shifted by 0.02 sd (trivial) | 50,000 | 0.001 | 0.02 |
| mean shifted by 0.02 sd | 500 | 0.029 | 0.04 |
| mean shifted by 0.5 sd (real) | 50,000 | 0.234 | ≈ 0 |

A p-value mixes the size of a change with the amount of data. With 50,000 rows a trivial shift of 0.02 standard deviations is
"significant", and any window can cross p < 0.05 by chance. PSI measures how much the distribution moved, and stays near zero for trivial
shifts at any sample size. So alarm on an effect size (PSI, the KS statistic itself, a difference in means in standard deviations), and use
tests only to check that a window is large enough to estimate it.

## Alert fatigue

Production models have dozens to hundreds of features, checked daily. The lab monitors 50 features for 90 days with *no drift at all*: a
KS test at p < 0.05 raises 206 alarms, 2.3 a day. A PSI threshold of 0.1 raises none. A monitor that cries wolf twice a day gets muted
within a week, and then misses the real event. The practices:

- **Effect-size thresholds**, not p-values, or a multiple-testing correction if you use tests (05.2).
- **Persistence**: require the condition for two or three consecutive windows before paging anyone.
- **Prioritize**: monitor the model's output distribution and its most important features closely, the rest in a daily report.
- **Route by severity**: data-quality failures page someone; mild drift goes to a dashboard for weekly review.

## Does the drift matter?

Input drift is a reason to look, not a verdict. The lab runs the model on three production scenarios:

| scenario | PSI on income | PSI on the score | true AUC | true default rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| like training | 0.001 | 0.000 | 0.847 | 0.270 |
| covariate shift: incomes fall 0.7 sd | 0.442 | 0.150 | 0.843 | 0.372 |
| concept drift: debt stops predicting default | 0.001 | 0.000 | 0.749 | 0.239 |

The covariate shift is loud in the inputs and in the score distribution, and the model is fine: same relationship, different
population. The concept drift is invisible to every input and score monitor, and the model has lost a tenth of its AUC. Only outcomes
measure performance. Which brings us to the hard part.

## Monitoring without labels (yet)

The self-check: how do you monitor a model whose labels arrive 90 days later?

1. **Estimate performance from the model's own probabilities.** If the model is calibrated (18.3) and $P(y \mid x)$ hasn't changed, its
   scores predict its outcomes: the expected default rate is the mean score, and the expected accuracy is the mean of
   $\max(s, 1-s)$. This is confidence-based performance estimation (CBPE, the method NannyML popularized). In the lab it's accurate under
   covariate shift (estimated default rate 0.372, actual 0.372; accuracy 0.773 against 0.774) and confidently wrong under concept drift
   (0.272 against 0.239), because the model can't know its probabilities are stale.
2. **Faster proxies**: a first missed payment at 30 days, early chargebacks, customer complaints, human review of a sample. Validate
   that the proxy tracks the real outcome.
3. **Realized performance as labels arrive**, cohort by cohort, compared with the estimate at prediction time. A growing gap between
   estimated and realized performance is the concept-drift alarm.
4. **Input and output drift** (above) as the early warning that something changed and deserves a look.

## Retraining

- **Scheduled** retraining (weekly, monthly) is simple and handles slow drift. It retrains even when nothing changed, and does nothing
  about sudden shifts between runs.
- **Triggered** retraining responds to monitors (performance below a threshold, sustained drift) and needs the monitors to be trustworthy.
- Either way, retraining runs through the full pipeline and tests (50.1) and a staged rollout (50.2). Automatic retraining without gates is
  automatic deployment of whatever the data did last week, including a poisoned or broken batch (53.1).
- **Feedback loops**: when the model's decisions shape its future training data (declined loans never show whether they'd have defaulted,
  47.2), retraining on that data drifts the model toward its own past choices. Keep some randomized or reviewed decisions to measure what
  the model can't see.

> **Scar tissue.** A churn model I built as a freelance job for a small mobile app fell off a cliff one Tuesday and nobody noticed for
> five weeks, because labels arrived with a long delay and I only monitored accuracy. An app update had started sending session duration
> in milliseconds instead of seconds. A simple range check on the inputs, or a drift statistic on that feature, would have fired the same
> day. Monitor inputs and predictions, not only the metric you can compute a month later.

## Things that will bite you

- **p-value alarms** on large samples, and a monitor nobody reads.
- **Only monitoring inputs**, and calling a covariate shift a failure or a concept drift a success.
- **Uncalibrated scores** used to estimate performance.
- **Label delay ignored**: comparing this month's predictions with labels that only exist for last quarter's.
- **Retraining on feedback-biased data** without noticing.
- **Data-quality bugs** misdiagnosed as drift, and "fixed" by retraining on the broken data.
