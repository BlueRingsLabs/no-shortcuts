# 47.1 From business problem to ML design doc

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 18.3 (thresholds and costs), 18.1 (honest splits), 09.2 (point-in-time joins). Lab: [`lab_47_1_design_numbers.py`](labs/lab_47_1_design_numbers.py).*

Most failed ML projects didn't fail at modeling. They failed before the first line of code: they solved the wrong problem, optimized a
metric nobody cared about, trained on data that wouldn't exist at prediction time, or built a model when a rule would have done. The design
doc is where you catch those mistakes cheaply. It's a few pages written before building, reviewed by the people who'll use, run and pay for
the system, and updated as you learn. This Part is about everything after the model, and it starts here because nearly every production
problem in it traces back to a decision that should have been in this document.

The lab designs a card-fraud detection system from the business problem backwards, on synthetic transactions with a 0.28% fraud rate.

## What goes in it

1. **The problem, in business terms.** Who has it, what it costs now, what "solved" means. "Reduce fraud losses on card-not-present
   transactions by 30% without raising false declines above 0.1%", not "build a fraud model".
2. **Is ML the right tool?** What's the non-ML baseline (rules, heuristics, a human process), and how good is it? Would a better
   rule get most of the value? ML earns its place when the pattern is too complex for rules and there's data to learn it from.
3. **The decision the model feeds.** Who or what acts on the prediction, at what moment, with what capacity, and what each kind of
   error costs.
4. **Metrics at three levels:** the business metric (money, retention, time saved), the online product metric that should move it
   (alerts confirmed, click-through), and the offline model metric used during development. Say how they're connected, and how you'll
   know if they stop agreeing.
5. **Data**: sources, labels (where do they come from, how late, how noisy), volume, and, for every feature, *when it becomes available
   relative to the decision*.
6. **The baseline and the first model**: the simplest thing that could work, and how much better than the baseline it has to be to be
   worth running.
7. **Architecture**: batch or online (49.1), latency and throughput budgets, where features are computed, how the model gets updated.
8. **Evaluation and rollout**: the offline evaluation protocol, the online test (50.2), the rollback plan.
9. **Risks**: failure modes, feedback loops (47.2), abuse, fairness (54.1), privacy (54.2), and what monitoring will catch (51.1).
10. **Cost and effort**, and what you're *not* doing.

Short beats complete. A three-page doc that answers these questions honestly is better than a thirty-page one that hides them.

## The baseline

The fraud team already has rules: flag new-device transactions over $150, and any card with five or more transactions in an hour. They flag
2.6% of transactions with 1.1% precision. That's the bar. A trained gradient-boosted model gets an AUC of 0.77 and an average precision
of 0.015 against a base rate of 0.003. Is that good? AUC can't say. It depends on what happens next.

## From a score to a decision

The review team can check 150 alerts a day, each costing about $4 of analyst time, and each confirmed fraud saves its amount. That fixes the
operating point: not a 0.5 threshold, but the 150 highest-ranked transactions each day. At that capacity, over 15 test days:

| policy | precision | recall | fraud caught | net value per day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| rules (150 of those flagged, at random) | 0.011 | 0.031 | $5,659 | −$223 |
| model, top 150 by probability | 0.029 | 0.079 | $8,368 | −$42 |
| model, top 150 by expected loss (probability × amount) | 0.019 | 0.051 | $29,129 | $1,342 |

Three things happened:

- The model beats the rules on every metric at the same capacity.
- Ranking by probability loses money: most frauds it finds are small, and the reviews cost more than they recover.
- Ranking by **expected loss** has *lower* precision and recall than ranking by probability, and makes the system profitable, because the
  objective is money and the amounts vary a lot.

So the design doc's offline metric for this system is dollars caught at capacity (or a cost-weighted metric with the same ordering), with
precision at capacity as the operational metric the review team will feel. AUC stays as a diagnostic (18.3).

## Same AUC, different value

The lab builds two degraded versions of the model with nearly identical AUC, 0.712 and 0.720. One lost its ranking ability inside the
largest 20% of transactions, the other inside a random 20%. Net value: $614 a day against $1,361. A metric that treats every fraud alike
can't see where the money is. Weight the offline metric the way the business weighs errors, or report the money directly.

## Offline, online, and why they disagree

The self-check question for this module is why offline and online metrics disagree. The usual reasons:

- **Leakage**: features that exist in the training data but not at decision time.
- **Distribution shift**: the offline test set is last quarter; production is this week (51.1).
- **The metric isn't the objective**: AUC vs dollars, above; click-through vs long-term satisfaction.
- **Feedback loops**: the model changes the data it will be evaluated on (47.2), such as fraudsters adapting or users responding to
  recommendations.
- **Interaction effects**: the model is one component. The review team's behavior, the product's UI and other models all change what
  its predictions do.
- **Selection in the labels**: you only learn the outcome for cases you acted on (declined transactions are never confirmed as fraud or
  not).

## Available at decision time?

The data warehouse has a column `chargeback_filed`, sitting in the same table as the features. Trained with it, the model's offline AUC
jumps from 0.77 to 0.87. In production, where a chargeback is always filed days after the decision, the column is always zero, and the same
model scores 0.72, worse than the honest model. This is the most common way an offline result evaporates. The design doc prevents it
with one column in the feature table: **available at**, relative to the decision. The training data is then built as of that moment
(point-in-time joins, 09.2 and 14.2), and splits are by time (18.1).

## Reviewing a design doc

Questions worth asking of any ML design doc, including your own:

- What happens if the model is switched off? (If nothing, why build it?)
- What's the baseline, and how much better is the model on the business metric?
- For each feature: available when? Computed the same way in training and serving?
- Where do labels come from, when do they arrive, and what's missing from them?
- What's the feedback loop?
- How will we know it's broken in production, and how fast?
- Who is harmed when it's wrong, and do they have recourse?

> **Scar tissue.** A friend who owns a small bakery asked me for a demand forecast, and I spent two months of evenings making it accurate
> to a few percent, delivered by a script that ran every morning at nine. He orders flour and prepares dough at five. Nobody could use it
> without him changing a routine he'd had for fifteen years, which he had no intention of doing. One page written before any code, with a
> line about who acts on the prediction and when, would have found that on day one. He still sends me bread, out of pity.

## Things that will bite you

- **No baseline**, or a baseline nobody measured.
- **A 0.5 threshold** instead of an operating point set by capacity or cost.
- **Optimizing a proxy metric** that doesn't track the business objective.
- **Features from the future**, invisible until production.
- **Random splits** for data with time in it.
- **Labels that only exist for acted-on cases**, treated as a random sample.

## Exercises

1. Write the problem statement and the three levels of metrics for a model that prioritizes customer-support tickets.
2. Your review team's capacity doubles. What changes in the fraud design, and what do you re-measure?
3. Why can ranking by expected loss have lower precision than ranking by probability and still be better?
4. List the features of a churn model for a subscription service with their "available at" times, relative to a prediction made on the
   first of each month.
5. A fraud model declines transactions it scores highly. Why does this make its future training labels biased, and what can you do?
6. When should a design doc recommend not using ML?

1. Problem: urgent tickets wait too long, so customers with outages churn; goal: halve the median first-response time for urgent tickets
   without raising it for others by more than 10%. Business metric: churn and revenue retained among customers with urgent issues.

2. The operating point moves to the top 300 a day: re-measure precision and value at 300, since marginal alerts are lower quality, and
   the net value of the extra 150 may be negative. Check whether the team's precision (how carefully they review) changes with volume.
   The ranking objective stays the same.

3. Precision counts frauds; expected-loss ranking optimizes dollars. It trades several small, likely frauds for fewer large, less
   likely ones. Each large one caught is worth many small ones, so fewer hits can be worth much more.

4. Tenure, plan and price as of the first (available). Usage counts through the last day of the previous month (available, if the

   point-in-time join.

5. Declined transactions never complete, so you never learn whether they were fraud: labels exist only for transactions the model
   let through, a sample biased toward low scores. Retraining on them can drift. Mitigations: let a small random sample of high-score
   transactions through with step-up verification instead of declining, which gives labeled outcomes with known propensities; use
   outcomes from manual reviews; and correct with propensity weights (47.2).

6. When a simple rule captures most of the value; when there's no reliable label or not enough data; when errors are unacceptable and
   unexplainable decisions aren't allowed; when the cost of building and running it exceeds the value; or when the problem is really
   a process or product problem that a model would only paper over.
