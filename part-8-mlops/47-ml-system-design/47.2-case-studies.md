# 47.2 Design case studies

*About 15 hours. Prerequisites: 47.1, 46.1 (bandits), 41.1 (retrieval). Lab: [`lab_47_2_recsys_case.py`](labs/lab_47_2_recsys_case.py).*

This lesson applies 47.1's template to four systems that cover most of what ML engineers build: recommendations, search, fraud and an
LLM product. For each: the objective, the architecture, the data and its traps, and what goes wrong. It's also the preparation for the
"design X" interview (56.1), which is this lesson in 45 minutes with a whiteboard. The lab simulates the recommendation case, because
it has the two problems every learning-from-interaction system shares: you can't evaluate a new policy from logs naively, and the system
shapes its own training data.

## Recommendations

**Objective.** Long-term engagement or revenue, which you can't optimize directly. In practice it's a weighted combination of shorter-term
signals (clicks, watch time, purchases, saves), with guardrails against clickbait (hides, reports, churn).

**Architecture: a funnel.**

- **Candidate generation** (retrieval, milliseconds): from millions of items down to hundreds, with cheap methods: two-tower embeddings
  and approximate nearest neighbors (41.1, 41.2), co-occurrence ("people who watched X"), popularity, and business rules.
- **Ranking**: a heavier model (gradient boosting or a deep model) scores the hundreds with rich user, item and context features.
- **Re-ranking**: diversity, freshness, business constraints, deduplication.
- **Logging**: what was shown, where, *with what probability*, and what happened.

**The two traps, measured.** The lab simulates 2,000 users and 300 items with hidden true preferences. People click what's on top.

*Offline evaluation from logs.* The current system ranks by popularity with some randomization. A candidate personalized ranker would
get a top-slot click-through rate of 0.691 if deployed (the simulation knows the truth). Estimating that from the old system's logs:

| logger's randomization | naive estimate | IPS estimate [95% interval] | largest IPS weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| low (temperature 0.5) | 0.716 | 0.687 [0.552, 0.881] | 1,753 |
| higher (temperature 2.0) | 0.643 | 0.635 [0.550, 0.726] | 289 |

The naive estimate uses only the logged cases where the old system happened to show what the new one likes, and inherits the old system's
choices. **Inverse propensity scoring** reweights each logged outcome by (probability the new policy shows it) / (probability the old one
did), which is unbiased if the old system logged its probabilities and gave every item some chance. The price is variance: when the logger
barely explored, a few weights run into the thousands and the interval is enormous. The design rules: log propensities, keep some
randomization in production, use offline estimates to shortlist candidates, and let an online test decide (50.2).

*Feedback loops.* A non-personalized system retrained on its own clicks for 8 rounds:

| exploration | share of impressions to the top 10 items | items shown at all, last round | true top 5 found |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | 1.00 every round | 5 of 300 | 0 of 5 |
| 10% | about 0.91 | 286 of 300 | 1 of 5 |

Without exploration, whatever won early gets all the exposure, and nothing else can prove itself: the system converges on 5 items, none
of which are the best. Exploration keeps the data informative, though 8 rounds of 10% exploration is still slow to find the best (46.1's
lesson about regret and horizons applies directly: use a bandit, not a fixed rate).

## Search

**Objective.** Users find what they're looking for, measured by successful sessions (a click followed by no quick return to the results,
a purchase), with relevance judgments for offline work.

**Architecture.** Query understanding (spelling, intent, entities), retrieval (BM25 plus dense, 41.1, hybrid), learning-to-rank on
features (text match, popularity, freshness, personalization), and re-ranking. Offline metrics are nDCG and MRR on judged queries (41.4);
online, click models and interleaving experiments, which compare two rankers within the same result page and detect differences with far
less traffic than A/B tests.

**Traps.** Position bias in click logs (the same problem as recommendations: clicks at rank 1 aren't worth the same as at rank 8);
head queries dominate metrics while the long tail is where users fail; exact-match needs (product codes, error IDs) that dense retrieval
misses (41.1).

## Fraud

47.1 designed it. The elements that recur: a heavily imbalanced target with delayed labels (chargebacks arrive weeks later); an operating
point set by review capacity and costs; an adversary who adapts, so the distribution shifts on purpose (51.1); selective labels (declined
transactions never get a label); latency budgets of tens of milliseconds for online decisions, so features must be precomputed (streaming
aggregates, 14.2) and the model small (49.3); rules and models side by side, because rules are how the fraud team responds within hours to
a new attack.

## An LLM product: a support assistant

**Objective.** Resolve customer questions without a human, correctly, measured by resolution rate (no escalation, no repeat contact within
a week), deflection with a quality floor, and customer satisfaction. Guardrails: wrong-answer rate on audited samples, escalation latency.

**Architecture.** Retrieval over help-center content and the customer's account data (Module 41, with access control applied by the system),
a model with tools for account lookups (40.3) scoped to the authenticated customer, structured escalation to a human with the full
context (42.3), evals built from real tickets (43.1), tracing and online evaluation (43.2), injection defenses on anything the model reads
(44.1), and a gateway with caching, routing and budgets (45.1).

**Traps.** Deflection optimized at the expense of correctness (the assistant "resolves" by giving up the customer's time); stale help
content retrieved with confidence; actions on accounts without authorization scoping; a silent model update (43.2); and the evaluation
that only tests questions someone thought of (43.1).

## A method for any case

1. Clarify the objective and constraints (users, scale, latency, what errors cost).
2. Propose the baseline and the metrics at three levels.
3. Sketch the data flow: sources, labels, features with their availability, training data construction.
4. Choose the model family and serving pattern for the constraints, starting simple.
5. Plan evaluation offline and online, and the rollout.
6. Name the failure modes: feedback loops, shift, abuse, fairness, privacy, and how monitoring catches them.
7. Say what you'd do first, and what you'd leave for later.

## Things that will bite you

- **Evaluating a new policy on logs as if they were unbiased.**
- **Logs without propensities**, and production with no randomization at all.
- **Systems that learn only from what they already show.**
- **Position bias** treated as relevance.
- **Optimizing short-term signals** until the long-term metric breaks.

## Exercises

1. Design the candidate generation and ranking stages for a job-recommendation site. What does each stage optimize, and how are they
   evaluated?
2. Why is IPS unbiased, and what makes its variance large? What does self-normalization trade?
3. A news recommender optimizes clicks and gets more clickbait. Which parts of the design would you change?
4. How would you evaluate a new search ranker with little traffic?
5. For the support assistant, what's the online metric, what's the guardrail, and what gets audited by humans?
6. What's the minimum logging a new ML-driven product should have from day one?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Candidate generation: embeddings of candidate profiles and jobs (two towers trained on applications and recruiter responses),
   location and eligibility filters as hard constraints, plus recent and popular postings; evaluated by recall of later applications
   within the candidate set. Ranking: predicted probability of a mutual match (application and recruiter interest), with features on skills,
   seniority, salary and location fit and the posting's freshness; evaluated offline with nDCG on held-out applications (time split), online
   with applications and interviews per session. Re-ranking for diversity of companies, and so no posting is flooded.

2. $\mathbb{E}_{\text{logger}}[\frac{\pi_{\text{new}}(a)}{\pi_{\text{log}}(a)} r] = \sum_a \pi_{\text{log}}(a) \frac{\pi_{\text{new}}(a)}{\pi_{\text{log}}(a)} \mathbb{E}[r \mid a] = \mathbb{E}_{\text{new}}[r]$,
   as long as $\pi_{\text{log}}(a) > 0$ wherever $\pi_{\text{new}}(a) > 0$. Variance is large when the new policy favors actions the
   logger rarely took (huge weights on few samples). Self-normalized IPS divides by the sum of weights: a small bias for much lower
   variance. Clipping weights is another bias-for-variance trade.

3. The objective: add dwell time, returns and hides to the reward, and down-weight clicks with short dwell. The evaluation: add long-term
   metrics (return visits over weeks) and a quality audit. The ranking features: publisher quality signals. And guardrail metrics in
   every experiment (50.2).

4. Interleaving: merge results from both rankers into one list for the same user and query and credit clicks to the ranker that
   contributed the clicked result. It needs one to two orders of magnitude less traffic than an A/B test to detect a preference. Also
   offline evaluation on judged queries (nDCG), and counterfactual estimates from logs if propensities are logged.

5. Online: resolution rate (no escalation or repeat contact within seven days). Guardrail: the wrong-answer rate on an audited sample,
   plus complaint and escalation rates. Audited by humans: a random sample of conversations weekly, all conversations with negative
   feedback or escalations, and any conversation in which the assistant took an account action.

6. For every prediction: a request ID, timestamp, model and feature versions, the input features (or a reference), the output, what was
   shown or done, with the probability if randomized, and later the outcome joined by request ID. Without this, you can't debug,
   evaluate a new model on past traffic, or measure drift.
