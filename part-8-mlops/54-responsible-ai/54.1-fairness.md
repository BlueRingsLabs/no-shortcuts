# 54.1 Fairness

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 18.3 (thresholds, error rates, calibration), 47.1 (design docs), 50.2 (segments in experiments). Lab: [`lab_54_1_fairness.py`](labs/lab_54_1_fairness.py).*

Models make decisions about people: who gets a loan, an interview, a medical follow-up, extra scrutiny at a border. When those decisions
systematically disadvantage groups of people, it's harmful, often illegal, and usually invisible in the aggregate metrics the team watches.
This lesson is about measuring it, about the uncomfortable mathematical fact that "fair" has several incompatible definitions, and about
what engineers can and can't decide on their own. It's not a compliance checkbox. It's the part where your model meets actual human beings.

The lab uses a synthetic lending decision with two groups, A (70% of applicants) and B (30%), whose repayment rates differ: 0.66 against 0.44.
In real data, such differences have causes, including the effects of past discrimination on income and credit history. A model trained on
that data learns the world as it was.

## Measuring: group metrics

The standard metrics compare groups on quantities from 18.3:

- **Demographic parity** (statistical parity): equal approval (selection) rates.
- **Equal opportunity**: equal true positive rates. People who would repay get approved at the same rate in each group.
- **Equalized odds**: equal true positive *and* false positive rates.
- **Predictive parity**: equal precision (PPV). Approved people repay at the same rate in each group.
- **Calibration within groups**: a score of 0.8 means an 80% repayment rate in every group.

The lab's model (logistic regression with the group column, approve when the predicted repayment probability exceeds 0.7):

| metric | A | B | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| approval rate | 0.532 | 0.220 | +0.31 |
| TPR (repayers approved) | 0.686 | 0.410 | +0.28 |
| FPR (defaulters approved) | 0.228 | 0.071 | +0.16 |
| PPV (approved who repay) | 0.857 | 0.820 | +0.04 |
| calibration gap | 0.006 | 0.015 | small |

By calibration and PPV, the model is nearly fair. By approval rate and TPR, it's far from it: a repayer in group B is 40% less likely to get a
loan than a repayer in group A. Which of these is "the" fairness of this model? That's the wrong question, as the next section shows.

## "Fairness through unawareness" doesn't work

The instinctive fix is to remove the group column. The lab's blind model still sees the applicant's neighborhood, which predicts group
membership with 89% accuracy on its own. Without the group column, the approval-rate gap only shrinks from 0.31 to 0.25, and group B's scores
become less calibrated (calibration gap 0.015 to 0.041): the same score now means less for them. The model reconstructs the attribute from
correlated features (postal codes, names, schools, purchase histories, even writing style). Meanwhile you've lost the ability to measure
the disparity.

So keep the protected attribute for evaluation, where the law allows it (and in some places it doesn't, which is its own problem), even when
the model must not use it for decisions.

## The impossibility results

Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2016) and Chouldechova (2017) proved that when base rates differ between groups, a score that's
calibrated within each group can't also give them equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates, except in degenerate cases
(perfect prediction). The lab shows it with the best possible score, the *true* repayment probability, calibrated for everyone by
construction. With a single threshold of 0.7: approval rates 0.536 against 0.217, TPR 0.690 against 0.406, FPR 0.232 against 0.069. The score
isn't biased; the groups' score distributions differ, so any shared threshold treats them differently in rates.

This is the answer to the module's self-check question, and its consequence matters more than its proof: **you have to choose** which
criterion to satisfy, and the choice is about values and law, not statistics. Equalizing error rates means a given score is treated
differently by group. Equal calibration means error rates differ. Demographic parity ignores the labels entirely, which is right when the
labels themselves are biased (past hiring decisions as "ground truth" for hiring) and wrong when they aren't.

## Mitigation, and its costs

Interventions happen at three points:

- **Pre-processing**: fix the data. Collect more representative data, reweight groups, question labels that encode past decisions.
- **In-processing**: constrain the training objective (fairness penalties, adversarial debiasing, constrained optimization as in
  Fairlearn's reductions approach).
- **Post-processing**: adjust decisions, for example with group-specific thresholds (Hardt, Price and Srebro, 2016).

The lab applies the last one: keep group A's threshold at 0.70 and lower group B's to 0.48, chosen so B's true positive rate equals A's:

| metric | A | B |
|---|---|---|
| approval rate | 0.532 | 0.430 |
| TPR | 0.686 | 0.686 |
| FPR | 0.228 | 0.228 |
| PPV | 0.857 | 0.703 |

Repayers in both groups now get loans at the same rate, and here the false positive rates happen to line up too. Accuracy even rises (0.711
to 0.721), because 0.7 was never the accuracy-optimal threshold: the lender chose it for its own costs. That cost appears in group B's
precision: 30% of approved B applicants now default, against 18% before. Someone has to decide whether that trade is right, and whether
group-specific thresholds are allowed at all: some jurisdictions and domains require them as remediation, others prohibit explicit use of
the protected attribute in decisions. That decision belongs to the organization, with legal counsel and the affected stakeholders, not to
the engineer who happens to be tuning thresholds. The engineer's job is to measure, to lay out the options with their costs, and to
document what was chosen and why (54.3).

## Beyond classification rates

- **Representation harms** in generative models: stereotyped images, dialects handled worse, some names "sounding risky". Measure with
  targeted test sets per group (43.1).
- **Quality-of-service gaps**: speech recognition that works worse for some accents, face detection worse for darker skin. Slice every
  evaluation (50.1) by the groups the system serves.
- **Feedback loops**: a model that denies loans to a group prevents that group from building the credit history the model rewards (47.2).
- **Who is missing**: people absent from the training data (declined applicants, the unbanked) are the ones the model knows least about.

> **Scar tissue.** As a student volunteer, I once reviewed a model that pre-screened applications for a university scholarship. It didn't
> use gender, and the people who built it were proud of that. It did use embeddings of free-text fields, including the names of clubs and
> sports, and a proxy check showed those features predicted gender with high accuracy. Selection rates differed accordingly. Removing a
> column doesn't remove the information, and "we don't use it" is a statement about your code, not about your model's behavior.

## Things that will bite you

- **Aggregate metrics only**, with no slices by the groups the system affects.
- **Dropping the protected attribute** and calling it fair.
- **Choosing a fairness metric** because it makes the numbers look good.
- **Labels that are past decisions**, treated as ground truth.
- **Engineers deciding value trade-offs alone**, undocumented.
- **Fairness measured once** at launch and never again, while the population shifts (51.1).

## Exercises

1. A hiring model has equal selection rates across groups but much lower precision for one group. Who is harmed, and how?
2. Why can't a calibrated score satisfy equalized odds when base rates differ? Give the intuition without the proof.
3. In the lab, dropping the group column made group B's calibration worse. Why?
4. When is demographic parity the right target, and when is it the wrong one?
5. Design the fairness evaluation for a speech-to-text system used in a call center.
6. Your manager asks you to "make the model fair" by Friday. What do you do on Monday?

1. The selected candidates from that group are less likely to be good fits, which can harm them (hired into roles where they're set up to
   fail, reinforcing stereotypes when they struggle) and the employer; and it suggests the model's scores mean different things per group,

2. Calibration fixes what a score means: among people scored 0.8, 80% repay, in every group. If groups have different base rates, their
   score distributions differ (more mass at low scores for the group with the lower rate). A single threshold then cuts the two
   distributions at different points of their mass, so the share of repayers above it (TPR) and of defaulters above it (FPR) differ. To
   equalize those rates you must move the thresholds per group, and then the same score leads to different decisions, or distort the
   scores, and then they're no longer calibrated.

3. With the group column, the model could account for group-level differences directly; without it, it uses the neighborhood as a partial
   proxy. For group B members whose neighborhood looks like group A's (and vice versa), the model's estimate mixes the two groups' patterns,
   so scores are systematically off for part of B, which shows up as a larger calibration gap for the smaller group.

4. Right when the labels or the outcome itself reflect bias you don't want to reproduce (historical hiring or policing decisions as
   labels), or when access matters in its own right (a share of opportunities). Wrong when there are real differences in the outcome the
   model must predict and meeting parity would mean approving people who'll be harmed (loans they can't repay) or denying people who
   qualify.
