# 54.3 Governance, regulation and documentation

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 54.1, 54.2, 48.1 (registries), 47.1 (design docs). Lab: [`lab_54_3_governance.py`](labs/lab_54_3_governance.py).*

Governance is the part of ML engineers like least and need most once their models touch anything that matters. It answers boring questions
that become urgent after an incident: who decided this model could be used this way, on what evidence, who checks it, and what happens when
it's wrong. Regulation is increasingly turning those questions into legal requirements. This lesson covers the documentation that makes the
answers exist, the frameworks that organize them, and the EU AI Act, the broadest AI law so far, as the example. It isn't legal
advice, and the specifics will keep changing; the engineering habits it asks for won't.

## Documentation that stays true

- **Model cards** (Mitchell et al., 2019): what the model is, what it's for and not for, how it was trained and evaluated, how it performs
  on relevant slices and groups (54.1), its limitations and ethical considerations, and who to contact.
- **Datasheets for datasets** (Gebru et al., 2021): why the dataset was created, how it was collected, what it contains and misses,
  preprocessing, recommended and inappropriate uses, maintenance.
- **System documentation**: the design doc (47.1), the risk assessment, the monitoring plan (51.1), the incident runbook.

The failure mode of documentation is staleness: a card written by hand at launch describes a model three retrainings ago. The lab generates
the card from what the system already knows: the registry's lineage (48.1), the evaluation report and slices (50.1), the fairness audit
(54.1). A registration check refuses a model whose card has an empty section. In the lab, the forgotten "human oversight" section blocks
registration until someone writes down how people review the scores, which is exactly the conversation the check exists to force. Treat the
card as a release artifact, tested like code.

## Frameworks

- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (AI RMF 1.0, 2023) organizes risk work into four functions: *Govern* (policies, roles,
  accountability), *Map* (context, intended use, impacts), *Measure* (evaluation, testing, monitoring), *Manage* (prioritize, respond,
  document). A Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) adds LLM-specific risks. Voluntary, widely used as a structure.
- **ISO/IEC 42001** (2023): an auditable management-system standard for AI, in the style of ISO 27001 for information security.
- **Sector rules** often apply first: model risk management in banking (the US SR 11-7 guidance), medical device regulation, employment and
  anti-discrimination law, consumer credit law requiring explanations for adverse decisions, data protection law (GDPR's rules on
  automated decision-making).

## The EU AI Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force in August 2024, with obligations phasing in over 2025 to 2027 (and a timeline that has been the
subject of amendment proposals, so check the current state). It regulates by **risk tier of the use**, not by technique:

- **Prohibited practices** (Article 5): social scoring, manipulation exploiting vulnerabilities, untargeted scraping of facial images to
  build recognition databases, emotion recognition in workplaces and schools (with narrow exceptions), biometric categorization to infer
  sensitive traits, and, with exceptions, real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement.
- **High-risk systems**: safety components of regulated products (Annex I: machinery, medical devices, toys...) and the areas of Annex III,
  including biometric identification, critical infrastructure, education (admissions, assessment), employment (recruiting, promotion,
  task allocation, monitoring), access to essential services (creditworthiness of individuals, life and health insurance pricing, public
  benefits), law enforcement, migration and border control, and the administration of justice. Obligations include a risk management
  system, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency to deployers, human oversight, accuracy and robustness,
  cybersecurity, conformity assessment and registration.
- **Transparency obligations** (Article 50): people must be told when they interact with an AI system, and AI-generated content must be
  marked as such, with specific rules for deepfakes and emotion recognition.
- **General-purpose AI models** have their own obligations (documentation, copyright policies, training-data summaries), with more for
  models posing systemic risk.
- Everything else is **minimal risk**: no specific obligations beyond other law.

The lab's triage of six use cases: the loan scorer and CV screening are high-risk; the support chatbot and the marketing image generator
carry transparency duties; the spam filter is minimal risk; employee mood detection from webcams is prohibited. The same gradient-boosted
trees are minimal-risk as a spam filter and high-risk as a credit scorer: **the tier depends on the use**. Triage at design time (47.1),
because high-risk obligations shape the system (logging, oversight, documentation) and are expensive to retrofit. And route everything
beyond the obvious to people who know the law.

## What engineers own

Most governance work isn't legal work; it's engineering done deliberately:

- **Traceability**: lineage from every prediction to model version, data and code (48.1, 43.2).
- **Logging** sufficient to reconstruct decisions, with retention that satisfies both audit needs and privacy law (54.2).
- **Evaluation** by slice and group, repeated on every release (50.1, 54.1).
- **Human oversight that works**: reviewers with the information, time and authority to disagree with the model, and records of when they
  do (a rubber stamp isn't oversight; neither is a 100% agreement rate).
- **Monitoring and incident response** (51.1): who is paged when the model harms someone, and how it's switched off.
- **Recourse**: a way for affected people to contest a decision and reach a human.

## Things that will bite you

- **Cards written once** and never updated.
- **"Intended use" nobody enforces**, so the model ends up used for something else.
- **Human oversight on paper**, with reviewers who can't see why the model decided or can't afford to disagree.
- **Risk classification after the build.**
- **No record** of the decisions made about trade-offs (54.1) and who made them.
- **Governance as a gate at the end** rather than a practice through the lifecycle.

## Exercises

1. Write the "out of scope" and "human oversight" sections of a model card for a model that flags insurance claims for fraud review.
2. Classify these under the EU AI Act, briefly justifying each: a model that ranks university applicants; a chatbot for restaurant bookings;
   a tool that detects defects in car brake parts on an assembly line; a workplace tool that infers employees' emotions from their voice.
3. What makes human oversight real rather than nominal? Give three measurable indicators.
4. Map the four NIST AI RMF functions onto the lessons of this Part.
5. A team wants to reuse a model built for one purpose for a new one. What does governance require before they do?
6. Which records would you need to answer a regulator's question "why was this applicant rejected on 3 March?"
