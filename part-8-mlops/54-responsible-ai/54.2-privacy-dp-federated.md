# 54.2 Privacy: differential privacy and federated learning

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 53.1 (membership inference), 04.2 (probability), 26.1 (SGD). Lab: [`lab_54_2_privacy.py`](labs/lab_54_2_privacy.py).*

Models trained on personal data can leak it. 39.1 and 53.1 measured the simplest leak: whether a record was in the training set. Worse leaks
exist: language models reproducing training text verbatim, including phone numbers and addresses (Carlini et al., 2021), and images
reconstructed from gradients. Privacy law (GDPR and its relatives) treats personal data, and sometimes models derived from it, with strict
rules on purpose, minimization, retention and deletion. This lesson covers the two technical tools people reach for, differential privacy
and federated learning: what each guarantees, what each costs, and what neither does.

## First, the ordinary controls

Most privacy failures in ML aren't subtle. Before any mathematics:

- **Minimize**: don't collect or train on personal data the task doesn't need. Drop direct identifiers; question quasi-identifiers.
- **Purpose and consent**: data collected for one purpose isn't automatically available for training a model for another.
- **Access control and retention** on training data, traces (43.2) and logs, with deletion that actually deletes (48.2's versioned data
  complicates this; design for it).
- **Pseudonymization** helps, and isn't anonymization: records can often be re-identified by joining quasi-identifiers (the classic result
  is that most people are unique by zip code, birth date and sex).
- **Audit before release**: membership inference and extraction tests on models trained on personal data (53.1).

## Differential privacy

**Differential privacy** (Dwork et al., 2006) is a guarantee about an algorithm: its output distribution barely changes whether or not any
single person's record is in the input. Formally, an algorithm $M$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differentially private if for any two datasets
differing in one record and any set of outputs $S$:

$$P[M(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\, P[M(D') \in S] + \delta.$$

Whatever an attacker does with the output, and whatever else they know, they learn almost nothing more about any individual than they would
if that individual's record had been left out. Smaller $\varepsilon$ means stronger privacy; values below about 1 are strong, single digits are
common in practice, and double digits are a weak guarantee. $\delta$ should be much smaller than one over the number of records.

**DP-SGD** (Abadi et al., 2016) makes training differentially private:

1. compute each example's gradient separately;
2. **clip** each to a maximum norm $C$, bounding any single example's influence;
3. sum, and **add Gaussian noise** with standard deviation $\sigma C$;
4. take the step;
5. **account** for the privacy spent over all steps. Each step on a random batch is a subsampled Gaussian mechanism, and accountants
   (Rényi DP, the moments accountant) add up the cost tightly.

The lab implements it from scratch on a logistic regression over 400 random features, trained on 1,000 records of which 15% have wrong
labels (the unusual records a model memorizes):

| training | $\varepsilon$ ($\delta = 10^{-5}$) | test accuracy | membership AUC |
|---|---|---|---|
| ordinary SGD | ∞ | 0.617 | 0.603 |
| clipping only, no noise | ∞ | 0.683 | 0.539 |
| DP-SGD, noise multiplier 0.6 | 44.9 | 0.680 | 0.539 |
| DP-SGD, noise multiplier 2.0 | 9.8 | 0.671 | 0.539 |
| DP-SGD, noise multiplier 4.0 | 4.5 | 0.652 | 0.534 |

(The lab's accountant is a simple approximation; use Opacus or TensorFlow Privacy for real work.)

Three things to read off:

- **Clipping alone removes most of the leak this audit can measure**, and here even improves accuracy, because ordinary SGD memorizes the
  mislabeled records and clipping limits how much any record can pull the model.
- **The noise is what buys the guarantee.** The audit shows almost the same AUC with and without noise, but an audit is one attack: it
  gives a *lower* bound on leakage. $\varepsilon$ is an *upper* bound, against every possible attack. Clipping without noise has no bound at all.
- **Accuracy falls as $\varepsilon$ shrinks**: 0.68 to 0.65 from $\varepsilon = 45$ to 4.5 on a thousand records. Privacy is cheaper with
  more data (the noise is added once per batch, and large batches dilute it) and with public pretraining followed by private fine-tuning,
  which is how DP is used for language models today.

Also: DP protects individuals, not groups. A model can be differentially private and still reveal that a population has some property,
which is often the point of the model. And DP costs accuracy unevenly: underrepresented groups, whose examples are rarer, lose more (Bagdasaryan
et al., 2019), a fairness issue (54.1).

## Federated learning

**Federated learning** trains a model across many data holders (phones, hospitals, banks) without collecting the data in one place.
**Federated averaging** (McMahan et al., 2017): each round, the server sends the current model; each client trains on its local data for a
few steps; the server averages the resulting models, weighted by data size.

The lab: ten hospitals with 30 patients each, each seeing a different population:

| training | mean accuracy on each hospital's population |
|---|---|
| each hospital alone | 0.622 |
| federated averaging, 30 rounds | 0.709 |
| federated, then fine-tuned at each hospital | 0.691 |
| all data pooled centrally (not allowed here) | 0.710 |

Federated averaging nearly matches pooling without any record leaving its hospital. Fine-tuning the shared model locally on 30 patients
makes it worse: personalization needs enough local data. With very different populations or many local steps per round, clients' updates
pull against each other, and federated training falls behind pooling (the non-IID problem).

What federated learning is not: private by itself. The updates are functions of the local data, and gradients can be inverted to reconstruct
training examples, especially for small batches. Real deployments add **secure aggregation** (cryptography so the server sees only the sum
of updates) and **differential privacy** on the updates (clipping and noise per client). It's also operationally hard: unreliable clients,
heterogeneous hardware, debugging a model trained on data you can't see.

## Other tools

- **Synthetic data**: generated records that mimic the statistics of real ones. Useful for testing and sharing, but only private if the
  generator is trained with DP; otherwise it can reproduce real records.
- **Secure enclaves and multi-party computation**: compute on data that stays encrypted or sealed. Protects against the operator, with
  performance and complexity costs.
- **Machine unlearning**: removing a record's influence from a trained model after a deletion request. Exact unlearning means retraining
  (sharding the data makes it cheaper, as in SISA training); approximate methods are an active research area.

## Things that will bite you

- **Pseudonymized data treated as anonymous.**
- **An audit read as a guarantee**, or $\varepsilon$ reported without $\delta$, the accountant and the number of steps.
- **Clipping without noise** called differential privacy.
- **Federated learning presented as privacy**, with no secure aggregation or DP.
- **Deletion requests** that can't be honored because data is copied into snapshots, caches and models.
- **Privacy costs** that fall hardest on minority groups, unmeasured.

## Exercises

1. Explain $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP to a product manager in two sentences, and what $\varepsilon = 8$ means.
2. Why does DP-SGD clip per-example gradients rather than the batch gradient?
3. The lab's audit shows almost no difference between clipping only and DP-SGD with $\varepsilon = 4.5$. Why keep the noise?
4. A hospital consortium wants to train a model without sharing records. Design it, and list what protects patient privacy.
5. How does training data size change the accuracy cost of DP, and why?
6. A user asks for their data to be deleted, and it was used to train a production model. What are your options?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. "Whatever the model reveals, it would reveal almost the same whether or not your data was used; $\varepsilon$ measures 'almost'."
   $\varepsilon = 8$ means the probability of any outcome changes by at most a factor of $e^8 \approx 3000$ with or without one person's
   record: a weak guarantee in the worst case, though typically far better protection in practice than none.

2. DP needs a bound on how much any *single* record can change the update. Clipping each example's gradient bounds each record's
   contribution to the sum by $C$, so noise proportional to $C$ hides any one of them. Clipping the batch gradient bounds the sum, not
   the contribution of any individual inside it.

3. The audit is one attack; it shows a lower bound on what leaks. Without noise, there's no upper bound: a better attack (or a worst-case
   record, like an outlier) could extract much more. The noise and the accounting give a guarantee that holds against every attack,
   including ones not yet invented, which is what makes it defensible to regulators and patients.

4. Federated averaging across hospitals, with secure aggregation (the coordinator sees only sums), DP on the client updates (clip and noise
   per hospital, accounted over rounds), a shared evaluation protocol with local test sets, governance agreements (who can query the model,
   for what), and an audit of the final model for memorization (53.1). Protections: data never leaves the hospitals; the coordinator can't
   see individual updates; DP bounds what the model reveals about any patient.

5. The noise per step is fixed by $\sigma C$, while the signal (the sum of gradients) grows with the batch size. Larger datasets allow larger
   batches at the same sampling rate, so the noise-to-signal ratio falls, and the privacy amplification from subsampling improves. So the
   accuracy cost of a given $\varepsilon$ shrinks as data grows, which is why DP works well at scale and poorly on small datasets.

6. Delete the data from all stores and snapshots. For the model: assess whether it can reveal the person's data (memorization audit) and
   what the law requires (it varies); retrain without the data (on schedule, or promptly if required), which is simplest if training is
   reproducible (48.2) and cheap; use sharded training so only one shard needs retraining; or, if the model was trained with DP, document
   that its influence is bounded. Record the decision.
