# 49.3 Making models small and fast: ONNX, TensorRT, distillation, pruning

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 49.1, 33.1 (memory, FLOPs and bandwidth), 37.3 (quantization). Lab: [`lab_49_3_small_fast_models.py`](labs/lab_49_3_small_fast_models.py).*

A model that's too slow or too expensive to serve is a research result, not a product. Somewhere between the notebook and production,
someone asks for p99 under 50 ms on a CPU instance, or for the thing to run on a phone, or for the GPU bill to halve, and the answer is
rarely "a better model". It's the same model, or a smaller one that behaves like it, run by software that wastes less. This lesson covers
the four levers (distillation, quantization, pruning, and compiled runtimes), what each costs in accuracy and engineering, and the order in
which to pull them.

## First, measure

Before making anything smaller, find out where the time goes (33.3). In 49.1, the small model took 82 µs and the request took 1.6 ms: shrinking
the model would have changed nothing a user could feel. The usual suspects before the model: serialization, feature fetching, Python
overhead, network hops, batch size one where batching was possible. Profile the request, not the forward pass.

Then decide what you're optimizing: latency at batch 1 (interactive), throughput per dollar (batch jobs, high-traffic services), memory
(fitting on a device or a smaller GPU), or model size on disk (mobile downloads). The levers help these differently.

## Distillation

**Knowledge distillation** (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015) trains a small **student** to imitate a large **teacher**. The student's loss
mixes the usual cross-entropy on labels with a term matching the teacher's output distribution, both softened by a temperature $T$:

$$\mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha)\,\text{CE}(y, p_s) + \alpha\, T^2\, \text{KL}\big(\text{softmax}(z_t/T)\,\|\,\text{softmax}(z_s/T)\big).$$

The soft targets carry more information than a label: which wrong classes the teacher found plausible ("dark knowledge"). The lab trains a
teacher (1.1M parameters) and 13x smaller students on a 10-class tabular task:

| student (85k parameters) trained on | test accuracy |
|---|---|
| (the teacher, 50k labels) | 0.889 |
| 50k labels | 0.861 |
| 50k labels + the teacher's outputs | 0.856 |
| 2k labels | 0.528 |
| 2k labels + the teacher's outputs on 48k unlabeled inputs | 0.852 |

The textbook version (same labels, plus soft targets) did nothing here: the student was limited by its size, not by its supervision. On
image and language tasks the soft targets usually help a few points; on this data they don't, which is a reason to measure rather than
assume. The row that matters is the last one: **a teacher labeling data nobody labeled** turns 2,000 labels into nearly the accuracy of
50,000. That's the dominant form of distillation in industry today: a large model (often an LLM, prompted carefully and checked, 40.2)
labels a large sample of real traffic, and a small, cheap model trained on those labels serves it. Distilled LLMs (the small members of most
model families) are trained the same way on the large model's outputs or logits.

Costs: a training pipeline, a teacher to run over a lot of data once, and a student that inherits the teacher's mistakes and biases (check
it by slice, 50.1 and 54.1).

## Quantization

Store and compute with fewer bits. For a weight matrix, symmetric quantization picks a scale $s$ and stores integers
$q = \text{round}(w/s)$ in $[-(2^{b-1}-1), 2^{b-1}-1]$. The **granularity** of the scale matters: one per tensor, one per output channel,
or one per group of 32 to 128 weights. The lab, on the teacher:

| weights | size | test accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| float32 | 4.51 MB | 0.889 |
| int8, per tensor | 1.13 MB | 0.889 |
| int8, per channel | 1.14 MB | 0.889 |
| int4, per tensor | 0.57 MB | 0.881 |
| int4, per channel | 0.57 MB | 0.885 |
| int4, per group of 32 | 0.64 MB | 0.888 |

Int8 weights are free on almost every model. At 4 bits, per-tensor scales lose a little here, and much more on LLMs, whose weights and
activations have outliers that stretch the scale (37.3 covers GPTQ, AWQ and the LLM-specific methods).

The kinds of quantization, from least to most effort:

- **Weight-only** (the table): smaller files and less memory traffic; the matmul may still run in float after dequantizing. The main
  win for memory-bound workloads, which includes LLM decoding.
- **Dynamic quantization**: int8 weights, activations quantized on the fly per batch, integer matmuls. One line in most frameworks, no
  calibration data. The lab's int8 teacher runs 2 to 3 times faster on one CPU core with the same accuracy.
- **Static (post-training) quantization**: activation ranges calibrated on a few hundred representative inputs, so everything runs in
  integers. Faster; needs good calibration data (use real traffic, not the training set's easy examples).
- **Quantization-aware training**: simulate quantization during training so the model adapts. For when post-training quantization loses
  too much, typically at 4 bits or on small, sensitive models.
- **Low-precision floats**: bf16 and fp16 are the default for GPU inference; fp8 on recent GPUs.

## Pruning

Remove parameters that don't matter. **Unstructured** pruning zeros individual weights, typically the smallest (Han et al., 2015); a short
fine-tune afterwards recovers accuracy:

| teacher weights zeroed | no fine-tuning | 2 epochs of fine-tuning |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | 0.886 | 0.890 |
| 80% | 0.840 | 0.894 |
| 95% | 0.519 | 0.808 |

And then the catch: the 95%-sparse teacher is **no faster** than the dense one (the lab measures it), because a dense matrix full of
zeros costs the same to multiply. Unstructured sparsity pays off only with sparse storage and kernels, which beat dense ones only at very
high sparsity, or with hardware-supported patterns such as NVIDIA's 2:4 sparsity (two zeros in every four weights, up to about 2x on
supporting GPUs).

**Structured** pruning removes whole units, channels, attention heads or layers, leaving a smaller dense model that's faster everywhere.
The lab cuts each hidden layer of the teacher from 1024 to 256 units by the norm of their outgoing weights: 0.427 before fine-tuning, 0.857
after five epochs, against 0.861 for the same architecture trained from scratch. That's the honest comparison for any pruning result:
**against a model of the same size trained normally**, not against the unpruned model. Often the pruned model's advantage is the head
start in training, which matters for large models (pruning and distilling a 15B model into an 8B one costs far less than pretraining an 8B
model from scratch: NVIDIA's Minitron results) and not much for small ones.

## Runtimes and compilers

The same model runs faster in software built for inference:

- **ONNX** is an interchange format: export the graph from PyTorch (`torch.onnx.export`) and run it in **ONNX Runtime**, which fuses
  operators, picks optimized kernels per hardware, supports quantization, and runs without Python. Often a 1.5-3x win on CPU for free,
  and a stable deployment artifact decoupled from the training code.
- **TensorRT** (NVIDIA GPUs) compiles a graph into fused, precision-tuned kernels for a specific GPU and input shapes; the fastest option
  on NVIDIA hardware, with the most build friction. **OpenVINO** does the same for Intel hardware; **Core ML** and **TFLite/LiteRT** for
  phones.
- **`torch.compile`** traces and fuses PyTorch code without leaving PyTorch.
- For LLMs, dedicated servers (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang, llama.cpp) combine all of this with batching and KV-cache management (45.2).

Every conversion step is a place for silent numerical differences. **Test the converted model against the original** on real inputs,
with a tolerance, in CI (50.1), and compare predictions by slice, not only the average.

## Latency, measured

One core, from the lab (your numbers will differ; the ratios are the point):

| variant | batch 1, µs | batch 256, µs per row |
|---|---|---|
| teacher, float32 | 243 | 14.7 |
| teacher, 95% unstructured sparsity | 244 | 14.6 |
| structured-pruned (256 units) | 23 | 1.4 |
| student (256 units) | 23 | 1.3 |
| teacher, int8 dynamic quantization | 77 | 7.4 |

The 13x smaller models are 10x faster at batch 1 and 11x at batch 256: a fixed cost per call keeps small models from getting
proportionally faster, and in a real server that fixed cost includes everything in 49.1. Int8 makes the big model 2 to 3 times faster.
The sparse teacher is exactly as fast as the dense one: zeros stored densely are multiplied like any other number. (Timings on a
shared machine drift by tens of percent from minute to minute; the lab measures all variants in turns, several rounds, and keeps each
one's best, so the drift hits them equally. Do the same when you compare.)

## The order to pull the levers

1. Profile the whole request, and fix what isn't the model.
2. Batch where you can (49.1), and use an inference runtime (ONNX Runtime, TensorRT, or a dedicated LLM server).
3. Quantize: bf16 or int8 first, 4-bit for LLMs under memory pressure. Validate accuracy by slice.
4. Distill into a smaller architecture when you need an order of magnitude, especially if you have unlabeled traffic for the teacher
   to label.
5. Structured pruning when distillation's training cost is the problem, or for large models.

## Things that will bite you

- **Optimizing the model** when the time goes elsewhere.
- **Unstructured sparsity** reported as a speedup without measuring one.
- **Pruning compared with the unpruned model** instead of a same-size baseline.
- **Calibration data** that doesn't look like production traffic.
- **Converted models not tested** against the original, or tested only on average accuracy.
- **Latency measured at the wrong batch size**, on the wrong hardware, or as a mean instead of percentiles.
- **A student that inherits the teacher's errors**, unmeasured by slice.

## Exercises

1. A 400 MB model must run on phones with 150 ms latency. List what you'd try, in order, and what you'd measure after each step.
2. Why can int8 quantization speed up a memory-bound workload even if the arithmetic is done in float?
3. In the lab, distillation with 50k labels didn't beat training on labels. Give two reasons it helps more on image or language tasks.
4. A paper reports "90% sparsity with no accuracy loss". What questions do you ask before expecting a speedup?
5. Your team exports a model to ONNX and the accuracy drops by 0.3 points. How do you find where?
6. You serve an LLM classifier at $0.004 per request and 2M requests a day. Sketch a plan to cut the cost by 10x, and what could go wrong.

1. Profile on a representative phone; convert to the mobile runtime (Core ML / LiteRT) and measure; quantize to int8 (static, calibrated
