# 00.3 How to study this without lying to yourself

*About 3 hours. No prerequisites.*

Here's an uncomfortable fact about learning: the study methods that *feel* most productive are, on average,
the least effective ones. Re-reading your notes feels great. Highlighting feels great. Watching a lecture at
1.5x while nodding feels fantastic. And a week later you remember almost nothing, because your brain confused
*familiarity* with *knowledge*. "Oh yes, I've seen this before" is not the same as "I can do this".

I spent a good part of my early career collecting courses and certificates the way some people collect stamps,
and I can tell you exactly how much of that stuck: the parts where I had to build something that broke. Everything
else evaporated the moment the exam was over. This lesson is about not repeating my mistake.

## What the research actually says

Learning science is one of the few areas of psychology with results that replicate well. The short version, from
Dunlosky et al.'s big 2013 review of study techniques:

- **Retrieval practice works.** Trying to recall something from memory (without looking) strengthens it much more
  than re-reading it. This is the "testing effect", and it's robust: in Roediger and Karpicke's classic 2006
  experiment, students who read a text once and then practiced recalling it retained far more a week later than
  students who read it multiple times, even though the re-readers felt more confident.
- **Spacing works.** The same total study time spread over days and weeks beats cramming, by a lot. Your brain
  consolidates between sessions. Cramming gets you through tomorrow's exam and nothing else.
- **Interleaving works.** Mixing different types of problems in one session (a probability problem, then a
  linear algebra one, then a coding one) beats doing twenty of the same type in a row. It feels worse. It's
  better, because it forces you to figure out *which* tool to use, which is the actual skill.
- **Re-reading and highlighting are weak.** Not useless, but the least efficient use of your time.

So the course is built around those three things. Every module starts with a self-check (retrieval). Every lesson
has exercises you should do *before* opening the answers (retrieval, again). Labs mix concepts from earlier
modules (interleaving). And later modules deliberately reuse earlier material (spacing).

But none of that works if you cheat. If you open the answers after five seconds of thinking, you skipped the part
that does the learning. The struggle is not an obstacle to learning. The struggle *is* the learning.

## How to work through a lesson

This is what I'd do, and roughly what I do when I learn something new:

1. **Skim the whole lesson first.** Headings, equations, code. Two minutes. You're building a map, not reading.
2. **Read it properly, with a pen.** When there's a derivation, close the lesson and try to reproduce it on paper.
   When you can't, look, then close it and try again. If you just read derivations, you'll be able to *follow*
   them and never *produce* them. Those are different skills, and only the second one is useful.
3. **Type the code yourself.** Not copy-paste. Typing forces you to read every line, and you'll make typos that
   teach you what the error messages mean. Then change something and predict what happens *before* you run it.
   Being wrong about your prediction is the most educational thing that can happen to you.
4. **Do the exercises before you look at the answers.** Give each one a real attempt, at least 20-30 minutes for
   the hard ones. If you're completely stuck, look at the answer, close it, and redo the exercise from scratch the
   next day.
5. **Run the lab.** Then break it on purpose: change a hyperparameter, remove a line, feed it garbage data. See
   which assertion fails. This is how you learn what each piece is *for*.
6. **Write a short summary from memory.** Five bullet points, no looking. Then check the lesson for what you
   missed. That gap is exactly what you need to review.

## Spacing, in practice

Keep a simple file (or an Anki deck, if you like flashcards) with one question per important idea. Not "what is
PCA?" but things that force you to produce something: "derive the first principal component as a variance
maximization problem", "why does `np.linalg.inv` lose precision on ill-conditioned matrices?", "what's the time
complexity of self-attention in sequence length, and why?".

Review a handful of old questions at the start of every study session. When you get one right three times over a
few weeks, drop it. When you get one wrong, it goes back to the front of the queue. That's all spaced repetition is.

## How to read a paper

From Part IV onward, I'll point you at original papers regularly. Nobody is born knowing how to read them, and
most people read them wrong: start to finish, stuck on the first equation, giving up on page 3.

Use the three-pass method (from S. Keshav's short and excellent note "How to Read a Paper"):

1. **First pass, 10 minutes.** Title, abstract, introduction, section headings, figures, conclusion. Skip the
   math. Goal: what problem, what's the claimed contribution, is it relevant to me?
2. **Second pass, an hour.** Read everything, but skip proofs and the details you don't get. Look hard at figures
   and tables: what exactly did they compare against, on what data? Write down what you don't understand.
3. **Third pass, several hours, only for papers that matter.** Re-derive the key results. Reimplement the core
   idea, ideally small. This is where you actually understand a paper, and where you discover that the method
   doesn't work quite as well as the abstract suggests. It happens more than you think.

And read papers with a pentester's eye. What did they *not* test? Which baseline is suspiciously weak? Would the
result survive a different random seed? Papers are arguments, not facts.

## Using AI assistants while you study

You'll use LLM assistants in your job every day, and you should. But while you're *learning*, they're a trap if
you use them wrong. If an assistant writes the code, your brain skips the step that builds the skill, and you
end up with a GitHub full of projects you can't explain in an interview. I've sat across the table from people
like that in study groups and hackathon teams, and watched them try to explain their own repository. It's painful for everyone.

My rules for this course:

- **Use it as a tutor, not as a ghostwriter.** "Explain why my gradient check fails" is good. "Write the backprop
  for me" is cheating yourself.
- **Ask it to quiz you.** "Ask me five questions about the bias-variance decomposition and don't give me the
  answers until I've tried." This is retrieval practice with infinite questions. It's genuinely great.
- **Verify everything.** Assistants are confidently wrong about math more often than you'd like, especially
  about signs, constants and edge cases. If it contradicts the lesson, check the primary source. Sometimes the
  lesson is wrong (tell me!), usually it's the assistant.
- **After Part VI, you'll know how these things work inside**, which makes you much better at using them. Until
  then, treat them like a very well-read intern: fast, helpful, and not to be trusted with anything important
  without review.

## When you're stuck

You'll be stuck a lot. Here's a protocol:

1. **Read the error message.** The whole thing. Out loud if necessary. A shocking fraction of "I'm stuck" is
   "I didn't read the traceback".
2. **Make the problem smaller.** Fewer data points, smaller model, fewer lines of code, until you have the
   smallest thing that still breaks. Most bugs become obvious at that point.
3. **Check your assumptions with prints or a debugger.** Shapes, dtypes, values. `print(x.shape, x.dtype,
   x.min(), x.max())` has solved more bugs than any fancy tool.
4. **Time-box it.** If you've made no progress in 45 minutes, look at the answer or ask someone. Then, and this
   is the important part, go back and figure out *why* you didn't find it yourself.
5. **Sleep on it.** I'm serious. A remarkable number of problems solve themselves in the shower the next morning.

## A realistic schedule

At around 15 hours a week, which is what most people with a job can sustain:

- **3 to 4 sessions of 2-3 hours during the week**, plus one longer session on the weekend for labs.
- **Start each session with 10 minutes of review** of old questions.
- **One module every one to three weeks**, depending on its size (the hours are in the syllabus).
- **Take a real week off every couple of months.** Burnout is not a badge of honor. It's a failure of planning.

Put the capstones in the calendar now, as dates. Deadlines you set yourself are weak, but they're better than
none.

## Build in public (a little)

Keep all your work in a Git repository. Write a short note when you finish a module: what you learned, what
confused you, what you built. After a year you'll have a portfolio with a real history and a pile of notes that
show how you think. That's worth more in an interview than any certificate. And I say that as someone who has
quite a few certificates.

> **Scar tissue.** I once spent a weekend "learning" transformers by reading three blog posts and watching two videos, and on Monday I
> explained attention to my study group with total confidence. Someone asked what the shape of the attention matrix was for a batch. I
> didn't know. I had recognized every diagram and could reproduce none of it. That's the fluency illusion, and I had it bad. Now I don't
> consider anything learned until I've written it from a blank file, and the first attempt is always humiliating. That's the point.
