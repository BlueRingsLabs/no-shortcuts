# 00.1 What the job titles actually mean

*About 2 hours. No prerequisites. No lab, just thinking.*

Job titles in this industry are a mess. I have seen a "Senior AI Engineer" whose whole job was writing SQL
reports, and a "Data Analyst" who was quietly running the company's entire recommendation system on a machine
under their desk. Recruiters copy-paste descriptions from each other, managers invent titles to justify budgets,
and every few years marketing renames the same work. "Big Data Engineer" became "Data Engineer", "Statistician"
became "Data Scientist", "ML Engineer" is sprouting a new sibling called "AI Engineer" and at some point someone
will announce "Agent Engineer" as a career path with a straight face. Probably already happened while I was
writing this sentence.

So before you invest two years into this course, let's agree on what the five roles actually *do* all day. Not
what the job ad says, but what the calendar looks like on a Tuesday.

## The pipeline, from left to right

It's easiest to see the roles as stations along the same pipeline:

```
 sources ──► ingestion ──► storage ──► transformation ──► analysis / features ──► models ──► serving ──► product
 (apps,       (batch,       (lake,      (SQL, Spark,       (stats, EDA,            (training,  (APIs,      (users,
  sensors,     streams,      warehouse)  dbt)               experiments)            tuning)     batch jobs, money,
  3rd party)   CDC)                                                                            agents)      complaints)

 |<──────── Data Engineer ────────>|
                         |<──────────── Data Scientist ────────────>|
                                                    |<──────── ML Engineer ────────>|
                                                            |<── DL Engineer ──>|
                                                                          |<──────── AI Engineer ────────>|
```

The overlaps are real. In a small company one person does all of it and is usually tired. In a big company each
station has its own team, its own Jira board and its own opinions about the other teams.

## Data Engineer

**What they own:** the systems that move data from where it's born to where it's useful. Ingestion from
databases, APIs and event streams. Storage in lakes and warehouses. Transformations that turn raw garbage into
clean, modeled tables. Orchestration so all of that runs on time. Data quality checks so it runs *correctly*.
Access control so it doesn't end up on a forum.

**A normal Tuesday:** a pipeline failed at 4 AM because an upstream team renamed a column without telling anyone.
You fix it, you add a schema check so it fails louder next time, and you send a polite message that is not
really polite. Then you spend the afternoon on a query that costs the company a few hundred dollars every time it
runs, and you make it cost ten.

**What they need most:** SQL (a lot of it), data modeling, one distributed engine (Spark, usually), an
orchestrator, cloud storage and IAM, software engineering discipline, and the patience of a saint.

**How you know you're good:** nobody notices your work. Data just arrives, correct, on time. Sadly, that also
means nobody notices *you* until something breaks. Welcome to infrastructure.

In this course: Part II carries most of it, plus the shared foundations in Part I and the infrastructure pieces in
Part VIII. Capstone A is your proof.

## Data Scientist

**What they own:** turning data into decisions. That can mean analysis ("why did churn go up in March?"),
experiments ("did the new checkout flow actually increase revenue, or was it Black Friday?"), statistical models
("what drives delivery delays?") or predictive models ("which customers will churn next month?").

**A normal Tuesday:** a product manager asks why a metric dropped. You spend three hours discovering that the
metric didn't drop, the tracking broke. Then you spend two hours explaining that to people who already made a
slide about it. Then, finally, an hour of actual modeling.

**What they need most:** statistics (the real kind, with confidence intervals and experiment design), classical
ML, SQL and pandas, visualization, and the ability to say "the data doesn't support that conclusion" to someone
more senior than you, without getting fired.

**The dirty secret:** the title has split. In some companies "Data Scientist" means analytics and experiments,
close to the business. In others it means someone who builds ML models and hands them to engineers. In a few, it
means research. Read the job description, not the title.

In this course: Parts I and III, plus the experimentation lessons in module 05 and the fairness lessons in
module 54. Capstone B is your proof.

## Machine Learning Engineer

**What they own:** getting models into production and keeping them alive there. Training pipelines, feature
pipelines, model serving, monitoring, retraining, rollbacks. The ML Engineer is the person who turns "it works in
my notebook" into "it has been answering 3,000 requests per second for six months and we know exactly how
good it is".

**A normal Tuesday:** a model's precision dropped five points over two weeks and nobody noticed until a customer
complained. You find that an upstream feature started arriving with a 24-hour delay, so the model has been
seeing stale data. You fix the pipeline, add a freshness monitor, write a post-mortem, and get asked why this
wasn't caught earlier by the same people who rejected your monitoring budget last quarter.

**What they need most:** strong software engineering, ML fundamentals deep enough to debug models (not just
deploy them), a deep learning framework, containers, CI/CD, cloud, and monitoring. It's a software engineer who
understands models, or a data scientist who learned to write production code. The good ones are both.

In this course: Parts I, III, IV and VIII. Capstone B is your proof, with Capstone D as a second one.

## Deep Learning Engineer

**What they own:** neural networks, specifically. Designing architectures, training them (often at scale, on
many GPUs), debugging why they don't converge, making them fast. In some companies this is a research engineer
role next to scientists; in others it's the team that fine-tunes and optimizes models for the product.

**A normal Tuesday:** your training run diverged at step 41,000 after two days on eight GPUs. The loss went to
`NaN` and so did your mood. You bisect: data, learning rate, a bad batch, a mixed precision overflow. It was the
data. It's usually the data. You add a check, restart from the last checkpoint and go get coffee while it
warms up again.

**What they need most:** the math (linear algebra, calculus, probability) at a level where papers are readable,
PyTorch internals, training dynamics, GPU performance, distributed training, and a debugging mindset that
borders on paranoia.

In this course: Parts I and IV, with Part V for language models. Capstone C is your proof.

## AI Engineer

This is the newest title, and the one with the most confusion around it, so let me be precise.

**What they own:** products built on top of foundation models. They mostly don't train models from scratch. They
take existing models (through APIs or self-hosted), and they build systems around them: prompts, retrieval (RAG),
tools, agents, evaluation, guardrails, caching, cost control. The model is a component, and the AI Engineer is
responsible for the system that component lives in.

**A normal Tuesday:** the support chatbot started recommending a competitor's product. You pull the traces, find
that a retrieved document from the public knowledge base contained a hidden instruction someone injected
through the product review form, and the model followed it. You fix the retrieval filter, add an injection
test to the eval suite, and write a very awkward incident report. After lunch, finance asks why the API bill
tripled; someone removed the cache during a refactor.

**What they need most:** solid software engineering, a real understanding of how LLMs work (so you can predict
how they'll fail instead of being surprised every time), retrieval and search, evaluation methodology, security,
and product sense.

**The trap:** because you can build a demo with an API key and a weekend, lots of people think this role
doesn't need fundamentals. It does. The demo is the easy 10%. The other 90% is evaluation, reliability, security
and cost, and all of that requires you to understand what's under the hood. The AI Engineers I'd hire are the
ones who could explain why the KV cache makes long prompts expensive, not the ones who memorized a framework's
API that will change next month anyway.

In this course: Parts V and VI, on top of everything before them. Capstone D is your proof.

## So which one should you pick?

You don't have to pick yet. That's the point of this course: the first half is the common core, and it's the
same for all five roles. By the time you reach Part V you'll know which parts made you happy and which parts
you did out of duty.

Some honest hints anyway:

- If you like building things that are reliable and you get satisfaction from a system that just works,
  **Data Engineer** or **ML Engineer**. These are also, in my experience, the roles with the most job openings
  per applicant, because they're less glamorous.
- If you like asking "why?" and you can live with ambiguity, **Data Scientist**.
- If you read papers for fun and debugging a training run sounds like a puzzle and not like a punishment,
  **Deep Learning Engineer**.
- If you like products, users, and moving fast, and you have a healthy paranoia about everything that can go
  wrong, **AI Engineer**.

And one more thing, from someone who has spent a lot of time breaking other people's systems: whatever you pick,
learn the security side of it. Every one of these five roles builds something that attackers want. Engineers who
know that are rare, and they're paid accordingly.

## Further reading

- Martin Kleppmann, *Designing Data-Intensive Applications*, O'Reilly, preface and chapter 1. The best
  description of the data systems world I know of.
- Joe Reis and Matt Housley, *Fundamentals of Data Engineering*, O'Reilly, chapter 1 on the data engineering
  lifecycle.
- Chip Huyen, *Designing Machine Learning Systems*, O'Reilly, chapter 1 on ML in production vs research.
- Chip Huyen, *AI Engineering*, O'Reilly, chapter 1 on what changes when you build on foundation models.
- D. Sculley et al., "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems", NeurIPS 2015. The famous diagram
  where the ML code is a tiny box in the middle of a huge system. Still true, still ignored.
