# 43.2 Tracing, monitoring and feedback loops

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 43.1, 40.1, 42.1. Lab: [`lab_43_2_tracing_monitoring.py`](labs/lab_43_2_tracing_monitoring.py).*

An eval suite (43.1) tells you how the system behaves on the cases you collected. Production tells you how it behaves on everything else,
if you're recording. LLM applications need the usual observability (logs, metrics, traces) plus things ordinary services don't: the full
prompt and response of each call, token counts and cost, the model version that actually answered, and some signal of whether the
answer was any good. Without these, the first question in every incident ("what did it actually say, and why?") has no answer.

The lab simulates a week of traffic to a course-QA assistant (retrieve, then generate) on the simulated API. On day 4 the provider
silently updates the model behind the alias being called: answers get longer, and somewhat worse. No deploy happened on our side.

## Traces

A **trace** records one request as a tree of **spans**: the request, and inside it retrieval, each model call, each tool call, each
retry. Each span has a start, an end, a status and attributes. The lab's minimal tracer produces:

```
request       2309 ms  ok  {'prompt_version': 'qa-v7'}
  retrieve      66 ms  ok  {'k': 5}
  generate    2243 ms  ok  {'model': 'medium@alias', 'input_tokens': 0, 'cached_input_tokens': 1804, 'output_tokens': 78}
```

Use OpenTelemetry in real systems. It has semantic conventions for generative-AI spans (model, token counts, operation), and most LLM
observability tools (Langfuse, Arize Phoenix, LangSmith, Honeycomb, Datadog) ingest it. What to record on each span:

- **Model and version** as reported by the API response, not the alias you requested.
- **Prompt template name and version**, so a change in behavior can be tied to a change in prompt.
- **Token counts**, including cached tokens (note the 0 non-cached input tokens above: prompt caching at work, 40.1), **cost**, and
  **latency** including time to first token.
- **Inputs and outputs**, full text, subject to your privacy rules: redact or tokenize personal data at capture (15.2, 54.2), restrict
  who can read traces, and set retention. Traces are among the most sensitive data you'll store, since they contain whatever users
  typed.
- **Retrieval results** (document IDs and scores), **tool calls and results**, **retries and errors**.
- **User and session IDs** (pseudonymized) and **feedback**, attached later.

## Metrics, from the traces

Aggregate traces into daily (or hourly) metrics:

| day | errors | p50 latency | p95 latency | output tokens | cost per 1k requests | quality (hidden) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1.2% | 3.02 s | 4.10 s | 106 | $2.12 | 0.80 |
| 3 | 0.5% | 3.08 s | 4.45 s | 111 | $2.20 | 0.78 |
| 4 | 1.0% | 4.77 s | 6.19 s | 178 | $3.18 | 0.73 |
| 6 | 1.7% | 4.87 s | 5.98 s | 179 | $3.17 | 0.70 |

A simple rule, "p95 latency above the baseline mean plus three standard deviations", fires on day 4 and stays on. Output length and cost
jumped by 60% and 45% the same day. That's a model change, and it's what pinning model versions prevents (40.1): with a dated model
identifier, this change would have been your decision, made after running your evals. The quality column is the catch. In real
life you can't see it, and nothing in latency, cost or errors shows it directly.

Monitor per request type (route, feature, customer tier), not only in aggregate, because a regression in a small category disappears in
the average. And alert on budgets: cost per day and per user, tokens per request, tool calls per request. A loop bug (42.1) shows up
in the bill first.

## Measuring quality in production

Three sources, each with a flaw:

**User feedback** (thumbs up and down, regenerations, edits, copy events). In the lab, 4.6% of requests get feedback, and unhappy users
give it five times more often:

| days | thumbs-up share | true share of good answers |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 | 0.40 | 0.79 |
| 4 to 6 | 0.23 | 0.71 |

The level is meaningless: 40% thumbs-up coexists with 79% good answers. The change points the right way, and with a few dozen events per
period it's noisy. Feedback is a way to **find** failures to look at, not a quality metric. Implicit signals (the user rephrased the
question, abandoned the session, copied the answer) are more plentiful and just as biased.

**Online evals**: run the code checks and a measured judge (43.1) on a random sample of production traffic, continuously. This is the
closest thing to a quality metric you'll have. Correct the judge's rates with its measured error rates, and re-measure the judge
periodically on human-labeled production samples.

**Human review** of sampled traces. The lab compares ways to pick 50 traces from days 4 to 6:

| sample | share that are failures |
|---|---|
| random | 34% |
| traces with a thumbs-down | 100% |
| the longest answers | 30% |

Targeted samples find failures fastest. Random samples estimate rates without bias. Do both: random for measurement, targeted for
discovery. (The longest answers weren't a useful target here, a reminder to check that a heuristic actually finds failures before
relying on it.)

## Closing the loop

Monitoring is useful only if it changes something. The loop that makes LLM products improve:

1. **Capture** traces with enough context to reproduce each request.
2. **Find** failures: alerts, feedback, targeted and random review, online evals.
3. **Diagnose**: read the trace. Was retrieval wrong, the prompt misread, a tool misused, the model changed?
4. **Add the case** to the eval suite (43.1), with what a good output must satisfy.
5. **Fix**, and verify on the whole suite that the fix didn't break other cases.
6. **Ship** behind a flag or canary (50.2), and watch the same metrics.

Teams that run this loop weekly improve steadily. Teams that don't are surprised by each incident.

## Things that will bite you

- **No record of the prompt and response** of each call.
- **Recording the requested alias** instead of the model version that answered.
- **Traces full of personal data** with no redaction, access control or retention limit.
- **Feedback rates used as the quality metric.**
- **Aggregate-only dashboards** that hide a broken category.
- **No cost or token alerts.**
- **Findings that never become eval cases.**

## Exercises

1. List the attributes you'd put on the span of a tool call in an agent.
2. Your cost per day doubled overnight, with no deploy. Name three causes and how the traces would distinguish them.
3. Why does the thumbs-up share move with quality even though its level is meaningless? When would its movement mislead you?
4. Design an online eval for a summarization feature: sampling rate, checks, judge, alerting.
5. Traces contain users' personal data. What controls do you put on the trace store?
6. A user reports a bad answer from yesterday. Walk through the loop above for that report.

  spans and traces come from.
- OpenTelemetry semantic conventions for generative AI (opentelemetry.io).
- Betsy Beyer et al. (eds.), *Site Reliability Engineering*, O'Reilly, 2016, chapter 6, "Monitoring Distributed Systems": the four
  golden signals, which still apply.
- Shreya Shankar et al., "Operationalizing Machine Learning: An Interview Study", 2022.

  it sends where.
