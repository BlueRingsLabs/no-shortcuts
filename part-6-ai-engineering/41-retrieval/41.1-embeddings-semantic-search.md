# 41.1 Embeddings and semantic search

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 34.2 (TF-IDF), 34.3 (word embeddings and anisotropy), 35.2 (a GPT), 30.2 (contrastive learning). Lab: [`lab_41_1_embeddings_search.py`](labs/lab_41_1_embeddings_search.py).*

A language model knows what it learned in training and nothing about your documents, your customers or last week. Retrieval is how you
give it the right context at the right time: find the few passages that answer the question and put them in the prompt. It sounds like a
solved problem, since search engines have existed for decades, and the embarrassing truth of this module is that it mostly is: a 1990s
algorithm is still the baseline to beat, and often wins. This lesson is about the two families of search, sparse and dense, measured
honestly on a real corpus, and about when each fails.

## A real test collection, for free

Retrieval can't be improved without being measured, and measuring needs queries with known answers. The lab gets them from this course.
The corpus is Parts 0 to V of this course (129 lessons), cut into about 2,000 chunks. Three query sets come with labels:

- **Exercises**: every lesson's exercise questions (672), which aren't in the searchable text. The answer is their lesson.
- **Cross-references**: sentences that point to another lesson, like "Streaming doesn't make the answer faster (37.2)", with the
  pointer removed (852). The answer is the lesson they point to: a concept described in another lesson's words.
- **Identifiers**: code names found in exactly one lesson (`nn.ModuleList`, `fit_transform`), 200 of them.

Metrics: **recall@5** (is the right lesson among the top five?) and **MRR@10** (the reciprocal rank of the right lesson, averaged). 41.4
covers metrics properly.

Build this kind of set for your own corpus before choosing anything: questions from support tickets or search logs, labeled with the
document that answered them. A few hundred are enough to see differences of a few points.

## Sparse retrieval: BM25

**BM25** (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) scores a document by the query terms it contains, weighted by how rare each term is in the corpus
(inverse document frequency) and by a saturating function of how often it occurs, normalized for document length:

$$\text{score}(q, d) = \sum_{t \in q} \text{idf}(t)\,\frac{\text{tf}(t, d)\,(k_1 + 1)}{\text{tf}(t, d) + k_1\big(1 - b + b\,|d|/\overline{|d|}\big)}$$

with $k_1 \approx 1.2$ and $b \approx 0.75$. The lab implements it as a sparse matrix in 30 lines; building the index takes 0.2 seconds.
Tokenization is the real design decision: here, lowercase words with a crude plural stemmer, plus code identifiers kept whole.

## Dense retrieval: embeddings

Dense retrieval represents query and document as vectors and ranks by cosine similarity. Neighbors in the vector space should mean
neighbors in meaning, so a query can match a document that shares no words with it. The lab tries three ways to get such vectors:

- **LSA** (Deerwester et al., 1990): compress the TF-IDF matrix to 256 dimensions with a truncated SVD. The original "embeddings" for
  search.
- **A language model's hidden states**, averaged over the text: the course's tiny GPT (35.2), untouched.
- **The same model trained contrastively**: pairs of random crops from the same passage are positives, every other passage in the batch
  is a negative (InfoNCE, as in CLIP, 30.2; the crop recipe is Contriever's, Izacard et al., 2022). Trained on Parts I to III only.

## Results

| system | exercises | cross-references | identifiers |
|---|---|---|---|
| BM25 | 0.930 | 0.792 | 0.985 |
| TF-IDF cosine | 0.943 | 0.824 | 0.985 |
| LSA, 256 dimensions | 0.924 | 0.809 | 0.920 |
| LM hidden states, untrained | 0.269 | 0.209 | 0.265 |
| LM, contrastively trained | 0.482 | 0.407 | 0.215 |
| hybrid: BM25 + trained LM (RRF) | 0.783 | 0.695 | 0.735 |
| hybrid: BM25 + LSA (RRF) | 0.927 | 0.810 | 0.955 |

(recall@5; the lab also prints MRR.)

Four lessons, in order of importance.

**1. A language model is not an embedding model.** The untrained model's averaged hidden states are nearly useless (0.27). The mean cosine
similarity between two random chunks is 0.66: all vectors point in roughly the same direction (anisotropy, 34.3), so everything looks
similar to everything. Centering removes the shared direction (mean cosine 0.003) but not the problem: nothing trained these vectors to put
related texts close together.

**2. Training for retrieval works, and it needs data at a scale this lab doesn't have.** Contrastive training brings the random-pair cosine
down to 0.14 and raises recall to 0.48. But look where it works: 0.58 on exercises whose answers are in Parts I to III, the text it trained
on, and 0.35 on Parts IV and V, which it never saw. A 1-million-parameter model trained on a million characters learns the vocabulary of its
training text, not the meaning of language. The embedding models you'd actually use are trained on hundreds of millions to billions of
pairs, which is why they transfer. Use one of those (and check the MTEB leaderboard for your language and domain, then test on your own
queries, since leaderboard rankings don't transfer reliably). Train or fine-tune your own only with a lot of in-domain pairs.

**3. The baselines are strong.** On questions written by the same author, in the same vocabulary as the documents, BM25 and TF-IDF are hard
to beat, and LSA, a linear method from 1990, is close behind. This matches the BEIR benchmark's finding (Thakur et al., 2021) that BM25 is a
robust zero-shot baseline that many neural retrievers fail to beat outside their training domain. Measure the baseline before buying
anything.

**4. Exact strings need exact matching.** On identifier queries, BM25 finds 98.5%; the trained encoder 22%. It misses 157 of the 200 that BM25
finds: `np.nan`, `duration_ms`, `fit_transform`, `max_iter`. An identifier is a rare string that means exactly itself, and an embedding
squeezes it through subword tokens into a vector about "what this is about". Product codes, error IDs, SKUs, function names and people's
names need lexical search. That's the answer to this module's self-check question.

## Hybrid search

Combine sparse and dense retrieval, and fuse their rankings. **Reciprocal rank fusion** (Cormack et al., 2009) is the simple, robust
default: each system contributes $1/(60 + \text{rank})$ for each document, and documents are sorted by the sum. No score calibration needed.

Fusion helps when both systems are good and fail on different queries: BM25 + LSA roughly matches BM25 on exercises (0.927 against
0.930) and edges it out on cross-references (0.810 against 0.792). Fusion with a much weaker system hurts: BM25 + the tiny trained encoder drops to
0.783, because RRF gives the weak system's top results as much say as the strong one's. In production, with a good embedding model, hybrid
search is usually the best default; check that on your queries, and weight the systems (or rerank, 41.3) rather than assuming.

## Things that will bite you

- **Using an LLM's hidden states as embeddings**, or any embedding model without measuring it on your queries.
- **Leaderboard-driven model choice** with no in-domain evaluation.
- **Dense-only search** for queries with IDs, codes and names.
- **Fusing a weak retriever** with a strong one and assuming the combination is better.
- **Mixing embeddings from different models** (or model versions) in one index (41.2).
- **Tokenization** for BM25 that splits identifiers or ignores the language's morphology.

## Exercises

1. Compute BM25's idf for a term that appears in 10 of 10,000 documents, and in 5,000 of them. What does the saturation in tf buy?
2. Why do average-pooled hidden states of a language model make poor embeddings, and why doesn't centering fix it?
3. The trained encoder scores 0.58 on Parts I to III and 0.35 on Parts IV and V. What does that tell you, and what would you change?
4. Design a retrieval evaluation set for an internal IT helpdesk assistant.
5. When does reciprocal rank fusion hurt, and what can you do instead?
6. A user searches "error E1047 after upgrade". Walk through what BM25 and a dense retriever each do with it.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. With $\text{idf} = \ln(1 + (N - n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5))$: for $n = 10$, $\ln(1 + 9990.5/10.5) \approx 6.86$; for $n = 5{,}000$,
   $\ln(1 + 5000.5/5000.5) = \ln 2 \approx 0.69$. Rare terms count about ten times more. Saturation means the 20th occurrence of a word adds
   almost nothing, so documents can't win by repeating a term, and long documents are normalized by $b$.

2. The model was trained to predict the next token, not to place similar texts close together. Its hidden states share a large common
   component (anisotropy), so cosine similarities are all high and dominated by it; and averaging across positions mixes information in
   ways nothing optimized for similarity. Centering removes the common component, but the remaining directions still aren't organized by
   meaning, since no objective asked them to be.

3. It learned mostly the vocabulary and topics of its training text: it generalizes poorly to unseen text, a domain gap. Options: a
   pretrained embedding model trained on large, diverse data; fine-tuning on in-domain pairs from the target corpus (unsupervised crops of
   the target documents, or synthetic questions generated per passage); a larger base model.

4. Collect 300 to 500 real questions from past tickets and chat logs, spanning categories (accounts, VPN, hardware, software) and including
   error codes and product names. For each, have IT staff label the knowledge-base article (or passages) that answer it, and mark
   questions with no answer in the KB. Keep a held-out part. Add every retrieval failure seen in production.

5. When one system is much weaker, or when both fail on the same queries: RRF gives the weak system's top documents real weight. Options:
   weighted fusion (tuned on a validation set), a learned reranker over the union of candidates (41.3), routing by query type (IDs and
   codes to BM25), or using only the strong system.

6. BM25: "e1047" is a rare token with very high idf, so documents containing it rank at the top, and "upgrade" helps order them; if no
   document contains it, results are driven by "error" and "upgrade", which are generic. Dense: the embedding captures "an error after an
   upgrade" and returns documents about upgrade errors in general, most likely not the one about E1047, since the code has little
   representation in the vector. Hybrid search gets the best of both.
