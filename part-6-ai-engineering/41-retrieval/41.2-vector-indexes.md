# 41.2 Vector indexes: HNSW, IVF, PQ

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 41.1, 21.1 (k-means), 19.1 (nearest neighbors and the curse of dimensionality). Lab: [`lab_41_2_vector_indexes.py`](labs/lab_41_2_vector_indexes.py).*

Exact nearest-neighbor search is a matrix product and a sort. For a million 768-dimensional vectors, that's 768 million
multiply-adds per query: fine for a batch job, too slow for a search box with a hundred queries a second, and hopeless at a billion
vectors. Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) indexes trade a little recall for orders of magnitude less work. Every vector database is
one or two of the three ideas in this lesson, plus storage, filtering and an API. Knowing the ideas is how you set their parameters
without guessing, and how you tell a vendor's benchmark from your workload.

The lab implements all three from scratch on 20,000 clustered 64-dimensional unit vectors, with 300 queries. Cost is counted in
distance computations rather than seconds, because pure Python's overhead would distort the timing; in a C++ library, time is roughly
proportional to the count.

## Recall is the metric

An ANN index is judged by **recall@k**: the fraction of the true $k$ nearest neighbors (by exact search) it returns. Always measure
it on your own vectors and queries, at the parameters you'll run in production, and look at the curve of recall against cost rather than
at one point. For retrieval-augmented generation, remember what recall is measured against: the index's recall is relative to exact
search in the embedding space, which is itself an imperfect proxy for relevance (41.1). A 0.95-recall index on a mediocre embedding
model loses less than switching to a better embedding model gains.

## IVF: partition, then probe

The **inverted file index** runs k-means on the vectors (21.1) and assigns each vector to its nearest centroid's list. A query compares
itself with the centroids, then searches exhaustively in the `nprobe` closest cells.

| nprobe | recall@10 | distances per query |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.804 | 297 |
| 4 | 0.909 | 778 |
| 16 | 0.972 | 2,636 |
| 32 | 0.991 | 5,152 |

One cell finds 80% of the neighbors; the rest sit on the other side of a cell border. Each doubling of `nprobe` buys a few points of
recall for roughly twice the work. Rules of thumb: `nlist` around $\sqrt{N}$ to $16\sqrt{N}$, training on a representative sample, and
retraining when the data distribution drifts, because cells trained on last year's vectors get unbalanced.

## PQ: compress the vectors

**Product quantization** (Jégou et al., 2011) splits each vector into $m$ sub-vectors and replaces each by the ID of its nearest
centroid among 256 learned for that subspace. A 64-dimensional float32 vector (256 bytes) becomes 8 bytes. To search, the query
computes a table of its distances to the 256 centroids of each subspace, and the distance to any stored vector is then a sum of $m$ table
lookups, with no floating-point vector ever decompressed.

| method | recall@10 |
|---|---|
| PQ codes only (8 bytes per vector) | 0.730 |
| PQ, rerank the top 100 with the original vectors | 0.999 |

The compressed distances are good enough to shortlist and not good enough to rank. So production systems combine the ideas: IVF to
choose the cells, PQ codes in each cell (IVF-PQ), and exact reranking of a shortlist using full vectors kept on disk or in cheaper
memory. That's how billion-scale search fits on one machine. (Scalar quantization to int8, and binary quantization with reranking, are
the simpler cousins, 37.3.)

## HNSW: a graph you can walk

**Hierarchical navigable small world** graphs (Malkov and Yashunin, 2018) link every vector to about $M$ of its near neighbors. A
random few also get layers above, each sparser than the last, like express lanes. A search enters at the top, walks greedily toward
the query, drops a layer when it can't get closer, and at the bottom layer runs a beam search that keeps the `ef` best candidates.

| ef | recall@10 | distances per query |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.719 | 181 |
| 40 | 0.932 | 314 |
| 80 | 0.988 | 569 |
| 160 | 0.999 | 1,120 |

For 95% recall, exact search computes 20,000 distances per query, IVF 2,636 and HNSW 569. That's why HNSW is the default in most vector
databases and libraries. What it costs:

- **Memory**: the links. With $M = 16$ the bottom layer averages 26 links per node, about 100 bytes per vector on top of the vector
  itself, and HNSW normally keeps full vectors in RAM.
- **Build time**: each insertion is a search. The lab's pure-Python build of 20,000 vectors takes about 17 seconds; production libraries
  build millions of vectors per minute across cores, and that still dominates re-indexing time.
- **Deletes and updates**: graphs handle them badly (tombstones, then periodic rebuilds). A corpus that churns needs a plan for this.

The parameters: `M` (links per node: more is higher recall and more memory, 16 to 64 is typical), `ef_construction` (build-time beam
width: higher builds a better graph, slowly) and `ef_search` (the query-time knob you tune for the recall you need).

## Why any of this works

The lab builds the same HNSW on 5,000 uniformly random 64-dimensional vectors. At `ef=40`, recall drops from 0.988 on the clustered data to
0.820. In high-dimensional random data every point is about equally far from every other (19.1): the "nearest" neighbors are barely
nearer than the rest, and graphs and cells have little structure to exploit. Real embeddings have low intrinsic dimension: they lie
near a curved surface of far fewer dimensions than their coordinates. That's what makes ANN indexes work, and also why an index that
benchmarks well on one embedding model can behave differently on another.

## Filters

Real queries come with conditions: this tenant, this language, documents newer than a date, documents this user may see. The naive
approach searches first and filters afterwards. In the lab, with a filter that keeps 1% of the vectors:

| method | results returned (of 10) | recall@10 |
|---|---|---|
| HNSW top 10, then filter | 0.1 | 0.010 |
| HNSW top 200, then filter | 2.2 | 0.222 |

Post-filtering collapses with selective filters. What works: **pre-filter and search exactly** when the allowed set is small (200 vectors
are nothing to scan), **filter during traversal** (most vector databases support this, with varying quality at low selectivity), or
**partition the index** by the attribute, such as one index or namespace per tenant. The last is also the safest for multi-tenant access
control: a query physically can't reach another tenant's vectors. Never enforce access control by asking the model to ignore documents
it shouldn't have seen (44.1).

## Choosing

- **Under about a million vectors**: exact search on a GPU or with a good BLAS is often fast enough, simpler and has perfect recall.
  Measure before adding an index.
- **Millions, fits in RAM, changes slowly**: HNSW.
- **Hundreds of millions to billions, or memory-bound**: IVF-PQ (or a disk-based graph like DiskANN), with reranking.
- **Already running PostgreSQL**: pgvector's HNSW or IVF indexes are good enough for many products, and keep your vectors next to the
  rest of your data, with the same transactions and backups (08.4). A separate vector database earns its place with scale or features.

## Things that will bite you

- **Not measuring recall** on your own data at your production parameters.
- **Post-filtering** with selective filters.
- **Access control implemented as a filter someone can forget**, instead of partitioning.
- **Changing the embedding model without re-indexing everything**: vectors from two models are not comparable, even at the same
  dimension.
- **Unnormalized vectors** with an inner-product index when you meant cosine.
- **IVF centroids trained once** on data that has since drifted.

## Exercises

   with $M = 32$.
2. Why does the lab's IVF need `nprobe > 1` to reach high recall, even though every vector lives in exactly one cell?
3. PQ-only search reaches 0.73 recall, but PQ with reranking of the top 100 reaches 0.999. What does this say about where PQ's

4. A multi-tenant RAG product stores all tenants in one HNSW index and post-filters by tenant ID. List the problems.
5. You upgrade the embedding model. What has to happen to the index, and how do you roll it out without downtime?
6. Your vector database's benchmark shows 0.99 recall at 2 ms. On your data you get 0.85. Name three possible reasons.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. float32: $50 \times 10^6 \times 768 \times 4 = 154$ GB. int8: 38 GB. PQ with 96 one-byte codes: 4.8 GB, plus codebooks (negligible).
   HNSW links: the bottom layer has up to $2M = 64$ links of 4 bytes, about 256 bytes per vector on average (the lab measured 26 links
   with $M = 16$), so roughly 10 to 13 GB more, and HNSW usually needs the float vectors in RAM too.

2. A query's nearest neighbors are near the query, and a query close to a cell border has neighbors on both sides. Cells are Voronoi
   regions of the centroids, not clusters of mutually near points, so near neighbors are often split across cells.

3. PQ's distance errors are small relative to the gaps between the near and far vectors, but large relative to the gaps among the
   near ones. So the true neighbors nearly always make the top 100, while their exact order is wrong. It's good for candidate
   generation and bad for final ranking.

4. Selective tenants get few or no results (post-filtering collapse), recall depends on the tenant's size, a bug in the filter leaks
   other tenants' documents, one tenant's data changes the graph everyone searches, and deleting a tenant's data from a graph is
   awkward. Partition by tenant (namespaces or separate indexes), or at least use in-traversal filtering with the tenant filter
   applied by the system.

5. All documents must be re-embedded with the new model and a new index built; vectors from different models aren't comparable.
   Build the new index alongside the old (dual-write new documents to both), evaluate retrieval on both with your test queries (41.4),
   switch queries over when the new one is ready and better, and keep the old one until you're sure. Store the model version with
   every vector.

6. Different data: benchmark datasets (SIFT, GloVe) have different dimension, distribution and intrinsic dimension from yours.
   Different parameters: the benchmark's `ef`, `nprobe` or quantization differ from your defaults. Filters, which the benchmark didn't
   have. Queries from a different distribution than the documents (question-shaped queries against passage vectors). Or an index built
   before most of your data arrived, with IVF centroids trained on too little or outdated data.
