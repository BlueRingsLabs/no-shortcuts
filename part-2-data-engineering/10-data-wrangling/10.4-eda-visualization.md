# 10.4 Exploratory analysis and visualization that says something

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 10.1 to 10.3, Module 04, 05.1. Lab: [`lab_10_4_eda.py`](labs/lab_10_4_eda.py).*

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small datasets with the same means, the same variances, the same
correlation and the same regression line, to two or three decimals. Plot them and they're completely different: a noisy
line, a perfect curve, a perfect line with one outlier, and a vertical stack with one point far away. His point was that
summary statistics can hide everything that matters, and you have to look. Fifty years later, people still train models
on datasets they've never plotted. Then they're surprised.

This lesson is about two related skills. **Exploratory data analysis** (EDA) is how you learn what's in the data before you
trust it or model it. **Explanatory visualization** is how you communicate a finding to people who weren't in the room,
which in most jobs is the part that decides whether your work has any effect at all. The first is for you; the second is
for them; they need different charts.

## EDA is a conversation with the data

Good EDA starts with questions, not with "let me plot everything":

- What does one row represent? (The grain, again, 09.2.)
- What's the time range? Are there gaps or sudden changes in volume?
- For the target (if there is one): distribution, base rate, how it changes over time.
- For each important feature: distribution, missingness, weird values (10.3), relationship with the target.
- What surprised me? Every surprise is either a data bug or an insight. Find out which.

And keep a log of what you looked at and what you concluded, because EDA findings are the raw material of your modeling decisions and of the "data" section
of your design doc (47.1).

A few techniques I use on every new dataset:

- **Distributions**: histograms (log x-axis for skewed positives like revenue and latency, 04.1), ECDFs (the empirical CDF; no binning decisions, and you
  can read medians and percentiles directly), and box or violin plots to compare groups.
- **Relationships**: scatter plots (with transparency or hexbin density for large n, otherwise you're looking at a black blob), binned means ("average target
  per decile of feature X", which is much more readable than a scatter of a binary target), correlation matrices, but remember 04.3: correlation only sees
  linear relationships.
- **Time**: plot counts and key metrics over time at daily resolution. Nearly every dataset I've ever received had at least one visible break in a daily
  plot: a tracking change, a migration, an outage, a bot.
- **Segments**: the same metric split by the main categorical variables (country, channel, device). Overall averages hide Simpson's paradox (04.2).

## Summary statistics lie by omission

The lab reproduces Anscombe's quartet and its modern cousin, the "Datasaurus Dozen" (Matejka and Fitzmaurice, 2017): twelve datasets, including a dinosaur,
with identical means, standard deviations and correlations. Nobody would fit the same model to a dinosaur and a line, but the summary statistics say they're
the same. Plot first.

## Principles of honest charts

Most bad charts I see aren't malicious. They're lazy defaults. The principles:

1. **One chart, one message.** Know what you want the reader to conclude before you draw it, and put that conclusion in the title: "Mobile conversion fell
   30% after the March release", not "Conversion by device".
2. **Choose the chart for the comparison**:
   - comparing categories: horizontal bar chart, sorted by value;
   - change over time: line chart;
   - distribution: histogram, ECDF, box plot;
   - relationship between two numeric variables: scatter;
   - part-to-whole: stacked bar (or, for two or three parts, just say the numbers).
3. **Axes tell the truth.** Bar charts start at zero (the bar's length *is* the value). Line charts can zoom in, but label it clearly. Use log scales for
   data spanning orders of magnitude, and say so on the axis.
4. **Show uncertainty.** Error bars or confidence bands (05.1). A line going up by 2% with a 5% confidence interval is not going up.
5. **Maximize data, minimize ink** (Tufte's data-ink ratio): no 3D, no shadows, no gratuitous gridlines, no legend when direct labels work.
6. **Color with purpose.** One highlight color for what matters and grey for context. Sequential palettes for ordered values, diverging for above/below a
   midpoint, qualitative for categories. Check that it works for color-blind readers (about 1 in 12 men): don't encode meaning in red vs green alone.
7. **Small multiples** (the same chart repeated per segment, with shared axes) beat one chart with twelve overlapping lines.

The classic crimes: pie charts with many slices (humans are bad at comparing angles), dual y-axes (you can make any two series look correlated by choosing the
scales), truncated bar axes, cumulative charts presented as growth (a cumulative line always goes up), and averages of skewed data without the median.

## Matplotlib, the object-oriented way

Every plotting library in Python sits on or next to Matplotlib, so it's worth knowing its core API. Use the object-oriented interface (explicit figures and
axes), not the stateful `plt.plot` style, which gets confusing as soon as you have more than one plot:

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4), sharey=False, constrained_layout=True)
ax = axes[0]
ax.hist(revenue, bins=np.logspace(0, 4, 40), color="0.6")
ax.set_xscale("log")
ax.set_xlabel("Revenue per order (EUR, log scale)")
ax.set_ylabel("Orders")
ax.axvline(np.median(revenue), color="C3")
ax.annotate(f"median {np.median(revenue):.0f}", xy=(np.median(revenue), 0), xytext=(5, 150), textcoords="offset points")
ax.set_title("Most orders are small; a long tail drives the mean")
fig.savefig("revenue.png", dpi=150)
```

For quick statistical EDA, `seaborn` is a convenient layer on top; for interactive dashboards, Plotly or a BI tool. For reports, static PNG/SVG from code,
versioned with the analysis, so anyone can regenerate them.

## From exploration to explanation

The chart you used to discover something is rarely the chart you should show. When you present:

- **Lead with the answer.** Executives read the title and maybe the first sentence. Put the conclusion there, then the evidence.
- **Give scale and context**: absolute numbers next to percentages ("a 40% increase, from 5 to 7 tickets per day"), comparison to a baseline, and the time range.
- **Say what you're not sure about.** Confidence intervals, data caveats, what would change your mind. It makes you more credible, not less.
- **Make it reproducible.** The notebook or script that produced the figure goes in the repository next to the report. "I don't remember how I made that chart"
  is how wrong numbers become folklore.

## Things that will bite you

- **Trusting summary statistics without plotting.**
- **Overplotting**: 100,000 points in a scatter look like a solid shape. Use alpha, hexbin, or sample.
- **Averages of skewed data** presented alone.
- **Default colors and chart types** chosen by the tool, not by the message.
- **Misleading axes**, intentional or not.
- **Discovering "insights" that are data bugs** (a spike that's a bot, a drop that's a tracking outage). Check with the data owners before presenting.

## Further reading

- John W. Tukey, *Exploratory Data Analysis*, Addison-Wesley, 1977. Where the term comes from.
- Edward R. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, 2nd edition, Graphics Press, 2001.
- Claus O. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, O'Reilly, 2019 (free online). The most practical modern guide.
- F. J. Anscombe, "Graphs in Statistical Analysis", *The American Statistician*, 1973; and Justin Matejka and George Fitzmaurice, "Same Stats, Different
  Graphs", CHI 2017.
- Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, *Storytelling with Data*, Wiley, 2015. For the explanatory side.
