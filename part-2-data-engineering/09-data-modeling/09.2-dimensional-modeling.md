# 09.2 Dimensional modeling: facts, dimensions and SCDs

*About 11 hours. Prerequisites: 09.1, 08.2. Lab: [`lab_09_2_dimensional.py`](labs/lab_09_2_dimensional.py).*

Normalized schemas are built so that writes can't corrupt data. Analytical schemas are built so that people can ask
questions without a PhD in your company's join graph. Ralph Kimball's **dimensional modeling**, from the 1990s, is still
the most common answer to "how do we structure the warehouse", and it has quietly survived every hype cycle since, including
"just put everything in one giant table". It's also a thing interviewers for Data Engineer roles ask about constantly:
star schemas, grain, slowly changing dimensions. Let's make sure you can answer from understanding, not memory.

## Facts and dimensions

Dimensional modeling splits the world into two kinds of tables:

- **Fact tables** record **measurements of business events**: a sale, a click, a shipment, a payment. Each row is one event (or one snapshot) at a
  declared **grain**, with numeric **measures** (quantity, amount, duration) and foreign keys to dimensions. They're long and narrow, and they grow
  forever.
- **Dimension tables** hold the **context** of those events: who, what, where, when, how. Customers, products, stores, dates, channels. They're wide
  (lots of descriptive columns for filtering and grouping) and comparatively short.

Put the fact in the middle and the dimensions around it, and you get the **star schema**:

```
             dim_date
                |
 dim_customer -- fact_sales -- dim_product
                |
             dim_channel
```

Queries follow one pattern: filter and group by dimension attributes, aggregate fact measures.

```sql
SELECT d.year_month, p.category, SUM(f.amount) AS revenue
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_date d     ON d.date_key = f.date_key
JOIN dim_product p  ON p.product_key = f.product_key
WHERE d.year = 2025 AND p.category IN ('gpus', 'laptops')
GROUP BY ALL;
```

Dimensions are deliberately **denormalized**: `dim_product` contains category, subcategory, brand and department in one table (instead of a normalized chain
product → subcategory → category). Fewer joins, easier for humans and BI tools, and the redundancy costs little because dimensions are small. (Normalizing
dimensions gives a **snowflake schema**; there's rarely a good reason to do it in a modern columnar warehouse.)

## The four-step design process

Kimball's recipe, which I still use:

1. **Pick the business process**: order fulfilment, web browsing, support tickets. Not a department, not a report.
2. **Declare the grain**: exactly what one fact row represents. "One row per order line." "One row per customer per day." This is the most important
   decision in the whole model, and you make it *before* choosing columns. Every fan-out bug from 08.2 is, at root, confusion about grain. The finest grain
   available (the atomic event) is usually the right choice: you can always aggregate up, never down.
3. **Identify the dimensions**: the descriptive context true at that grain.
4. **Identify the facts** (measures): numbers true at that grain.

## Kinds of fact tables

- **Transaction facts**: one row per event (a sale line, a click). The most common.
- **Periodic snapshot facts**: one row per entity per period (account balance per customer per day, inventory per product per week). Needed when the
  thing you measure is a *level*, not an event.
- **Accumulating snapshot facts**: one row per process instance, updated as it moves through milestones (order placed, paid, shipped, delivered, with a
  timestamp per milestone and durations between them). Great for pipeline and funnel analysis.
- **Factless facts**: events with no measures, just dimension keys (a student attended a class, a promotion was active for a product on a day). You count
  them.

### Additivity

Measures differ in how they can be summed:

- **Additive**: can be summed across all dimensions (revenue, quantity).
- **Semi-additive**: can be summed across some dimensions but not time. An account **balance** summed across customers is fine; summed across days is
  nonsense (the balance on Monday plus the balance on Tuesday isn't anything). Use the last value in the period, or the average.
- **Non-additive**: ratios and percentages (margin %, conversion rate). Never sum them, and never average them naively across groups of different size (you
  know Simpson from 04.2). Store the numerator and denominator as additive facts and compute the ratio at query time.

## Dimensions in detail

**Surrogate keys.** Dimensions get their own integer surrogate key (`customer_key`), separate from the source system's ID (`customer_id`, now called the
**natural** or **business key**). Why? Because of history (next section), because several source systems may disagree on IDs, and because it lets you
represent "unknown" or "not applicable" members with special rows (key -1: "Unknown customer") instead of NULL foreign keys that silently drop rows in
inner joins.

**The date dimension.** One row per calendar day, with every attribute anyone might group by: year, quarter, month, week, day of week, is_weekend,
is_holiday (per country!), fiscal year, fiscal period. It's generated once, not loaded from anywhere, and it saves every analyst from writing (and getting
wrong) date logic in every query. The same idea applies to time of day if you need it.

**Degenerate dimensions**: identifiers with no other attributes (an order number on a sales line), kept directly in the fact table.

**Junk dimensions**: a bag of low-cardinality flags (is_gift, payment_type, has_coupon) combined into one small dimension instead of cluttering the fact
table.

**Conformed dimensions**: the same `dim_customer` and `dim_date` shared by every fact table in the warehouse. This is what lets you combine processes
("revenue per customer" from sales and "tickets per customer" from support) and have them agree on who a customer is. Without conformed dimensions, every
team builds its own `customer` table, and the numbers in different dashboards never match. Kimball called the matrix of processes × conformed dimensions the
**bus matrix**, and it's still a great planning tool.

## Slowly changing dimensions

Dimension attributes change: a customer moves country, a product changes category, a sales rep changes region. How the dimension handles change decides
what historical reports mean. The standard types:

- **Type 0: retain original.** Never change (date of birth, original signup channel).
- **Type 1: overwrite.** Update the row in place. Simple. History is lost: last year's sales now appear under the customer's *current* country. Correct for
  fixing typos, wrong for real changes you want to analyze.
- **Type 2: add a new row.** Close the current row (set `valid_to` and `is_current = false`) and insert a new row with a new surrogate key, the new
  values, and `valid_from = now`. Facts recorded while the old row was current keep pointing to the old surrogate key, so **history is preserved exactly
  as it was**. This is the one you'll implement most often, and it's what interviewers mean by "SCD".
- **Type 3: add a column.** Keep `previous_country` next to `country`. Only one level of history; rarely enough.
- **Type 6** (1 + 2 + 3): Type 2 rows plus a column with the *current* value on every row, so you can report by historical or current value from the same
  table.

A Type 2 customer dimension looks like this:

| customer_key | customer_id | country | valid_from | valid_to | is_current |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 101 | 7 | ES | 2024-01-10 | 2025-03-01 | false |
| 877 | 7 | PL | 2025-03-01 | 9999-12-31 | true |

When loading facts, you look up the surrogate key valid **at the event time** (a "point-in-time" or "as-of" join): an order from February 2025 gets key 101,
one from April gets 877. Then "revenue by country" attributes each sale to the country the customer was in when they bought. The lab implements the whole
Type 2 merge and the as-of join. It also shows a bug I've seen in production: overlapping validity intervals after a pipeline ran twice, which silently
duplicates facts through the join.

The same "as-of" logic appears in ML as **point-in-time correctness** for features: when you build training data, each example must see feature values as
they were at prediction time, not as they are today. Otherwise you leak the future into training (18.4, 22.1). Feature stores implement exactly this join.

## Beyond Kimball

Modern practice mixes approaches:

- **One big table (OBT)**: fully denormalize the fact and its dimensions into one wide table. With columnar storage (reading only needed columns) and cheap
  storage, it can be fast and convenient for specific dashboards or ML training sets. The costs: dimension changes require rewriting the huge table, and
  different OBTs drift apart. A good pattern: model a star schema as the source of truth, and build OBTs *from* it for specific consumers.
- **Data Vault**: hubs (business keys), links (relationships) and satellites (attributes with history), designed for auditability and for integrating many
  sources. Heavier; common in regulated enterprises as an integration layer beneath dimensional marts.
- **Semantic layers / metrics layers**: define "revenue", "active customer" and "churn" once, in code, and have every dashboard and notebook use that
  definition. The same problem conformed dimensions solve, extended to metrics. It's the cure for "why do these two dashboards show different revenue?",
  which is the question data teams hear most often in their lives.

> **Scar tissue.** A CTF platform some friends and I ran for a season stored each player's university as a plain attribute, overwritten
> when it changed. Halfway through the season a few strong players transferred, and every point they'd ever scored moved with them. The
> all-time ranking by university rewrote itself overnight, and two universities traded first place because of an UPDATE statement. The
> group chat was not calm. Slowly changing dimensions aren't an academic nicety; they're the difference between history and whatever the
> database feels like today.

## Things that will bite you

- **Undeclared or mixed grain** in a fact table: some rows per order, some per order line.
- **Summing semi-additive or non-additive measures.**
- **Type 1 where you needed Type 2**, and historical reports quietly rewriting themselves.
- **Joining facts to the current dimension row** instead of the row valid at event time.
- **Overlapping SCD2 intervals** from non-idempotent loads, duplicating facts.
- **NULL foreign keys** in facts, silently dropped by inner joins. Use "unknown" members.
