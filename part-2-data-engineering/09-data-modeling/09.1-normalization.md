# 09.1 Normalization and entity modeling

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: Module 08. Lab: [`lab_09_1_normalization.py`](labs/lab_09_1_normalization.py).*

A schema is an API. Every table and column you create will be read by people and programs you'll never meet, for years
after you've moved on, and every ambiguity in it will be resolved differently by each of them. I've seen a column called
`amount` that meant "with tax" in one service and "without tax" in another, joined together in a finance report for a
year before anyone noticed. That's not a SQL bug. It's a modeling bug, and no query optimizer can fix it.

This lesson is about modeling data for systems that *write* it: entities, relationships, keys and normalization, the
discipline that keeps operational data consistent. The next lesson is about modeling for systems that *read* it
(dimensional modeling), which deliberately breaks some of these rules. You need to know the rules before you break them.

## Entities, attributes, relationships

Start from the business, not from the tables:

- **Entities**: the things the business cares about and can identify: customer, product, order, warehouse, shipment.
- **Attributes**: properties of an entity: a customer's email, a product's price.
- **Relationships** between entities, with **cardinality**: a customer places *many* orders (one-to-many); an order contains *many* products and a product
  appears in *many* orders (many-to-many); a user has *one* profile (one-to-one).

An entity-relationship (ER) diagram is just a drawing of that. It's worth drawing, even roughly, before writing DDL, because arguments about the drawing are
cheap and arguments about a production schema are expensive.

In tables:

- One-to-many: a foreign key on the "many" side (`orders.customer_id`).
- Many-to-many: a **bridge** (junction) table with two foreign keys (`order_items(order_id, product_id)`), which often carries attributes of the relationship
  itself (quantity, unit price at the time of purchase).
- One-to-one: a foreign key with a unique constraint, or just columns on the same table. Split only for a reason (different access patterns, sensitive data
  with different permissions, optional data).

## Keys

- A **natural key** comes from the business: email, national ID, ISBN, a product SKU. Meaningful, but they change (people change emails), get reused, have
  formatting variations, and sometimes contain personal data you don't want to spread through every table (15.2).
- A **surrogate key** is generated by the system (an auto-increment integer, a UUID): meaningless, stable, compact. Use surrogate keys as primary keys and
  enforce natural keys with `UNIQUE` constraints where they exist.

UUIDs vs integers: UUIDs can be generated anywhere without coordination (useful in distributed systems) and don't leak business information (an
auto-increment order ID tells a competitor how many orders you had last month, and invites enumeration attacks: if `/orders/1001` is yours, an attacker
tries `/orders/1002`). Random UUIDs (v4) fragment B-tree indexes on insert; time-ordered UUIDs (v7) fix most of that. Integers are smaller and faster.
Pick deliberately.

## Normalization: one fact in one place

Consider this table, the kind you get from an "export to CSV" button:

| order_id | order_date | customer_id | customer_name | customer_email | product_id | product_name | category | unit_price | qty |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2025-03-01 | 7 | Ana Garcia | ana@x.com | 42 | Keyboard #42 | keyboards | 59.90 | 1 |
| 1 | 2025-03-01 | 7 | Ana Garcia | ana@x.com | 13 | Cable #13 | cables | 9.90 | 2 |
| 2 | 2025-03-02 | 7 | Ana Garcia | ana@x.com | 42 | Keyboard #42 | keyboards | 59.90 | 1 |

It works for reading. For writing, it's a minefield of **anomalies**:

- **Update anomaly**: Ana changes her email. You must update every row of every order she ever placed. Miss one, and the database now contains two emails for
  the same customer, with no way to know which is right.
- **Insert anomaly**: you can't record a new customer who hasn't ordered yet, or a new product nobody has bought, without inventing a fake order.
- **Delete anomaly**: delete Ana's only order and you lose the fact that Ana exists.

The cause is the same in all three: facts about different entities (customers, products, orders) are stored together, so each fact is repeated. The cure,
**normalization**, puts each fact in exactly one place.

### Functional dependencies and the normal forms

A **functional dependency** $A \to B$ means: the value of $A$ determines the value of $B$. Here, `customer_id → customer_name, customer_email`, `product_id →
product_name, category`, `order_id → order_date, customer_id`, and `(order_id, product_id) → qty, unit_price`.

The normal forms are progressively stricter rules about dependencies:

- **1NF**: every column holds atomic values (no lists or repeated groups like `phone1, phone2, phone3`), and rows are identifiable by a key.
- **2NF**: 1NF, and no non-key column depends on only *part* of a composite key. In the table above, with key `(order_id, product_id)`, `order_date` depends only
  on `order_id`: violation. Move it to an `orders` table.
- **3NF**: 2NF, and no non-key column depends on another non-key column (no transitive dependencies). `customer_name` depends on `customer_id`, which depends
  on `order_id`: violation. Move customer attributes to a `customers` table.
- **BCNF** (Boyce-Codd): every determinant is a candidate key. A slightly stricter 3NF; the difference rarely matters in practice.

The informal version, which is the one people actually remember: **every non-key attribute must depend on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key**.

Normalizing the export gives the schema you've been using since 08.1: `customers`, `products`, `orders`, `order_items`. And normalization must be
**lossless**: joining the normalized tables back must reproduce exactly the original rows. The lab checks that.

Notice one subtle thing: `unit_price` stays in `order_items` even though `products` has a `price`. That's not a normalization violation. The price *at the time
of the order* is a different fact from the product's *current* price. Confusing "current value" with "value at the time" is one of the most common modeling
mistakes, and it destroys historical reports: recompute last year's revenue with today's prices and you get fiction. Snapshot the values that matter at the
moment of the event.

### When to denormalize

Normalized schemas are optimal for writes and consistency. For reads, they cost joins. So you denormalize deliberately:

- In **analytical** stores, almost always (09.2: star schemas and wide tables), because data is written in batches by pipelines, not by concurrent users, and
  reads dominate.
- In **operational** stores, sparingly, for a proven performance need, with a mechanism to keep the copies in sync (triggers, application logic, or accepting
  eventual consistency). Every denormalization is a promise to keep two copies consistent. Most teams break that promise eventually.

## Semi-structured data

Modern databases support **JSON** columns (PostgreSQL's `jsonb`, DuckDB's `JSON` and `STRUCT`, BigQuery's `JSON` and nested records). Useful for:
attributes that vary per row (product specifications: a laptop has RAM, a hoodie has a size), raw payloads you want to keep as received (API responses,
events), and fast-moving prototypes.

Dangerous as a way to avoid designing a schema: no constraints, no types, every consumer parses it differently, and the "schema" lives implicitly in the code
of whoever wrote it last. My rule: JSON for genuinely variable or raw data, proper columns for anything you filter, join or aggregate on regularly. Promote
fields from JSON to columns when they become important.

## Practical conventions

Small things that make a schema pleasant to live with:

- **Naming**: `snake_case`, singular or plural consistently (pick one), `<entity>_id` for keys, units in names when ambiguous (`duration_ms`, `price_eur`,
  `weight_kg`), booleans as `is_active` / `has_discount`, timestamps as `created_at` / `shipped_at` in UTC.
- **Audit columns**: `created_at`, `updated_at` (maintained reliably), sometimes `created_by`. Incremental data pipelines depend on `updated_at` being correct
  (13.1). I've seen more than one pipeline silently miss data because an application updated rows without touching `updated_at`.
- **Soft deletes** (`deleted_at` instead of `DELETE`) keep history and make CDC easier, at the cost of remembering to filter them everywhere. Hard deletes
  may be *required* for personal data (the right to erasure, 15.2). Decide per table.
- **Enumerations**: a lookup table or a `CHECK` constraint, not free text. Free-text status columns end up containing `'shipped'`, `'Shipped'`, `'SHIPPED '`
  and `'shiped'` (the dirty shop in 10.3 has exactly this).
- **Document the columns.** Most databases support comments on tables and columns (`COMMENT ON COLUMN ...`). A data catalog (15.2) can pick them up.

## Exercises

1. Identify the functional dependencies in this table and normalize it to 3NF: `enrollments(student_id, student_name, course_id, course_title, instructor_id,
   instructor_name, grade)`.
2. For the export table in the lesson, write one concrete example each of an update, insert and delete anomaly.
3. Why keep `unit_price` in `order_items` when `products.price` exists? Give a report that would be wrong without it.
4. A product catalogue sells laptops, books and hoodies with very different attributes. Propose two schema designs (one relational, one using JSON) and list
   the trade-offs.
5. Your API exposes `/invoices/{invoice_id}` with sequential integer IDs and checks authentication but not ownership. What's the vulnerability, and what
   modeling and application-level fixes would you apply?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. FDs: `student_id → student_name`; `course_id → course_title, instructor_id`; `instructor_id → instructor_name`; `(student_id, course_id) → grade`.
   3NF: `students(student_id, student_name)`, `instructors(instructor_id, instructor_name)`, `courses(course_id, course_title, instructor_id)`,
   `enrollments(student_id, course_id, grade)`. (If a course can have several instructors, it's a many-to-many and needs a bridge table.)

2. Update: Ana changes her email; updating only her most recent order's rows leaves two different emails. Insert: a new product can't be stored until
   someone buys it. Delete: removing order 2 is fine, but removing order 1 and 2 (e.g. a GDPR-unrelated cleanup of test orders) erases Ana entirely.

3. Prices change. "Revenue in Q1 2024" computed as `quantity * products.price` uses today's prices, so every price change silently rewrites history, and
   discounts applied at checkout vanish. The order line must record the price actually charged.

4. Relational: a `products` table with common columns, plus per-type tables (`laptop_specs`, `book_specs`) or an entity-attribute-value table. Strong typing
   and constraints; more tables and joins; schema changes for each new product type. JSON: `products.attributes jsonb`. Flexible; no constraints; every
   consumer parses it; filtering and indexing need JSON-aware indexes. A common compromise: typed columns for what you filter on (brand, price, category),
   JSON for the long tail of specs.

5. Insecure direct object reference (IDOR, broken object-level authorization, #1 in the OWASP API Top 10): any logged-in user can read anyone's invoice by
   incrementing the ID. Fix at the application level: every query must check ownership (`WHERE invoice_id = ? AND customer_id = <the authenticated user>`).
   Non-sequential identifiers (UUIDs) make enumeration harder but are **not** a fix on their own: they're defense in depth.
