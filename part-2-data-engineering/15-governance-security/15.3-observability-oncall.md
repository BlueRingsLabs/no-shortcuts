# 15.3 Data observability and on-call

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 10.3, Module 13, Module 14. Lab: [`lab_15_3_data_observability.py`](labs/lab_15_3_data_observability.py).*

The worst data incidents aren't the pipelines that fail. A failed pipeline turns red, pages someone, gets fixed. The worst ones
are the pipelines that succeed while doing the wrong thing: the job that ran on time and loaded 40% of the usual rows because an
upstream export was truncated, the column that quietly became all NULL after a vendor changed their API, the join that started
duplicating rows after someone added a second address per customer. Everything is green, the dashboards update, and people make
decisions on garbage for two weeks until someone in finance says "that can't be right".

**Data observability** is about catching those: monitoring the *data*, not just the jobs. And since someone has to respond when
the monitors fire, this lesson also covers the operational side: alerts that deserve a human, runbooks, incidents and post-mortems.
Being on call for a data platform is part of the Data Engineer job at most companies (the job postings I reviewed for this course
mentioned it explicitly), so it's worth doing well.

## The pillars

Data observability tools (Monte Carlo, Soda, Elementary, Great Expectations, or your own SQL checks) look at the same handful of
signals per table:

1. **Freshness**: when was the table last updated, and when was the newest record's event time? A daily table not updated by 9 AM is late.
2. **Volume**: row counts per load or per partition, compared with history. A drop to 40% or a doubling is almost always a problem.
3. **Schema**: columns added, removed, renamed or retyped since the last run.
4. **Distribution**: null rates, distinct counts, min/max, mean, category frequencies per column, compared with history. The all-NULL column and the new
   unexpected status value show up here.
5. **Lineage** (15.2): which upstream tables fed this one, and which downstream tables and dashboards are affected when it breaks.

The first four are metrics you compute cheaply after every load (a `SUMMARIZE`-style query) and store in a metrics table. The detection is then a time
series problem.

## Detecting anomalies without drowning in alerts

Static thresholds ("alert if fewer than 10,000 rows") break as soon as the business grows or has a seasonal pattern. Better:

- **Compare with the same period in the recent past**: Tuesday vs the last few Tuesdays, handling weekly seasonality.
- **Use robust statistics**: median and MAD instead of mean and standard deviation (10.3, 15.1), so one past incident doesn't widen the "normal" band forever.
- **Alert on relative deviations with a minimum absolute size**, so a table that goes from 3 rows to 6 doesn't page anyone.
- **Combine explicit rules with learned baselines**: some things are known to be wrong regardless of history (a primary key that isn't unique, negative
  amounts), and those are tests (10.3, 13.3), not anomalies.

And be ruthless about alert quality. Every alert that fires without requiring action trains the team to ignore alerts, and then the real one gets ignored
too (that's 04.2's base-rate problem, in operations clothing). Track what fraction of alerts led to real action, and tune or delete the ones that don't.

## Severity and routing

Not everything needs to wake someone up. A simple scheme:

- **Page** (immediate, any hour): critical data is missing or wrong and something important consumes it soon (the pricing model's features, a regulatory
  report due today, customer-facing numbers).
- **Ticket** (next business day): degraded but not urgent (a secondary dashboard is a day late).
- **Log** (reviewed weekly): informational anomalies.

Define **SLAs/SLOs** for important tables ("`fct_orders` for day D is complete by 07:00 UTC on D+1, 99% of days") with their consumers. Then alerts are about
breaking a promise someone relies on, not about any deviation you can measure.

## Runbooks

A **runbook** is the page an on-call engineer reads when an alert fires, ideally linked from the alert itself. It answers:

- What does this alert mean, and what's the impact?
- How do I confirm it's real? (Which query, which dashboard.)
- The common causes and how to fix each (re-run a task, backfill a date, contact the source owner, roll back a deploy).
- How do I stop the damage while investigating? (Pause downstream jobs so bad data doesn't propagate; mark the affected table as stale in the catalog; tell
  consumers.)
- Who to escalate to.

The best runbooks are written right after an incident, by the person who handled it, while the details are fresh.

## Incidents and post-mortems

When something breaks:

1. **Stop the bleeding**: prevent bad data from spreading (pause downstream, quarantine), and communicate to consumers early ("revenue numbers for March 14
   are wrong, fix in progress, don't use them"). Silence is worse than bad news.
2. **Fix** the immediate problem (re-run, backfill, restore a table from time travel, 11.2).
3. **Write a blameless post-mortem**: timeline, impact, root cause, why detection took as long as it did, and concrete follow-up actions (a new check, a
   contract with the source team, a runbook update). Blameless means you look for the conditions that made the mistake easy, not for someone to punish.
   People hide problems in cultures that punish them, and hidden problems are the expensive ones.
4. **Track the follow-ups** to completion. A post-mortem whose action items never get done is a very expensive document.

## Monitoring the platform itself

Besides the data: job durations (a job that's getting slower every week will eventually miss its SLA), cost per job and per table (a sudden jump means a
query started scanning much more data, 08.3), queue and consumer lag for streams (14.1), orchestrator health (a scheduler that stopped scheduling produces
no failures at all: monitor freshness of outputs, not only task failures, 13.2), and storage growth.

## Things that will bite you

- **Monitoring jobs but not data.**
- **Static thresholds** that page every Monday and never on the real incident.
- **Too many alerts**, and then nobody reads them.
- **No runbook**, so every incident is solved from scratch at 3 AM.
- **Silent fixes**: consumers never learn the numbers they used were wrong.
- **Post-mortems that blame people** instead of fixing conditions.

## Exercises

1. Define freshness, volume and distribution checks for the shop's `fct_orders` table, with thresholds and severities.
2. The daily row count for `events` is 1.2M on weekdays and 400k on weekends. Design a volume check that doesn't fire every Saturday.
3. Write a runbook for the alert "`fct_orders` volume for yesterday is 45% below expected".
4. A column's null rate went from 0.1% to 12% overnight, and all tests passed. Why might tests not have caught it, and which check would?
5. Draft the structure of a blameless post-mortem for an incident where a timezone change in a source made "daily revenue" shift by one day for two weeks.

  incidents) and 15 (post-mortem culture). Written for services; almost all of it applies to data.
