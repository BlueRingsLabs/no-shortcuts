# 15.1 Securing data platforms

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: Modules 08 to 14, 07.4. Lab: [`lab_15_1_platform_security.py`](labs/lab_15_1_platform_security.py).*

A data platform puts the most valuable information a company has in one place and makes it easy to query, join and export.
That's the product. It's also why its security is part of *your* job and not something you can hand to "the security team"
at the end. Almost every data incident I've seen or read about started with something mundane: an access grant nobody
reviewed, a credential in the wrong place, a storage bucket shared "temporarily", a service account with far more rights
than its job needed. No clever exploit, just configuration drift that nobody owned.

This lesson is about owning it: deciding who can see what, keeping credentials out of places they don't belong, encrypting
what matters, and keeping a record of who did what so you can answer questions later. It's written from the builder's side
of the table. The goal is a platform where doing the secure thing is also the easy thing.

## Start with an inventory, not a tool

Before choosing controls, answer three questions and write the answers down:

1. **What data do we hold, and how sensitive is it?** Personal data, payment data, health data, credentials, internal financials, model weights trained on any of
   those. Classification is covered in 15.2; for now, you need at least "public, internal, confidential, restricted" per table or column.
2. **Who and what needs access, and to which part?** People (analysts, engineers, data scientists, support staff) and machines (pipelines, the orchestrator,
   BI tools, CI, SaaS connectors). Machines usually outnumber people and are the ones people forget.
3. **Where does data leave the platform?** Dashboards, CSV downloads, reverse ETL into SaaS tools, notebook exports, model training jobs, emails. Each of these is
   a place where your access controls stop applying.

That inventory is your threat model in plain words: valuable assets, the identities that can reach them, and the exits. Revisit it whenever you add a data
source, a tool, or an integration.

## Identity and least privilege

Every access should be tied to an identity, and every identity should have the smallest set of permissions that lets it do its job.

- **People authenticate through single sign-on** with multi-factor authentication, not with shared database users. When someone leaves, disabling one account
  must remove all of their access.
- **Machines get their own service identities**, one per pipeline or component, not one shared "etl_admin" account. Then a compromised or buggy job is limited
  to what that one job could do, and your audit log says *which* job did something.
- **Prefer short-lived credentials**: cloud roles assumed by workloads (workload identity, instance profiles) instead of long-lived keys stored in config files.
  A credential that expires in an hour is a much smaller problem than one that works until someone remembers to rotate it.
- **Grant through roles, not to individuals.** `analyst_marketing`, `pipeline_orders_writer`, `bi_readonly`. Access reviews are only practical when there are a few
  dozen roles to check, not thousands of individual grants.
- **Separate read, write and admin.** The pipeline that loads `orders` can write to `bronze.orders` and nothing else. Analysts read gold tables. Very few people can
  change permissions, and those changes are logged and reviewed.

### Row- and column-level security

Coarse "can read the table or can't" isn't enough for most real data. Warehouses and lakehouse catalogs support finer controls:

- **Column-level**: sensitive columns (email, phone, national ID) are hidden or **masked** for roles that don't need them. A support analyst sees `a***@example.com`;
  the fraud team sees the full value.
- **Row-level**: a policy filters rows by the reader's attributes. The Spain team sees Spanish customers; a partner sees only their own orders.

When a platform doesn't have native support, secure **views** that apply masking and filters, combined with granting access only to the views, give you the
same effect. The lab builds exactly that on DuckDB.

## Secrets management

Credentials are the most common thing that ends up where it shouldn't. The rules:

- **Never in code, notebooks, config files in Git, container images, or logs.** Git history keeps them forever (00.2); notebooks get shared and exported; logs get
  shipped to systems many people can read (01.2).
- **Store them in a secrets manager** (cloud secret managers, HashiCorp Vault, your orchestrator's secrets backend) and inject them at runtime, ideally as
  short-lived values.
- **Scan for accidents.** Pre-commit hooks and CI jobs that look for credential-like strings catch mistakes before they're pushed. The lab writes a small scanner
  so you understand what these tools look for.
- **Rotate, and have a plan for leaks.** If a secret might have been exposed, assume it was: revoke and replace it first, investigate second.

## Encryption

- **In transit**: TLS everywhere, including internal connections between services and to the database. Never disable certificate verification to make an error go
  away (07.4).
- **At rest**: every major cloud storage service and warehouse encrypts by default. Your decisions are about **keys**: provider-managed keys are the default;
  customer-managed keys (in a key management service) let you control rotation and revoke access by disabling the key.
- **Field-level encryption** for the most sensitive columns (national IDs, full card numbers, health details): encrypt the value in the pipeline before it lands,
  so the warehouse only ever stores ciphertext and only services with access to the key can decrypt. This also limits what ends up in backups, logs and extracts.
  The lab does this with a symmetric key.
- **Tokenization and pseudonymization** (15.2) are related options when you need to join on a sensitive value without revealing it.

## Network boundaries

Databases, Kafka brokers, notebook servers and orchestrator UIs should not be reachable from the public internet. Use private networking (VPCs, private
endpoints), bind services to internal interfaces (07.4), and block public access on storage accounts by default at the organization level. When something must be
shared externally, share a specific object through a signed, expiring link, not a public bucket.

## Audit logging and review

You can't investigate what you didn't record. Turn on:

- **Access logs** for the warehouse (who ran which query, how many rows or bytes it returned), for storage (who read or wrote which objects), and for the catalog
  (who changed which permission).
- **Pipeline and deployment history** (who changed which DAG or dbt model, and when: this is your Git history plus CI logs).

Then actually look at them, with automated checks for things like: a user suddenly exporting far more data than their usual volume, access from a service account
outside its normal schedule, permission grants to broad roles, or reads of restricted tables by roles that shouldn't have them. The lab computes a simple
per-user baseline and flags unusual export volumes; real platforms feed these logs into the company's security monitoring.

And run **periodic access reviews**: every quarter, each role owner confirms who still needs what. Removing access nobody uses is one of the cheapest, most
effective security improvements there is.

## The platform's own supply chain

Your pipelines run third-party code: Python packages, dbt packages, Airflow providers, container base images, SaaS connectors. Pin versions with lock files
(01.2), review what you add, keep dependencies updated, and give connectors read-only access scoped to what they need. We'll go deeper on this for ML in 53.2.

> **Scar tissue.** Most of my paid security work has been freelance pentesting, and the finding I've reported most often on data platforms
> isn't clever. It's personal data in logs. One client had debug logging of full request bodies in a scoring API, shipped to a hosted log
> service with two-year retention and access for everyone in the company, and those bodies included national ID numbers. The database was
> locked down beautifully. The logs were the database, with worse permissions.

## Things that will bite you

- **Shared credentials and shared service accounts**: no accountability, no way to revoke one without breaking everything.
- **Permissions granted in a hurry and never removed.**
- **Secrets in notebooks, config files and logs.**
- **Exports that bypass your controls**: the CSV on a laptop no longer has row-level security.
- **Audit logs that are enabled but never read.**
- **Public network exposure "just for testing".**

## Exercises

1. List the identities (people and machines) that need access to the shop's `customers` table and the minimum permissions each needs.
2. Design column masking for `customers.email` for three roles: support (partial), marketing analytics (none, but needs to count distinct customers), fraud (full).
3. A data scientist asks for full read access to production "to explore". What do you offer instead, and why?
4. Write the checks a CI pipeline should run on every pull request to a data platform repository, from a security point of view.
5. What would you want to see in the audit logs to answer "did anyone export customer emails in the last 30 days?", and how would you make that question easy to
   answer next time?

1. The CDC or extraction pipeline (write to `bronze.customers` only), the transformation job (read bronze, write silver/gold customer models), the BI tool's service

4. Secret scanning on the diff, dependency vulnerability scanning and lock file checks, infrastructure-as-code checks (no public buckets, no `0.0.0.0/0` rules, encryption

5. Warehouse query logs with user, timestamp, the tables and columns read, rows and bytes returned, and the export destination if the platform records it; storage access
   logs for any extract location. To make it easy next time: tag sensitive columns in the catalog (15.2), keep query logs in a queryable table with a retention that matches
   your policy, and have a saved report (or alert) of reads of tagged columns by role.

## Further reading

- Reis and Housley, *Fundamentals of Data Engineering*, chapter 10 (security and privacy).
- NIST SP 800-207, *Zero Trust Architecture*, sections 2 and 3. The principles behind "every access is authenticated and authorized".
- The security and access-control documentation of your warehouse or catalog (row access policies, masking policies, column tags). Read it once end to end; most
  platforms have more controls than teams use.
- OWASP, *Secrets Management Cheat Sheet*.
- Adam Shostack, *Threat Modeling: Designing for Security*, Wiley, 2014, chapters 1 to 3.
