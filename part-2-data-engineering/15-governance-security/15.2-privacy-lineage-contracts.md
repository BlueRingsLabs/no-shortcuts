# 15.2 Privacy, lineage, catalogs and data contracts

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 15.1, Module 13, 11.2. Lab: [`lab_15_2_privacy_lineage.py`](labs/lab_15_2_privacy_lineage.py).*

Two questions that every data platform eventually has to answer, usually on short notice and usually from someone senior:
"where does this number come from?" and "where is this person's data?". The first comes from a CFO staring at a revenue figure
that doesn't match finance's spreadsheet. The second comes from a legal team holding a deletion request with a deadline. If the
honest answer to either is "let me grep the codebase and ask around", the platform has a governance problem, and those problems
get more expensive every month you ignore them.

This lesson covers the tools that make those questions answerable: data classification, privacy techniques and the obligations
behind them, lineage, catalogs, and data contracts between the teams that produce data and the teams that consume it.

## Personal data and what the law expects

Most jurisdictions now regulate personal data. The EU's GDPR is the reference many others echo (Brazil's LGPD, California's CCPA/CPRA,
and many more). I'm an engineer, not a lawyer, and you should involve your legal and privacy people for real decisions, but the engineering
consequences are fairly consistent:

- **Personal data** is anything relating to an identifiable person: names and emails obviously, but also IDs, IP addresses, device IDs, precise
  locations, and combinations of innocent-looking fields (postcode + birth date + gender identifies a large fraction of people).
- **Purpose limitation and minimization**: collect and keep only what you need for a stated purpose. "We might use it someday" is not a purpose.
- **Retention limits**: data has an expiry date. Pipelines must delete or anonymize it when it expires, including in backups and derived tables.
- **Data subject rights**: access (give a person their data), rectification, **erasure** (delete it), portability. Erasure is the hard one for data
  platforms, because data gets copied into dozens of derived tables, extracts, feature stores and model training sets.
- **Special categories** (health, biometrics, religion, and so on) have stricter rules.
- **Accountability**: you must be able to show what you do with data. That's lineage and documentation.

## Classification

You can't protect what you haven't labeled. Tag every table and column with a sensitivity level and, for personal data, a category:

| Level | Examples | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| Public | product catalogue | no restrictions |
| Internal | aggregated sales | employees only |
| Confidential | customer emails, order history | role-based, masked by default (15.1) |
| Restricted | national IDs, payment details, health data | encrypted, tightly scoped, every access logged |

Tags live in the catalog (below) and drive policy: masking rules, who can request access, retention, what may be exported. Automated scanners help find
personal data in new columns (patterns for emails, phone numbers, card numbers), but a human owner confirms the classification.

## Privacy techniques, and what each one actually protects

- **Masking**: hide part of a value for display (`a***@example.com`). Protects against casual exposure; the original still exists.
- **Pseudonymization**: replace identifiers with consistent tokens (a keyed hash or a lookup table). Lets you join and count without seeing the identity.
  Under GDPR, pseudonymized data is **still personal data**, because whoever holds the key or the lookup table can reverse it.
- **Tokenization**: store the real value in a separate, tightly controlled vault and use random tokens everywhere else.
- **Generalization and suppression**: coarsen quasi-identifiers (birth year instead of date, first digits of the postcode) and suppress rare
  combinations. **k-anonymity** requires every combination of quasi-identifiers to appear at least k times. It helps, but it's not bulletproof: combined with
  outside information, "anonymized" datasets have been re-identified many times.
- **Aggregation with minimum group sizes**: only publish counts over groups of at least N people.
- **Differential privacy**: add calibrated noise so that the output barely changes whether or not any single person is in the data. It's the only one of these
  with a mathematical guarantee, and it has a cost in accuracy. We cover it properly in 54.2.

The honest summary: masking and pseudonymization reduce exposure inside your organization; they don't make data anonymous. Treat "anonymized" claims with
healthy skepticism, including your own.

## Designing for erasure

Deleting one person's data from a platform with 400 derived tables is a design problem, not a query. What works:

1. **Know where personal data lives**: classification plus lineage (below) gives you the list of tables derived from customer data.
2. **Key everything by a stable internal ID**, not by email, so you can find a person's rows everywhere.
3. **Minimize copies**: marts that don't need identity shouldn't carry it (aggregate or pseudonymize early).
4. **Make deletion a pipeline**: an erasure request becomes a record in a `deletion_requests` table; scheduled jobs delete matching rows from every
   registered table, and the table formats' maintenance (snapshot expiry and vacuum, 11.2) removes the physical files within your retention window.
5. **Verify**: a job that checks, after the deadline, that no table still contains the ID.
6. **Models trained on the data** raise their own question (retrain on schedule, or document why the model doesn't retain individual records); 54.2 goes into it.

## Lineage

**Lineage** is the graph of which datasets are derived from which. It answers "where does this number come from?" (walk upstream) and "what breaks, or what
contains personal data, if this source changes?" (walk downstream).

Sources of lineage:

- **Declared** in transformation tools: dbt knows its graph from `ref()` and `source()` (13.3); orchestrators know task dependencies (13.2).
- **Parsed** from SQL: tools like sqlglot parse queries and extract which tables and columns they read and write.
- **Captured at runtime** with the **OpenLineage** standard: Spark, Airflow, dbt and others emit lineage events as jobs run, collected by a backend (Marquez,
  or your catalog).

Table-level lineage is the minimum. **Column-level** lineage (this column in the dashboard comes from these columns upstream) is what really answers the CFO's
question and tells you exactly where an email address propagated.

## Catalogs

A **data catalog** is the searchable inventory of your data: every table, its schema, description, owner, classification tags, freshness, quality checks, usage
statistics and lineage. Examples: DataHub, OpenMetadata, Amundsen, Unity Catalog and cloud-native catalogs. It's where an analyst goes to find "the right orders
table" instead of asking in chat, and where governance policies attach to data.

A catalog is only as good as its metadata. Automate what you can (schemas, lineage, usage, dbt docs), and make **ownership** mandatory: every dataset has a team
that answers for it.

## Data contracts

Most data incidents come from an upstream change nobody told downstream about: a column renamed in the application database, a status value added, an event field
that changed from dollars to cents. A **data contract** is an explicit, versioned agreement between a producer and its consumers about a dataset:

- **Schema**: fields, types, nullability, allowed values.
- **Semantics**: what each field means, units, time zones, the grain.
- **Quality and service levels**: freshness, completeness, uniqueness guarantees.
- **Ownership and change process**: who owns it, how breaking changes are announced and versioned.

Enforce contracts where the data is produced: validate in CI before a producer deploys (compare the new schema against the contract, 11.1's compatibility rules),
and at ingestion (reject or quarantine violating records, 10.3). The point isn't the YAML file; it's moving the responsibility for data quality to the team that
can actually fix it.

## Things that will bite you

- **"Anonymized" data that's merely pseudonymized.**
- **Personal data copied into every mart**, making erasure nearly impossible.
- **Deletes that don't reach backups, snapshots and extracts.**
- **Catalogs full of tables with no owner.**
- **Upstream schema changes without a contract**, discovered by a broken dashboard.
