# 14.1 Logs, Kafka and event-driven architecture

*About 12 hours. Prerequisites: 08.4, 11.1, 13.1. Lab: [`lab_14_1_partitioned_log.py`](labs/lab_14_1_partitioned_log.py).*

Batch pipelines answer "what happened yesterday?". A lot of the valuable questions are "what's happening right now?": is this
card transaction fraudulent, is this server under attack, which products are trending in the last ten minutes, what should we
recommend to the user who just added a GPU to their cart. For those, you can't wait for the nightly job. You need data as a
**stream**, and the backbone of almost every streaming architecture is one surprisingly simple data structure: an append-only,
partitioned **log**. Apache Kafka is the famous implementation (Redpanda, Amazon Kinesis, Google Pub/Sub, Azure Event Hubs and
Apache Pulsar are variations on the theme).

I like logs as a concept because you've already met them several times: the write-ahead log that makes databases durable (08.4),
the transaction log of a lakehouse table (11.2), the CDC stream from a database's binlog (13.1). Kafka takes that idea and makes it
the center of the architecture. The lab builds a small but faithful partitioned log with consumer groups and offsets, so the
guarantees stop being magic words.

## The log

A log is an ordered, append-only sequence of records. Each record gets a sequential **offset**. Writers append at the end; readers read from any
offset they choose and move forward at their own pace. Records aren't deleted when read; they're kept for a **retention** period (hours, days,
forever) or until the log reaches a size limit, or **compacted** (keeping only the latest record per key).

Why such a simple structure goes so far:

- **Decoupling**: producers don't know who consumes, consumers don't know who produces. Add a new consumer (a fraud model, a search indexer, a data
  lake sink) without touching the producer.
- **Replay**: a consumer that had a bug can rewind to an old offset and reprocess. A new consumer can read history from the beginning.
- **Buffering**: a slow consumer falls behind (its **lag** grows) without slowing the producer or losing data, as long as retention covers the gap.
- **Ordering**: within a log, everyone sees records in the same order.

## Kafka's model

- A **topic** is a named log, split into **partitions** for scalability. Each partition is an independent ordered log, stored on disk and replicated
  across **brokers** (servers).
- **Ordering is guaranteed only within a partition**, not across a topic.
- Producers choose the partition, usually by **hashing the record key**: all records with the same key (the same customer, the same account, the same
  device) land in the same partition, so they're ordered relative to each other. No key means round-robin: good balance, no ordering per entity.
- **Consumer groups**: consumers that share a group ID split the partitions among themselves; each partition is read by exactly one consumer in the
  group at a time. That's how you scale consumption horizontally, and why **the number of partitions caps the parallelism of a consumer group**.
  Different groups read the same topic independently, each with its own position.
- **Offsets are committed** by consumers to record their position per partition. If a consumer dies, another consumer in the group takes over its
  partitions (a **rebalance**) and resumes from the last committed offset.
- **Replication**: each partition has a leader and followers on other brokers. With `acks=all`, a write is acknowledged only after the in-sync
  replicas have it, so it survives a broker failure.

Choosing the key is a design decision with the same trade-off as partitioning in Spark (12.2): a key gives per-entity ordering, but a hot key (one
huge customer, one busy device) creates a hot partition, and skew is back.

## Delivery guarantees

The part everyone gets asked about in interviews, and the part that's genuinely subtle. The question is: what happens to a record when something
fails between reading it and finishing processing it?

- **At-most-once**: commit the offset *before* processing. If the consumer crashes mid-processing, the record is skipped forever. No duplicates,
  possible loss.
- **At-least-once**: process, *then* commit. If the consumer crashes after processing but before committing, the new consumer re-reads the record
  and processes it again. No loss, possible duplicates. This is the practical default.
- **Exactly-once**: each record affects the result exactly once, even with failures. Achieved in practice by either:
  1. **Idempotent processing** on top of at-least-once: design the sink so that applying a record twice has the same effect as once (upsert by key,
     dedupe by event ID, 13.1). This is the approach I trust most, because it holds end to end, including sinks outside Kafka.
  2. **Transactions**: Kafka can atomically write output records and commit input offsets in one transaction (read-process-write within Kafka,
     as Kafka Streams and Flink do), with idempotent producers that deduplicate retried sends. Exactly-once *within* the system; the moment your
     side effect is an email or an API call, you're back to idempotency keys.

"Exactly-once" is best read as "effectively once": duplicates may be delivered, but their effect is deduplicated. Anybody who promises you exactly-once
*delivery* over an unreliable network is selling something. The lab crashes a consumer at the worst moment and shows loss (at-most-once), duplicates
(at-least-once), and a correct result with an idempotent sink.

## Event-driven architecture

Kafka is often the backbone for systems that communicate through events rather than direct calls:

- **Events** record facts that happened (`OrderPlaced`, `PaymentFailed`), in the past tense, immutable. **Commands** ask for something to happen
  (`ChargeCard`). Mixing the two in the same topic confuses everyone.
- **Event sourcing**: store the sequence of events as the source of truth and derive current state by replaying them (a bank account's balance is the
  sum of its transactions). Powerful for auditability, demanding in practice.
- **CDC as events**: Debezium turns every row change in an operational database into an event on a topic, which downstream systems consume (14.2,
  13.1). The database's log becomes everyone's log.
- **Schemas and contracts**: events are an API between teams. Use a schema format with evolution rules (Avro, Protobuf) and a schema registry (11.1),
  and treat breaking changes like breaking API changes (15.2).

For ML engineers specifically: real-time features (the number of transactions for this card in the last 10 minutes) are computed from streams, online
predictions are often triggered by events, and predictions themselves are published as events for monitoring and feedback (49.1, 51.1).

## Operating a log, briefly

Things you'll monitor and tune: **consumer lag** (the gap between the latest offset and the committed offset: the single most important streaming
metric, 15.3), throughput, partition balance (hot partitions), retention vs lag (if lag exceeds retention, data is lost before it's read), broker disk,
and under-replicated partitions. Security: Kafka without authentication and ACLs on an open network lets anyone read every event and inject fake ones.
It happens more than you'd think; I've seen exposed brokers in the wild. Enable TLS, SASL authentication and topic-level ACLs.

## Things that will bite you

- **Assuming global ordering** across partitions.
- **Too few partitions** (capping consumer parallelism) or too many (overhead); partitions are hard to change later without reshuffling keys.
- **Hot keys.**
- **At-least-once processing with a non-idempotent sink**: duplicates in the warehouse, double emails, double charges.
- **Auto-commit** of offsets on a timer, which commits records you haven't finished processing (at-most-once by accident).
- **Lag exceeding retention.**
- **Unauthenticated brokers.**

## Exercises

1. A topic has 6 partitions and a consumer group has 8 consumers. How many are active? What happens if one of the active ones crashes?
2. You need all events for the same bank account processed in order. What key do you use, and what's the risk?
3. A consumer reads a batch of 100 records, writes them to a database, and commits the offset. It crashes after writing 60. What happens with at-least-once
   semantics, and how do you make the result correct?
4. Why does "exactly-once" in Kafka Streams not protect you from sending a customer two emails?
5. Your fraud model's consumer lag has grown from seconds to 40 minutes during a sales peak. List three possible causes and remedies.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. 6 active (one per partition), 2 idle. If an active one crashes, a rebalance assigns its partition to one of the idle consumers (or redistributes), which
   resumes from the last committed offset: records processed but not committed by the crashed consumer are reprocessed.

2. Key by account ID: all its events land in one partition, in order. Risk: a very active account (a payment processor's settlement account, say) makes its
   partition hot, and every other account on that partition waits behind it.

3. The new consumer starts from the last committed offset (before the batch), re-reads all 100, and writes them again: 60 duplicates. Make the write idempotent:
   upsert by event ID or primary key, or insert with `ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING` on the event ID, or write the batch and the offset in the same database
   transaction (store offsets in the sink).

4. Kafka's transactions cover reading input offsets and writing output records *within Kafka*. Sending an email is an external side effect: if the processor
   crashes after sending but before its transaction commits, the record is reprocessed and the email is sent again. You need an idempotency key per email
   (the event ID) checked by the email service or a dedupe table.

5. (a) Throughput exceeds consumer capacity: add consumers (up to the number of partitions) or add partitions; batch more. (b) A slow dependency in processing
   (a feature store lookup, a model server at its limit): scale it, cache, or make calls async. (c) A hot partition from a hot key: one consumer is behind while
   others are idle; re-key or split the hot key. Also check for rebalance storms (consumers repeatedly timing out and triggering rebalances).

</details>
