# 34.1 Text, tokens and n-gram language models

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 04.2 (conditional probability, the chain rule of probability), 06.1 (cross-entropy), 01.1 (Python strings; regular expressions help). Lab: [`lab_34_1_ngram_lms.py`](labs/lab_34_1_ngram_lms.py).*

Part V is about language models, and every one of them, from a 1990s speech recognizer to the model you chatted with this morning, does
the same thing: assign a probability to the next token given the previous ones. The only differences are what a token is, how much of the
past the model can use, and how the probabilities are estimated. This lesson does it with counting, which is where the field started and
where most of the vocabulary (perplexity, smoothing, backoff, out-of-vocabulary) comes from. You'll also meet the two problems that no
amount of data solves, only better models: most word sequences are rare, and many are unseen.

It starts somewhere less glamorous: turning raw text into tokens, where more production bugs live than in any model.

## Text is messier than it looks

- **Unicode**: "café" can be four code points (é as one character) or five (e plus a combining accent). They display identically and compare unequal until you **normalize** (NFC or NFKC). The lab shows it. Add zero-width characters, non-breaking spaces, smart quotes, right-to-left marks and homoglyphs (Cyrillic "а" vs Latin "a", a favorite of phishing domains and prompt-injection payloads, 44.1), and "are these two strings the same" becomes a policy decision.
- **Case**: lowercasing merges "Apple" the company with "apple" the fruit, and helps with sparse data. A choice, not a default.
- **What is a word?** Splitting on whitespace keeps "it." and "it" apart; a regex split turns "U.S." into "u" and "s" and "0.93" into "0" and "93". Contractions, hyphens, URLs, emoji, and languages without spaces (Chinese, Japanese, Thai) all need rules. Sentence splitting on periods fails on "e.g." and "U.S.".
- **Encoding errors** (mojibake like "Ã©" for "é") come from decoding UTF-8 as Latin-1 somewhere upstream; fix them at the source.

Every one of these choices changes the vocabulary, the counts and every metric downstream, so write them down and apply them identically in training and serving. Subword tokenizers (35.1) sidestep most of the "what is a word" problem, not the Unicode one.

## Zipf's law and the long tail

Word frequencies follow **Zipf's law**: the $r$-th most frequent word has frequency roughly proportional to $1/r$. On this course's text, the lab measures a log-log slope of $-1.02$. A few words ("the", "of", "a") are everywhere; most words are rare; **37% of the vocabulary appears exactly once** in 190,000 tokens.

The consequence is that unseen words never go away. The lab's out-of-vocabulary rate on held-out lessons falls from 23% with 10,000 training tokens to 2.8% with 190,000, and would keep falling slowly with more: new names, typos, numbers and technical terms keep arriving. Every language model needs a strategy for things it hasn't seen: an `<unk>` token (here), characters, or subwords (35.1).

## Language models and the chain rule

A **language model** assigns a probability to a sequence. By the chain rule of probability,

$$P(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}).$$

No approximation yet: predicting the whole sequence is the same as predicting each token given everything before it. Every language model is a way of estimating those conditionals. The **n-gram** approximation is the Markov assumption: only the last $n - 1$ tokens matter.

$$P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}) \approx P(w_t \mid w_{t-n+1}, \dots, w_{t-1}).$$

A bigram model conditions on one previous word, a trigram model on two. The **maximum likelihood estimate** is a ratio of counts: $P(w \mid v) = c(v, w) / c(v)$.

## Perplexity

The standard metric: the geometric mean of the inverse probability the model assigns to each test token,

$$\text{PPL} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log P(w_t \mid \text{history})\right),$$

the exponential of the per-token cross-entropy (06.1). Read it as **the effective number of equally likely choices** the model is hesitating between at each step. A uniform guess over a 5,800-word vocabulary has perplexity 5,800; the lab's best model gets 173. Lower is better, and it's only comparable between models that use the same test text **and the same tokenization** (the lab's last section is a warning about this).

## Smoothing

The MLE is useless on new text: any bigram that never appeared in training gets probability 0, and a single zero makes the perplexity infinite. The lab's MLE bigram model scores exactly that. Test text always contains unseen n-grams (the long tail again, now for pairs). **Smoothing** moves some probability mass from seen events to unseen ones:

- **Add-one (Laplace)**: add 1 to every count. With a vocabulary of 5,800, each context gets 5,800 imaginary observations, drowning the real ones: the lab's add-one bigram (908) is worse than a unigram model (450).
- **Add-$k$** with small $k$ (0.01): much better (335), but it spreads the stolen mass uniformly over all unseen words, which is naive.
- **Backoff and interpolation**: when the trigram is unseen or rare, lean on the bigram, then the unigram. Interpolation mixes all levels, $\lambda_3 P_3 + \lambda_2 P_2 + \lambda_1 P_1$, with weights tuned on held-out data.
- **Kneser-Ney** (Kneser and Ney, 1995; Chen and Goodman, 1998): subtract a fixed discount $d \approx 0.75$ from every seen count and give the mass to the lower-order model, and make the lower-order model a **continuation probability**: how many *different* contexts a word appears after, not how often it appears.
  The classic example: "Francisco" is frequent, but almost only after "San", so as a guess for a new context it should be unlikely; a unigram model would rate it highly. Interpolated Kneser-Ney was the best n-gram smoothing for decades. In the lab, a trigram KN model reaches perplexity 173, against 450 for unigrams, and its probabilities sum to 1.0000 over the vocabulary (the lab checks: getting a smoothing method to normalize is where most implementations go wrong).

## Generation

Sample the next word from $P(w \mid$ last $n - 1$ words$)$, append, repeat. The lab's trigram samples are locally fluent and globally aimless ("the key, sort chunks, write them in the worst moment and shows the classical methods"): with two words of memory, nothing ties the sentence together. Longer n-grams are more fluent and mostly copy the training text, because long sequences are almost all unique. That tradeoff, sparsity against context, is what neural language models broke: they share statistical strength between similar contexts through embeddings (34.3, then Bengio et al.'s neural LM, 2003) instead of treating every word sequence as a separate event.

## Where n-grams still live

- **Baselines** and sanity checks: if your neural model doesn't beat a Kneser-Ney 5-gram, something is broken.
- **Data filtering for LLM pretraining** (35.3): fast n-gram models (KenLM) trained on high-quality text score web documents, and perplexity thresholds filter out junk.
- **Keyboard prediction, spelling correction and speech decoding** on constrained devices.
- **Deduplication and contamination checks** (35.3, 39.1): overlapping n-grams between training and test data.
- **Infini-gram** style indexes answer n-gram queries over trillions of tokens, for studying what's in a training corpus.

## Things that will bite you

- **Normalization done differently in training and serving** (NFC vs raw, lowercasing in one place only).
- **Mojibake** from a wrong encoding somewhere upstream.
- **Zero probabilities** from an unsmoothed model: infinite perplexity, or a crash on `log(0)`.
- **Probabilities that don't sum to 1** in a hand-rolled smoothing method, making perplexities look better than they are.
- **Comparing perplexities across tokenizations or vocabularies.** A model with a smaller vocabulary, or more text mapped to `<unk>`, gets a lower perplexity for free.
- **Test data overlapping training data**: split by document, not by sentence.
