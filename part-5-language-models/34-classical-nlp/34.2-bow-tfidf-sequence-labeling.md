## Bag of words

Represent a document as the counts of the words in it, ignoring order: a vector with one dimension per vocabulary word, almost all zeros. Throw away word order and you lose "not good" vs "good, not", and keep most of what topic and sentiment classification need. Any linear classifier works on top; with **naive Bayes** (19.2) it's the spam filter of the early 2000s.

**TF-IDF** reweights the counts: a word's **term frequency** in the document times its **inverse document frequency**, $\log\frac{N}{\text{df}(w)}$, which downweights words that appear everywhere ("the", "model" in an ML course) and upweights distinctive ones. Common refinements: **sublinear TF** ($1 + \log \text{tf}$, so the 20th occurrence counts less than the 2nd), L2-normalizing each document (so long documents don't dominate), and word **n-grams** (bigrams catch "not good", "new york").

The lab classifies paragraphs of this course by which Part they come from (foundations, data engineering, classical ML), 803 paragraphs, 5-fold cross-validation:

| model | accuracy |
|---|---|
| majority class | 0.45 |
| bag of words + naive Bayes | 0.82 |
| TF-IDF + logistic regression | 0.82 |
| TF-IDF with bigrams + logistic regression | 0.80 |

With 800 short documents, naive Bayes matches logistic regression and bigrams make it slightly worse, the small-data story of 19.2. The top-weighted words per class are the model's evidence and the first thing to check: "query, join, table, sql, rows" for data engineering, "matrix, tests, function, theta, vectors" for foundations. If the top features are artifacts (a signature line, a template phrase, the name of the person who writes all the tickets of one category), you've found leakage (18.4) before it found you.

**The hashing trick** maps each word to one of $B$ buckets with a hash function instead of a vocabulary: no dictionary to fit, store or keep in sync between training and serving, and new words just land somewhere. Collisions cost accuracy when $B$ is small: in the lab, 256 buckets give 0.62 and 4,096 give 0.76, the same as 262,144. The remaining gap to TF-IDF's 0.82 isn't collisions; the hashed counts lack the IDF weighting, which a `TfidfTransformer` after the hasher would restore. Used for large-scale online learning on streams (14.1) and anywhere the vocabulary can't be known in advance.

What bag of words can't do: understand word order beyond short n-grams, share information between synonyms ("car" and "automobile" are unrelated dimensions, which is what embeddings fix, 34.3), or handle long-range meaning. What it can do: give you a baseline in five minutes that most fancier approaches have to justify beating.

## Sequence labeling

Many tasks label each token:

- **Part-of-speech tagging**: noun, verb, adjective...
- **Named entity recognition** (NER): person, organization, location, product, date... extracting entities from contracts, tickets, clinical notes and threat reports.
- **Chunking**, slot filling ("book a flight to **Lisbon** on **Friday**"), and in security, extracting indicators (IPs, hashes, CVE IDs, domains) from free-text reports, where a regex covers the easy half.

Multi-token entities are encoded with **BIO tags**: B-PER begins a person, I-PER continues it, O is outside any entity. "Blue Harbor Bank" is B-ORG I-ORG I-ORG. Not every tag sequence is valid: I-ORG can't follow O or B-PER.

**Evaluation** is at the entity level: a predicted entity counts only if its span and type exactly match a true one, and you report precision, recall and F1 over entities (the CoNLL convention, implemented by `seqeval`). Token accuracy is misleading because most tokens are O.

### Three approaches

**Hidden Markov models** (19.2's generative approach, applied to sequences): tags form a Markov chain, $P(t_i \mid t_{i-1})$, and each tag emits a word, $P(w_i \mid t_i)$, both estimated by counting. The best tag sequence for a sentence is found by the **Viterbi algorithm**, dynamic programming over tags: $O(T K^2)$ for $T$ tokens and $K$ tags instead of $K^T$ sequences. Its weakness is the emission model: a word is either a known identity or unknown, so it can't use what an unseen word *looks like*.

**Feature-based classifiers** (a MEMM or just logistic regression per token): describe each token by features, such as the word itself, whether it's capitalized, its suffix, and the neighboring words and their shapes, and classify each token. This generalizes to unseen names ("capitalized, and after 'according to'"), but each decision is made independently, so nothing stops it from predicting I-ORG right after O.

**Conditional random fields** (Lafferty, McCallum and Pereira, 2001) combine both: feature-based scores for each token's tag plus learned **transition** scores between adjacent tags, trained to maximize the probability of the whole correct sequence:

$$P(t \mid w) \propto \exp\left(\sum_i \left[\text{score}(t_i, w, i) + T_{t_{i-1}, t_i}\right]\right).$$

The normalizer sums over all $K^T$ tag sequences, computed efficiently with the **forward algorithm** (the same dynamic program as Viterbi, with log-sum-exp instead of max); decoding is Viterbi. The lab implements it in about thirty lines of PyTorch.

### What the lab shows

Synthetic sentences with people, organizations and places, where the test set uses **entities never seen in training**, sentence-initial words are capitalized (so capitalization isn't a perfect signal), and 15% of entity tokens are lowercased, as in chat logs and tickets:

| model | token accuracy | entity F1 | sentences with invalid BIO |
|---|---|---|---|
| word identity (most frequent tag per word) | 0.66 | 0.00 | 0% |
| HMM + Viterbi | 0.78 | 0.41 | 0% |
| features, token by token | 0.89 | 0.70 | 29% |
| features + linear-chain CRF | 0.92 | 0.75 | 0% |

Token accuracy looks respectable for every model; entity F1 separates them. Models that rely on word identity can't recognize entities they haven't seen; features about shape and context can; and the CRF's transitions fix the incoherent sequences that the token-by-token model produces in almost a third of the sentences, which also improves F1.

**BiLSTM-CRF** models (Lample et al., 2016) replaced the handcrafted features with learned ones from a bidirectional LSTM (28.1), keeping the CRF on top; fine-tuned transformers (36.1) replaced those. The CRF layer and the entity-level evaluation stayed.

## When to use which

- **Bag of words or TF-IDF with a linear model**: topic, routing, spam, language ID, any task where a few words decide the answer. Always compute it as a baseline.
- **Fine-tuned small encoders** (36.1): when word order and context matter, and you have a few thousand labels.
- **An LLM with a prompt** (40.2): when you have no labels, the categories change often, or the task needs reasoning; at a cost per call, a latency, and an attack surface (44.1).
- For sequence labeling, a fine-tuned encoder with token classification is the default; a CRF on top helps when labels are scarce and span consistency matters.

> **Scar tissue.** A sentiment classifier I built on posts from a hacking forum was mysteriously bad at negative posts, which on that
> forum was most of them. The preprocessing removed stopwords with a standard list, and the standard list includes "not", "no" and "nor".
> "Not good" and "good" became the same document. Stopword lists were built for search engines in the days of tiny indexes, not for
> classifiers, and they're one of many defaults that are somebody else's assumptions about your problem.

## Things that will bite you

- **Fitting the vectorizer (vocabulary, IDF) on all data** before the split: mild leakage; worse when the test set has its own vocabulary.
- **Artifact features**: signatures, templates, IDs, dates that correlate with the label.
- **Token accuracy reported for NER.**
- **Invalid BIO sequences** from independent token classifiers, silently producing broken entities.
- **Tokenization mismatch** between the labeling tool and the model (a label on "New York" vs tokens "New", "York,").
- **A test set with the same entities as training**: it measures memory, not extraction.

6. Entity F1 (with precision and recall, per entity type), because the business uses entities, not tokens. Token accuracy is dominated by the O tokens, which are easy, and gives partial credit to half-found entities; a system that finds "Blue Harbor" but not "Bank" gets 2 of 3 tokens right and zero entities right.
