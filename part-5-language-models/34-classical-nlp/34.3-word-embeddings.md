# 34.3 Word embeddings: word2vec and GloVe

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 34.1, 34.2, 21.3 (SVD), 06.2 (mutual information), 30.1 (contrastive learning, negative sampling). Lab: [`lab_34_3_word_embeddings.py`](labs/lab_34_3_word_embeddings.py).*

In a bag of words, "car" and "automobile" are two unrelated dimensions, as far apart as "car" and "photosynthesis". Every model built on
that representation has to learn about each word separately, from the examples where that word appears. **Word embeddings** give each
word a dense vector, a few hundred numbers, placed so that words used in similar ways end up close together. Then what a model learns
about "car" partly transfers to "automobile" for free.

The principle behind all of them is a sentence from 1957: "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth). The **distributional
hypothesis**: words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings. This lesson builds embeddings two ways, by counting and by
prediction, finds that they're closer relatives than they look, and ends with why contextual embeddings (every transformer since 2018)
replaced them.

## Counting: co-occurrence, PPMI and SVD

Count how often each word appears within a few words of each other word: a $V \times V$ co-occurrence matrix. Raw counts are dominated by frequent words ("the" co-occurs with everything), so reweight with **pointwise mutual information**:

$$\text{PMI}(w, c) = \log\frac{P(w, c)}{P(w)\,P(c)},$$

how much more often $w$ and $c$ appear together than if they were independent (06.2). Negative values are noisy estimates of rare events, so clip them: **positive PMI** (PPMI). Each row is now a sparse, high-dimensional representation of a word; a **truncated SVD** (21.3) compresses it to a dense one, 100 to 300 dimensions. This is essentially latent semantic analysis (Deerwester et al., 1990) with better weighting.

## Predicting: word2vec

word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) trains vectors by prediction instead. **Skip-gram**: from a center word, predict the words around it. Each word $w$ gets an input vector $v_w$ and an output (context) vector $u_c$, and the probability of context $c$ given $w$ is a softmax over the vocabulary of $u_c^\top v_w$. Computing that softmax over 100,000 words for every pair is too expensive, so **negative sampling** replaces it with a binary task: tell the real context word apart from $k$ random "noise" words:

$$\log\sigma(u_c^\top v_w) + \sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\sigma(-u_{n_i}^\top v_w), \qquad n_i \sim P_{\text{noise}}.$$

That's a contrastive loss (30.1) for words. The details that made it work, and that the lab implements:

- The noise distribution is the unigram distribution raised to the **0.75** power, so rare words are sampled a bit more often than their frequency.
- **Subsampling**: frequent words are randomly dropped from the training text with a probability that grows with their frequency, which speeds training and improves the vectors of rarer words.
- **Dynamic windows** (nearer words count more) and $k$ = 5 to 20 negatives.

CBOW, the other variant, predicts the center word from the average of its context. **fastText** (Bojanowski et al., 2017) represents each word as the sum of its character n-gram vectors, so it produces vectors for unseen and misspelled words and handles morphology.

**GloVe** (Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014) sits between the two: a weighted least-squares fit of $v_w^\top u_c + b_w + b_c \approx \log X_{wc}$ to the co-occurrence counts, with a weighting function that limits the influence of very frequent pairs.

**They're the same thing, roughly.** Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed that skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI matrix, $\text{PMI}(w, c) - \log k$. And Levy, Goldberg and Dagan (2015) found that most of word2vec's advantage over count-based methods came from its preprocessing and hyperparameters (subsampling, the 0.75 smoothing, window weighting), not from the neural network: apply the same tricks to PPMI + SVD and it does about as well. The lab does exactly that, applying the 0.75 context smoothing and distance weighting to the counts, and on this course's 155,000 words the count-based vectors are better than word2vec by rank.

## Evaluating embeddings

- **Intrinsic**: word similarity datasets (human-rated pairs such as WordSim-353, SimLex-999) and **analogies** ("king − man + woman ≈ queen", tested by nearest neighbor to the vector arithmetic). The analogy results made word2vec famous; they need large corpora and are fragile (they depend on excluding the input words from the answer, and many "analogies" reduce to nearest neighbors of one word).
- **Extrinsic**: does a downstream task improve? That's the one that matters.

The lab's corpus is too small for analogies, so it uses 26 related pairs I picked from the course ("precision"/"recall", "sql"/"query", "tree"/"forest", "kafka"/"stream") against random pairs, and the rank of each word's partner among all 3,382 words. Random vectors put the partner at a median rank of about 1,800; PPMI + SVD at 5; word2vec at 45. On a corpus this small, the count-based vectors win. Nearest neighbors are the words the lessons use together: "gradient" is near "descent", "stochastic", "steepest"; "kafka" near "streams", "messages", "flink".

Two cautions from the same run:

- **Raw cosines aren't comparable across methods.** word2vec's random pairs have an average cosine of 0.76 in the lab, not 0: its vectors share a common direction (**anisotropy**), which inflates every cosine. Compare rankings, or center the vectors first. Contextual embeddings from transformers have the same problem, which matters when you set similarity thresholds for retrieval (41.1).
- **Embeddings measure association, not synonymity.** "Variance" is close to "bias", and "hot" is close to "cold" in general-corpus embeddings: antonyms share contexts.

For document classification, averaging word vectors (0.76 in the lab) doesn't beat TF-IDF (0.81): the average blurs away exactly which words were present. Embeddings pay off when the model must *compare* words: small training sets where synonyms matter, retrieval, and as input layers for neural models.

## Bias

Embeddings learn the associations in their training text, including the ones you'd never write into a system on purpose. Bolukbasi et al. (2016) found "man is to computer programmer as woman is to homemaker" in word2vec trained on news; Caliskan, Bryson and Narayanan (2017) showed that embeddings reproduce the associations measured by human implicit-association tests, including between names associated with ethnic groups and pleasant or unpleasant words. Debiasing by projecting out a "gender direction" reduces the obvious measures and leaves the information recoverable (Gonen and Goldberg, 2019).
Any system that ranks résumés, scores applicants or matches people using embeddings inherits this. Measure it on your task (54.1), don't assume it away. The course's own corpus has few gendered words, so the lab doesn't demonstrate it; the effect in general web text is large and well documented.

## From static to contextual

A static embedding gives "bank" one vector, whether it's a river bank or a savings bank: an average over senses. **Contextual embeddings** (ELMo, 2018, from a bidirectional LSTM language model; then BERT and every transformer, 36.1) compute a different vector for each occurrence, from the whole sentence. They replaced static vectors for almost everything. The ideas survived: a transformer's input embedding layer *is* a learned word (token) embedding table, the training objectives are still "predict words from their context", and the sentence embeddings used for search (41.1) are trained contrastively with negative sampling, word2vec's trick at the sentence level.

Static vectors are still useful when you need something tiny and fast (fastText for language identification, on-device features) and as an easy-to-inspect tool for exploring what a corpus associates with what.

## Things that will bite you

- **Comparing cosine values across embedding methods** (anisotropy).
- **Analogy benchmarks** as evidence of understanding.
- **Out-of-vocabulary words** at inference with a fixed word2vec vocabulary (fastText or subword tokenizers handle them).
- **Bias** inherited from the training corpus, in any system that affects people.
- **Averaged embeddings** assumed to beat TF-IDF for classification.
- **Domain mismatch**: general-web embeddings for legal, medical or security text, where words mean different things.

2. Why does skip-gram use negative sampling instead of a full softmax?

5. "Bank" appears as a financial institution 90% of the time and a river bank 10% in your corpus. What does its static embedding represent, and what changes with contextual embeddings?
6. A recruiting tool ranks résumés by the cosine similarity between their average word embedding and the job description's. What could go wrong?

Negative sampling replaces it with $k + 1$ binary classifications ($k$ around 5 to 20), a cost independent of $V$, and still produces good vectors (it optimizes a different objective, related to shifted PMI, not the softmax likelihood).

3. Sampling by raw frequency would make almost every negative a function word ("the", "of"), which teaches little; sampling uniformly would overweight very rare words. The 0.75 power flattens the distribution: frequent words are still sampled more often, but rare words more than their frequency would suggest. It was found empirically to work best.

4. Skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorizes a shifted pointwise mutual information matrix, so prediction-based and count-based methods learn closely related representations; most of word2vec's advantage came from its preprocessing and hyperparameters. Practically: with the same tricks, a PPMI + SVD pipeline (deterministic, fast, easy to inspect) is competitive, and hyperparameters matter more than the method.

5. The static vector is a frequency-weighted blend of both senses, dominated by the financial one: close to "loan" and "account", slightly toward "river". For a sentence about rivers it's the wrong representation. Contextual embeddings compute the vector from the surrounding sentence, so "bank" in "the river bank flooded" gets a representation near other geography words.

Averaging blurs the résumé into its general topic, so exact qualifications (a specific certification, a required skill) barely move the score, and verbose résumés with the job description's vocabulary win. Embeddings carry demographic associations from their training text (names, gendered words, schools, activities associated with groups), so the ranking can systematically disadvantage protected groups, which is a legal problem in many jurisdictions as well as an ethical one.
