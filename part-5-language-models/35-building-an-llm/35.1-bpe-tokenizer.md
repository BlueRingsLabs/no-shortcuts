# 35.1 Tokenization: BPE from scratch

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 34.1 (normalization, the long tail), 34.3 (why `<unk>` hurts). Lab: [`lab_35_1_bpe.py`](labs/lab_35_1_bpe.py).*

Every strange thing a language model does with spelling, arithmetic, trailing spaces, non-English text or the word "SolidGoldMagikarp"
starts here. The model never sees characters. It sees integers from a fixed vocabulary of 30,000 to 200,000 **tokens**, pieces of text
chosen by a compression algorithm run once, before training, on whatever data happened to be at hand. Then the model is trained on those
pieces, and it inherits their quirks for the rest of its life. You can't fix a tokenizer after pretraining without retraining the model.

This lesson builds the algorithm almost every modern LLM uses, byte-level **BPE**, and measures what it does to cost, to non-English
languages, and to numbers.

## Why subwords

Words (34.1) have an unbounded vocabulary and a long tail: every new name, typo and number is `<unk>`. Characters (or bytes) have a tiny vocabulary and no unknowns, but sequences become four or five times longer, and attention cost grows with the square of the length (29.3). **Subwords** are the compromise: frequent words are single tokens, rare words are split into frequent pieces ("tokenization" might be "token" + "ization"), and nothing is ever unknown.

## Byte pair encoding

BPE (Gage, 1994, as a compression algorithm; Sennrich, Haddow and Birch, 2016, for translation) builds the vocabulary bottom-up:

1. Start with a base vocabulary. **Byte-level** BPE (GPT-2, Radford et al., 2019) uses the 256 byte values, so any text, in any language, in any encoding mess, can be represented. No `<unk>`, ever.
2. **Pre-tokenize**: split the text into chunks with a regular expression (words with their leading space, runs of up to three digits, punctuation, whitespace), and never merge across chunks, so "dog." and "dog!" share " dog".
3. Count every adjacent pair of symbols across the corpus (weighted by chunk frequency).
4. Merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol, add it to the vocabulary, and record the merge.
5. Repeat until the vocabulary has the target size.

**Encoding** new text applies the recorded merges in the order they were learned; **decoding** concatenates the tokens' bytes. The lab implements it in about 60 lines, trained on this course: the first merges are " t", " a", "in", "he", " the"; after 2,000 merges the longest tokens are " regularization", " probabilities", " distribution" (the course's vocabulary), and 16 spaces (its code indentation).

Two things from building it that also bite in real tokenizers:

- **The pre-tokenizer must be lossless.** My first regex had a punctuation class that excluded "word" characters, and underscores are word characters that are neither letters nor digits: every `_` silently vanished, and the encode-decode round trip failed. Test `"".join(pattern.findall(s)) == s` on nasty strings.
- **Training must update incrementally** to be fast: recounting every pair after every merge is quadratic; updating only the chunks containing the merged pair takes the lab's 2,000 merges on 850 KB from minutes to 3 seconds. Production trainers (Hugging Face `tokenizers`, `tiktoken`'s training code, SentencePiece) are in Rust or C++ and do the same thing.

Other algorithms: **WordPiece** (BERT) picks merges by likelihood gain instead of frequency; **Unigram LM** (Kudo, 2018; SentencePiece's default) starts from a large vocabulary and prunes it, and can sample alternative segmentations as regularization. **SentencePiece** treats the input as a raw character stream with spaces as ordinary symbols (▁), which suits languages without spaces. The behaviors below apply to all of them.

## What tokenization does to cost and fairness

Compression is measured as bytes (or characters) per token. The lab's 2,000-merge tokenizer gets 2.7 bytes per token on held-out English lessons and 2.3 on Python code; production tokenizers with 100,000+ tokens get about 4 on English.

Tokens are what you pay for, what fills the context window, and what the model spends compute on. The same paragraph, written in English, Spanish and Chinese, through the lab's English-trained tokenizer:

| | characters | bytes | tokens |
|---|---|---|---|
| English | 379 | 379 | 124 |
| Spanish | 406 | 412 | 213 (1.7×) |
| Chinese | 128 | 384 | 384 (3.1×) |

Chinese gets no merges at all: every character costs its three UTF-8 bytes as three tokens. Real tokenizers trained on multilingual data narrow the gap without closing it: Petrov et al. (2023) measured differences of up to 15× between languages for the same content in commercial tokenizers. That's a price difference per request, a smaller effective context window, and usually worse quality (more steps to generate the same content, fewer examples per token in training) for the languages the tokenizer's data underrepresented. If your users write in more than one language, some of them are paying this tax right now.

## The weird parts

- **Numbers.** Pre-tokenization splits digits into chunks of up to three, and merges fall wherever the training data put them. In the lab, "2024" becomes "2", "02", "4" while " 2025" becomes " 202", "5". The model has to learn arithmetic on inconsistent, arbitrary chunks, which is part of why LLMs are bad at multi-digit arithmetic. Some tokenizers split numbers into single digits (Llama, PaLM) to make it consistent.
- **Leading spaces.** " the", "the" and " The" are three different tokens with three unrelated embeddings. A prompt ending in a space leaves the model to predict a token that starts without a space after it, which is rare in training: "trailing whitespace" degrades completions in subtle ways.
- **Case.** " THE" is three tokens where " the" is one. Shouting costs more.
- **Spelling.** "How many r's in strawberry" is hard because the model sees " straw" and "berry", not letters; it has to have learned the spelling of each token from context.
- **Glitch tokens.** Tokens that were frequent in the tokenizer's training data (a Reddit username, in GPT-2's famous case "SolidGoldMagikarp") but almost absent from the model's training data get embeddings that were barely trained. Prompting with them produced bizarre behavior. Check for undertrained tokens when you build a tokenizer on a different corpus than the model (Land and Bartolo, 2024).
- **Special tokens** (`<|endoftext|>`, chat-template markers): if user input can contain the literal string and your pipeline encodes it as the special token, users can forge message boundaries, a prompt-injection vector (44.1). Encode user text with special tokens disabled.

## Choosing a vocabulary size

Larger vocabularies compress better (fewer tokens per text, cheaper inference and longer effective contexts) and cost a larger embedding and output matrix ($V \times d$ each, 33.1) and more rarely seen tokens. Current models use 32,000 (Llama 2) to 128,000 (Llama 3) to 200,000+ (GPT-4o). Multilingual models need more. Train the tokenizer on data that looks like the model's training mix (35.3), and never change it after pretraining.

> **Scar tissue.** After a fine-tuning run on my home GPU, a model started producing fluent nonsense, and every metric I'd logged during
> training was fine. The training script had loaded the tokenizer from the base model's directory; my serving script loaded one saved with
> the checkpoint, which had two added special tokens. Every token id above a certain value was shifted by two. The model was fine; it was
> being spoken to in a slightly different language. Tokenizers are part of the model. Version them with the weights and test that they
> match.

## Things that will bite you

- **A lossy pre-tokenizer**: characters silently dropped.
- **Different tokenizers in training and serving** (a version bump, a different special-token setting).
- **Perplexity compared across tokenizers**: per-token numbers aren't comparable; use bits per byte (34.1).
- **Costs estimated in words**: estimate in tokens, per language.
- **Trailing spaces in prompts.**
- **Special tokens parsed from user input.**
- **Arithmetic and character-level tasks** assumed to be easy.

## Exercises

1. Run BPE by hand on the corpus "low low low lower lowest" (treat each word as a chunk, spaces removed) for three merges. What are the merges and the resulting vocabulary?
2. Why does byte-level BPE never need an `<unk>` token?
3. A 1,000-word English support ticket costs about 1,300 tokens. Estimate the cost of the same ticket in Chinese with the lab's tokenizer, and say what would change with a multilingual tokenizer.
4. Why does pre-tokenization split digits into groups of three (or single digits) rather than letting merges run freely over numbers?
5. How can the "strawberry" problem be explained in terms of tokens?
6. Your team extends a model's tokenizer with 5,000 domain-specific tokens before fine-tuning. What must happen to the model, and what's the risk?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Chunks: "low" ×3, "lower", "lowest" (as characters). Pair counts: (l,o) 5, (o,w) 5, (w,e) 2, (e,r) 1, (e,s) 1, (s,t) 1. Merge 1: (l,o) → "lo" (ties broken by order; (o,w) has the same count). Now (lo,w) 5: merge 2 → "low". Now (low,e) 2: merge 3 → "lowe". Vocabulary: the characters plus "lo", "low", "lowe"; "lower" is "lowe" + "r", "lowest" is "lowe" + "s" + "t".

2. Its base vocabulary is all 256 byte values, and every string is a sequence of bytes (in UTF-8). Anything not covered by a merge is left as individual bytes, which are always in the vocabulary.

3. The lab measured 3.1× as many tokens for the same content in Chinese, so about 4,000 tokens: three times the cost and context use. A multilingual tokenizer with Chinese in its training data would merge common characters and words, bringing it closer to English (typically 1.2× to 2× in commercial tokenizers, depending on the language), but rarely to parity.

4. Free merges over numbers would create tokens for whatever digit strings were frequent in the training data (years, prices, phone prefixes), splitting other numbers inconsistently and making the same digit mean different things in different positions. Fixed chunking (or single digits) gives numbers a consistent structure, which makes arithmetic and numeric comparison easier to learn.

5. The model receives tokens such as " straw" and "berry", not letters. Counting letters requires knowing each token's spelling, which the model only learns indirectly (from rare contexts that spell words out) and imperfectly. It's a question about a representation the model doesn't directly see.

6. The embedding matrix and output layer must grow by 5,000 rows, initialized somehow (the mean of existing embeddings, or the mean of the subword embeddings each new token replaces), and trained. Risks: the new tokens are undertrained compared with the old ones (glitch-token behavior); existing text now tokenizes differently wherever new tokens apply, shifting the distribution the model sees; and the fine-tuning data must contain the new tokens often enough. Measure whether the compression gain is worth it; often it isn't for fine-tuning.
