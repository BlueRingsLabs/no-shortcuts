# 35.4 Scaling laws and compute budgets

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 33.1 (FLOPs, $6ND$), 35.2, 16.2 (bias and variance), 05.1 (estimation and uncertainty). Lab: [`lab_35_4_scaling_laws.py`](labs/lab_35_4_scaling_laws.py).*

The most consequential empirical finding in modern machine learning is a straight line on a log-log plot. Train language models of
different sizes on different amounts of data, and their test loss falls as a smooth **power law** in parameters, data and compute, over
many orders of magnitude, with no sign of a plateau in the range anyone has measured. That regularity is why labs could decide to spend
hundreds of millions of dollars on a single training run: they could predict, from runs a thousand times smaller, roughly how good the
result would be.

It's also the most overinterpreted finding in the field. This lesson covers what the laws say, how to use them for an actual budget,
where the famous numbers came from and how uncertain they are, and why "emergent abilities" deserve suspicion.

## The laws

**Kaplan et al. (2020)**: loss scales as a power law in non-embedding parameters $N$, dataset size $D$ and compute $C$, each when the others aren't the bottleneck, and architecture details (depth vs width, heads) matter much less than scale. Their allocation rule said: with more compute, grow the model much faster than the data. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) was designed in that spirit.

**Hoffmann et al. (2022), "Chinchilla"**: with learning-rate schedules matched to each run's length (Kaplan's weren't), the optimal allocation is different. They fit

$$L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta},$$

an irreducible loss $E$ (the entropy of text, which no model beats) plus terms for finite model size and finite data. Minimizing $L$ subject to $C \approx 6ND$ (33.1) gives $N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{a}$ and $D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{b}$ with $a \approx b \approx 0.5$: **grow parameters and tokens equally**. Their headline number, **about 20 tokens per parameter**, came from their first two methods. Chinchilla itself (70B parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens, the same compute as the 280B Gopher) beat Gopher and GPT-3 across the board.

## What the numbers really are

The lab plugs in the published constants of Chinchilla's third method ($E = 1.69$, $A = 406.4$, $\alpha = 0.34$, $B = 410.7$, $\beta = 0.28$). With them, the compute-optimal ratio isn't 20: it climbs from 32 tokens per parameter at $10^{19}$ FLOPs to 122 at $10^{25}$, because $\alpha > \beta$. The three methods in the paper disagreed with each other, and Besiroglu et al. (2024) later showed that the published fit for the third one was flawed (their refit agrees with about 20).

The practical lesson isn't about Chinchilla: scaling-law constants are **estimates**, from one data mix, one tokenizer, one architecture family, one training recipe. Change any of them and the constants move. Use the laws for the shape of the tradeoff and for rough budgets, and fit your own for decisions that matter.

The lab shows how fragile a fit is at small scale. Four GPTs of increasing width, trained the same number of steps on this course, give validation losses of 5.41, 4.67, 4.12 and 3.65: clean monotone improvement. Fitting $E + A N^{-\alpha}$ gives a prediction for a model 30 times larger, with a bootstrap 90% interval of 2.84 to 2.99, which looks tight; the fitted irreducible loss $E$ ranges from 1.74 to 2.30. Four points over one order of magnitude constrain the trend nearby and hardly constrain the asymptote, which dominates any long extrapolation. Real scaling studies use dozens of runs across several orders of magnitude, and still argue.

## Budgeting a training run

The recipe (33.1 for the arithmetic):

1. Fix the compute budget: GPUs × days × achievable FLOP/s (with a realistic MFU).
2. Choose $N$ and $D$ from a scaling law, then **check that $D$ unique tokens exist** (35.3). If they don't, repeating data is fine up to about four epochs (Muennighoff et al., 2023), and falls off quickly after.
3. Run a sweep of small models (a hundredth to a thousandth of the budget) to tune the learning rate, batch size and data mix, and fit your own curve.
4. Extrapolate, then train the big model with frequent evaluation, and compare its loss curve against the prediction as it goes.

The lab compares real models with the (approach-3) frontier at their own compute:

| model | tokens per parameter | predicted loss | best achievable at the same compute |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-3 (175B, 300B tokens) | 2 | 2.002 | 1.954 |
| Chinchilla (70B, 1.4T) | 20 | 1.937 | 1.930 |
| Llama 2 7B (2T) | 286 | 2.020 | 2.014 |
| Llama 3 8B (15T) | 1,875 | 1.949 | 1.923 |

GPT-3 was far too large for its data. Llama 3 8B is the opposite, nearly 2,000 tokens per parameter, a worse use of *training* compute on purpose.

## Training once, serving forever

Chinchilla optimizes training compute. But a model is trained once and then used for trillions of tokens of inference, each costing about $2N$ FLOPs (33.1). Counting both, total cost is $6ND + 2N D_{\text{inference}}$, and for a target quality the optimum moves toward **smaller models trained on more data** (Sardana et al., 2023). The lab finds the cheapest way to reach Llama 2 7B's predicted loss: with no inference, a 12.7B model on 961B tokens; with $10^{14}$ lifetime inference tokens, a 3B model on 13.8 trillion tokens, 4,600 tokens per parameter. That's the economics behind every small, heavily trained open model: a few percent more training compute buys a model that is cheaper to serve for its whole life, and that fits on a phone or a single GPU.

## Emergence, and the metric problem

Some capabilities seem to appear suddenly with scale: near-zero accuracy on a task, then a jump (Wei et al., 2022, "emergent abilities"). Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) showed that many of these jumps are created by the **metric**. If per-token accuracy improves smoothly, then exact match on a 10-token answer, which needs all 10 tokens right, behaves like $p^{10}$: flat near zero for a long time, then a steep rise. The lab's example: per-token accuracy climbs smoothly from 0.03 to 0.97 over eight orders of magnitude of compute, while 10-token exact match reads 0.000, 0.001, 0.033, 0.217, 0.522: an "ability emerging" at $10^{23}$ FLOPs, created by the scoring.
With continuous metrics (per-token log-likelihood, partial credit), most such curves are smooth and predictable. Some genuine threshold effects may exist, but "emergent" should be the conclusion after checking the metric, not before. The practical consequence: when you evaluate small models to predict a big one (39.1), use smooth metrics.

## What scaling laws don't tell you

- **Downstream performance**: loss predicts benchmark scores only loosely, per task, and not across different data mixes.
- **Data quality**: better-filtered data (35.3) shifts the whole curve; the laws are conditional on the data.
- **What happens after pretraining**: post-training (38) changes behavior far more than a small difference in pretraining loss.
- **Inference-time compute**: reasoning models (38.4) trade inference compute for accuracy along a separate scaling curve.
- **Whether the lines continue**: every extrapolation eventually leaves the measured range. Treat claims about models ten times larger than anything measured as forecasts, not facts.

## Things that will bite you

- **Quoting "20 tokens per parameter"** as a law of nature.
- **Extrapolating a fit** from a narrow range of small runs, especially the irreducible term.
- **Comparing losses across tokenizers or data mixes** in a scaling study.
- **Planning a compute-optimal run** without checking that the unique data exists.
- **Ignoring inference cost** when choosing a model size.
- **"Emergent" results measured with exact match.**
- **Learning rate schedules not matched to run length** (Kaplan's issue): short runs look worse than they should.

## Exercises

1. With $C = 6ND$ and the rule of 20 tokens per parameter, what model size and token count are compute-optimal for $C = 10^{23}$ FLOPs?
2. Why did Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. reach different conclusions about allocation?
3. A team has 50 billion unique tokens of domain text and wants a compute-optimal model. How large should it be, and what if they want a larger model?
4. Why might a company deliberately train a 7B model on 15 trillion tokens instead of a compute-optimal larger model?
5. A capability shows 0% accuracy at 1B and 10B parameters and 40% at 100B, measured by exact match. What would you check before calling it emergent?
6. Using $L = E + A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta$, explain what happens to the benefit of more parameters when $D$ is held fixed.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $D = 20N$, so $C = 6N \cdot 20N = 120N^2$: $N = \sqrt{10^{23}/120} \approx 2.9 \times 10^{10}$ (29B parameters) and $D \approx 5.8 \times 10^{11}$ (580B tokens).

2. Kaplan et al. used a fixed learning-rate schedule length across runs, so their shorter runs weren't trained to the end of their cosine schedule and looked worse, which biased the fit toward "more parameters" rather than "more data". They also counted non-embedding parameters and used smaller models. Hoffmann et al. matched each run's schedule to its length and covered larger scales, finding equal scaling of parameters and data.

3. At 20 tokens per parameter, 50B tokens support about 2.5B parameters compute-optimally with one epoch. With about 4 epochs of repetition (200B effective tokens) they could go to about 10B with modest loss of efficiency. A much larger model would be data-limited: the $B/D^\beta$ term dominates, and extra parameters buy little; mixing in general-domain data or synthetic data are the usual options.

4. Inference cost: a smaller model is cheaper and faster to serve for its whole lifetime, fits on smaller hardware (a single GPU, a laptop, a phone), and at very high usage the extra training compute is repaid quickly. Also: a fixed deployment size target, and continued gains from more data at small size (the loss keeps improving, just less per FLOP than a bigger model would).

5. Look at a continuous metric (per-token log-likelihood of the correct answer, partial credit) across the same models: if it improves smoothly, the "emergence" is the exact-match threshold. Check more model sizes in between; check whether the task's answer length or format makes success multiplicative; check for contamination in the largest model's data (39.1); and check variance across prompts and seeds.

6. With $D$ fixed, $B/D^\beta$ is a constant floor. More parameters shrink only $A/N^\alpha$, so the loss approaches $E + B/D^\beta$ from above, with diminishing returns: each doubling of $N$ reduces the parameter term by the factor $2^{-\alpha} \approx 0.79$, and once it's small next to the data term, extra parameters buy almost nothing.
