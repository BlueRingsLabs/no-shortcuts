# 35.3 Pretraining data

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 35.1, 34.1 (n-grams, perplexity), 34.2 (text classifiers), 15.2 (privacy and lineage), 12.1 (distributed processing). Lab: [`lab_35_3_pretraining_data.py`](labs/lab_35_3_pretraining_data.py).*

Ask anyone who has trained a competitive language model what mattered most and they'll say the data, then change the subject, because
the data pipeline is the part companies keep secret. The architecture of a 2024 model is a few tweaks on a 2019 one (35.5); the training
code is public; the difference between a good model and a mediocre one of the same size is mostly what went into it and what was thrown
out. Llama 3's report says as much, and FineWeb's authors showed that careful filtering of the same Common Crawl snapshots produced
measurably better models at equal compute.

It's also the most engineering-heavy part of the whole stack: petabytes of HTML, billions of documents, a dozen processing stages, each
one a tradeoff between quality, diversity and who gets left out. This lesson walks through the stages, and the lab runs each one on a small
synthetic crawl with known ground truth, so you can see what every filter catches and what it breaks.

## Where the text comes from

- **Web crawls**: Common Crawl (petabytes, monthly snapshots since 2008) is the backbone of almost every open dataset: C4, RefinedWeb, FineWeb, DCLM, Dolma. Mostly junk before filtering.
- **Curated sources**: Wikipedia, books, academic papers (arXiv, PubMed), patents, legal text, Stack Exchange.
- **Code**: GitHub and similar (The Stack), with license filtering and deduplication; heavily weighted in current models because it helps reasoning.
- **Math and synthetic data**: math web pages, and increasingly text generated by other models (rephrased web pages, textbooks, exercises), which brings its own risks (model collapse when models train on their own outputs; license terms of the generating model).
- **Licensed data** from publishers and platforms, and the ongoing legal fights over everything that wasn't licensed. Know your jurisdiction and your data's provenance (54.3).

## The pipeline

A modern web pipeline (FineWeb and RefinedWeb describe theirs in detail):

1. **Extraction**: turn HTML into text. Tools such as trafilatura remove navigation, ads and boilerplate; the choice of extractor changes downstream model quality noticeably. Keep the URL and crawl date for everything.
2. **URL filtering**: blocklists of adult, spam and malware domains.
3. **Language identification**: fastText's language classifier on each document; keep the languages you want, with a confidence threshold.
4. **Quality heuristics**: rules from Gopher (Rae et al., 2021) and C4 (Raffel et al., 2020): document length, mean word length, fraction of symbols, fraction of lines ending in punctuation, repeated lines and n-grams, presence of common stopwords, "lorem ipsum", curly braces (for non-code), bullet-heavy pages.
5. **Deduplication**: exact (hashes of documents or lines) and **near-duplicate** (MinHash + LSH), within and across crawl snapshots.
6. **Model-based filtering**: a classifier or a small language model scores "quality", and documents below a threshold are dropped.
7. **PII removal and safety filtering**: emails, phone numbers, IP addresses, keys; toxic and sexual content classifiers.
8. **Decontamination** against the benchmarks you'll evaluate on.
9. **Mixing**: decide how much of each source and language goes into training, and in what order.

## What the lab's pipeline shows

The synthetic crawl has 780 documents: 400 good paragraphs (from this course), boilerplate, keyword spam, gibberish, Spanish pages, exact and near duplicates, planted PII and leaked benchmark questions.

**Heuristics** remove 97% of the junk and none of the good text, and each removal has a reason you can read (spam: "no stopwords" and "repetitive"; boilerplate: "lines without punctuation"). They also remove **70% of the Spanish pages**, through the "at least two English stopwords" rule. That's the general lesson about heuristic filters: each rule encodes assumptions about what good text looks like, usually for one language and one register, and applies them to everything. Filters are where multilingual datasets quietly lose languages, and where dialects, informal writing and some communities get filtered out as "low quality" (Dodge et al., 2021, documented this in C4, whose blocklist removed text about and by LGBTQ+ people and African American English disproportionately).

**Language ID** from character trigrams, with about 500 characters of each language as training data, gets every Spanish page and misclassifies no English ones: languages are very distinctive at the character level. Real classifiers cover 176+ languages, and struggle with short texts, closely related languages and code-switching.

**Deduplication.** Exact hashing of normalized text finds the 60 planted duplicates (and 34 more from the junk generators, which repeat themselves). Near-duplicates need **MinHash**: represent each document by its set of word 5-grams ("shingles"); the Jaccard similarity of two sets equals the probability that their minimum hash values agree under a random hash function, so 128 min-hashes estimate it. **Locality-sensitive hashing** splits the signature into bands (here 32 bands of 4) and only compares documents that collide in at least one band: 649 candidate pairs instead of 303,810, and 100% recall of the planted near-duplicates. Why deduplicate at all:

- duplicated text is **memorized** (Carlini et al., 2021 and 2023 extract training data verbatim, and duplication makes it far more likely), which is a privacy and copyright problem;
- duplicates waste compute and skew the model toward whatever was repeated (boilerplate, templates, SEO spam);
- Lee et al. (2022) showed deduplicated training data gives models that are as good or better with fewer steps, and that train-test overlap from duplicates inflated benchmark scores.

**PII scrubbing** with regular expressions catches all 30 planted emails, phone numbers, IP addresses and AWS-style keys, and also replaces "version 2.10.3.1", "1.1.1.1" and "3.14.15.92" with `<IPV4>`: regexes catch the formats you thought of, plus things that look like them. Names, addresses and anything unstructured need NER models (34.2) and still leak. Treat any web-scale corpus as containing personal data and secrets; the model trained on it can regurgitate them (53.1, 54.2).
As a practical matter from the security side: web crawls contain enormous numbers of leaked API keys and credentials, and models trained on them have been shown to emit working ones. Scrub key formats aggressively, and never assume the model "doesn't remember".

**Contamination.** A model whose training data contains a benchmark's test questions is partly being tested on memory. GPT-3's check flags a test item if any 13-gram from it appears in the training data (the lab uses 8-grams for its short questions). It catches 9 of the 10 planted questions, with no false positives among the 20 clean ones; the one it misses has only 5 words, too short to contain an 8-gram at all. N-gram checks also miss paraphrased, translated or reformatted leaks. Contamination detection has a recall problem, which is one reason benchmarks go stale and newer evaluation uses held-out or freshly written items (39.1).

**Classifier-based filtering.** Train a classifier to separate trusted "reference" text from random crawl text, and keep what scores like the reference (GPT-3 did this with WebText as reference; DCLM and FineWeb-Edu are the current, carefully engineered versions, the latter using an LLM to label educational value). In the lab: at threshold 0.5 it keeps 58% of the good pages and 2% of the junk; at 0.3, 94% and 24%. Because the negatives were a random crawl sample, half of which is good text, the classifier is unsure about good pages, and the threshold decides the precision/recall tradeoff. Choosing negatives and thresholds is most of the work.
And it learned what the reference looks like, which includes "English, about machine learning, written by one person": it keeps 0% of the Spanish pages. A filter trained on one voice keeps text that sounds like that voice. Decide what "quality" means before a classifier decides for you, and measure what each filter removes, by language, domain and source.

## Mixing and scheduling

The final dataset is a **mixture**: web text, code, math, books, papers, multilingual data, in proportions chosen by experiments at small scale (DoReMi and data-mixing laws formalize it). Choices that matter:

- **Upsampling** high-quality sources (several epochs of Wikipedia and books, one of the web).
- **Code and math proportions**: more than their natural share, for reasoning.
- **Multilingual share and the tokenizer**: the tokenizer (35.1) should be trained on the same mixture.
- **Annealing**: many recent models end pretraining with a phase on the highest-quality data (and some instruction-like data) at a decaying learning rate, which improves benchmark scores noticeably.
- **Repetition**: with limited unique data, a few epochs are nearly as good as fresh data; beyond about four, returns fall off quickly (Muennighoff et al., 2023).

> **Scar tissue.** I once continued pretraining a small model on a few hundred million tokens I'd scraped from Spanish-language tech
> forums, and it got worse at everything I cared about. A sample of the data explained why: thousands of copies of the same forum
> signatures, navigation menus, and one legal disclaimer that appeared about forty thousand times. Deduplication and boilerplate removal
> cut the corpus by a third and the model improved. More data is only better when it's more information.

## Things that will bite you

- **Filters that encode one language or dialect**, applied to everything.
- **No deduplication**: memorization, wasted compute, inflated benchmarks.
- **Regex-only PII scrubbing**, reported as "PII removed".
- **Benchmarks in the training data**, discovered after the press release.
- **Undocumented provenance and licenses**: you can't answer "what was this trained on?" when legal or a regulator asks (54.3).
- **Synthetic data from another model** without checking its license or its failure modes.
- **Changing the pipeline without re-measuring**: every filter changes the mixture.

## Exercises

1. Why does MinHash estimate Jaccard similarity? Give the one-line argument.
2. With 32 bands of 4 rows, what's the probability that two documents with Jaccard similarity 0.8 become candidates? With similarity 0.3?
3. Name three heuristic quality rules and one kind of legitimate text each would wrongly remove.
4. How would you check whether a model you're evaluating was trained on your benchmark, if you can't see its training data?
5. Your quality classifier uses Wikipedia as the positive class. What biases does that introduce?
6. Design the PII-handling part of a pretraining pipeline for a model that will be released publicly.

1. For a random hash function (a random permutation of the shingle universe), the element of $A \cup B$ with the minimum hash is equally likely to be any element of the union; the two sets' minima agree exactly when that element is in $A \cap B$. So $P(\min h(A) = \min h(B)) = |A \cap B| / |A \cup B|$.

2. A band matches with probability $s^4$; the pair becomes a candidate if at least one of 32 bands matches: $1 - (1 - s^4)^{32}$. For $s = 0.8$: $0.8^4 = 0.41$, $1 - 0.59^{32} \approx 1.0$ (essentially certain). For $s = 0.3$: $0.3^4 = 0.0081$, $1 - 0.9919^{32} \approx 0.23$. The band/row choice sets where this S-curve rises.

3. Minimum length (removes short poems, recipes, product specifications); "at least N stopwords" in a language's list (removes other languages, code, tables, lists); fraction of lines ending in punctuation (removes poetry, song lyrics, structured lists and many forums); symbol ratio (removes math and code); blocklisted words (removes health information, LGBTQ+ content and reclaimed language, as documented for C4).

4. Membership inference-style tests: compare the model's likelihood (perplexity) on the benchmark items with its likelihood on comparable held-out items written after its training cutoff, or on perturbed versions of the items (paraphrases, reordered answer options); much lower loss on the originals suggests exposure. Prompt it with the beginning of a test item and check whether it completes it verbatim; check whether it reproduces the canonical answer order. And evaluate on fresh items that couldn't have been in its data.

5. It rewards text that resembles Wikipedia: encyclopedic, formal, English (or the language of that Wikipedia), on the topics and from the perspectives Wikipedia covers well, written by a demographically skewed editor community. It penalizes informal but valuable text (forums, how-tos, conversation), other dialects and languages, fiction, code, and topics underrepresented there. The model's knowledge and style shift accordingly.

6. Detection with several methods: regexes for structured formats (emails, phones, IPs, credit cards with checksum validation, national IDs per country, API key and token formats with provider-specific prefixes and entropy checks), NER for names and addresses, and deduplication (repeated PII is what gets memorized). Decide per type: remove, mask with placeholders, or keep (public figures, business contact pages). Special handling for secrets: remove and, if found at scale, notify providers. Measure precision and recall on a hand-labeled sample, per language. Test the trained model for extraction (prompting with prefixes of known PII, canary strings inserted before training). Document the process, honor removal requests, and assume residual risk: combine with output filtering at serving time.
