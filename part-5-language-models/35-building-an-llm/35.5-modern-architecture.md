Open the architecture section of almost any open-weights model released since 2023 and you'll find the same list: a decoder-only
transformer with pre-normalization using RMSNorm, rotary position embeddings, SwiGLU feed-forward layers, grouped-query attention, no bias
terms, and, in the largest ones, mixture-of-experts layers. The GPT-2 block of 29.2 with five or six substitutions. Each substitution
earned its place in ablations at scale, each is worth a few percent, and none of them explains why one model is good and another is
mediocre. That's data (35.3), scale (35.4) and post-training (38).

Still, you need to know what's in the block to read a model card, estimate memory, port weights, or debug a fine-tune. The lab builds a
Llama-style block from parts, trains it against a GPT-2 block, and runs an ablation that shows where the difference actually came from at
small scale.

## The substitutions

**RMSNorm** (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019): $x / \text{RMS}(x) \cdot g$, LayerNorm without subtracting the mean and without a bias. Cheaper (one reduction instead of two, fewer parameters), and as good in practice. Applied pre-norm (29.2), with a final RMSNorm before the output layer.

**RoPE** (29.3): rotate query and key pairs by position-dependent angles, so attention scores depend on relative position. No learned position table, better length behavior, and extendable with interpolation methods (position interpolation, YaRN) for long contexts. The **base** (10,000 in the original, 500,000 in Llama 3) sets the slowest rotation frequency and is raised for long-context models.

**SwiGLU** (Shazeer, 2020): the MLP becomes $W_2(\text{SiLU}(W_1x) \odot W_3x)$, a gated linear unit with a SiLU (Swish) gate. Three matrices instead of two, so the hidden size is cut from $4d$ to about $\frac{8}{3}d$ to keep the parameter count. It trains to a slightly better loss for the same compute.

**Grouped-query attention** (Ainslie et al., 2023; 29.3): $h$ query heads share $h_{kv} < h$ key/value heads. It barely affects quality and shrinks the KV cache by $h/h_{kv}$, which is what limits long-context and high-throughput inference (37.2). Llama 2 70B and later use 8 KV heads. **Multi-head latent attention** (DeepSeek-V2) compresses keys and values into a low-rank latent instead, shrinking the cache further.

**No biases** in the linear layers: fewer parameters, slightly better stability, no measurable loss.

Other common details: tied or untied embeddings (29.2; large models usually untie), **QK-norm** (normalizing queries and keys before the dot product, to stop attention logits from growing and causing loss spikes), a **z-loss** term that penalizes large output logits for the same reason, sliding-window attention in some layers (Mistral, Gemma) to bound cost, and vocabulary sizes of 128k or more (35.1).

## What the lab shows

A GPT-2 block at width 512 has 3.15M parameters; a Llama-style block with 8 query heads and 2 KV heads has 2.75M (the savings are in $W_K$, $W_V$ and the biases). At width 128, trained for 400 steps on this course with the same data and schedule:

| block | parameters | validation loss |
|---|---|---|
| GPT-2 (LayerNorm, learned positions, GELU, multi-head attention) | 1.12M | 4.161 |
| GPT-2 with RoPE instead of learned positions | 1.11M | 3.798 |
| Llama-style (RMSNorm, RoPE, SwiGLU, GQA 4/2, no biases) | 1.04M | 3.810 |

The Llama-style block wins by 0.35 nats, which is far more than published ablations report at scale, so I ran the ablation. **RoPE alone accounts for all of it** (0.36 nats): at this tiny scale and short budget, learning 64 position embeddings from scratch is a real handicap, and a relative position scheme that works from step one is a head start. The other changes are a wash here ($-0.01$ nats) with 7% fewer parameters.
If I had reported only the first and last rows, you'd have concluded that "the modern block is much better", which is true in the wrong way. Ablate one change at a time before you attribute a gain.

## Mixture of experts

A dense model uses all its parameters for every token. A **mixture-of-experts** layer replaces the MLP with $E$ experts (each an MLP) and a **router**, a linear layer followed by a softmax, that sends each token to its top-$k$ experts ($k$ = 1 or 2, sometimes 8 of 256 very small ones), combining their outputs weighted by the router probabilities (Shazeer et al., 2017; GShard; Switch Transformer). Total parameters scale with $E$; compute per token scales with $k$. The lab's model with 8 experts and top-2 routing has 1.70M total parameters and about 0.82M active per token.

Why it's used: at a fixed compute budget, a sparse model with more total parameters reaches a lower loss than a dense one (Mixtral 8x7B, DeepSeek-V3 with 671B total and 37B active, and most frontier models, reportedly). What it costs:

- **Memory**: all experts must be stored, so a 671B-parameter MoE needs 671B parameters' worth of memory to serve, even though each token uses 37B.
- **Communication**: experts live on different GPUs (**expert parallelism**, 33.2), and each layer routes tokens with an all-to-all exchange.
- **Load balancing**: a router can learn to favor a few experts, which then get more training and more traffic, and the rest waste memory (and overflow capacity limits, dropping tokens). The standard fix is an **auxiliary loss**, $E \sum_i f_i P_i$ (fraction of tokens routed to expert $i$ times its mean router probability), minimized by uniform use; DeepSeek-V3 instead adjusts per-expert bias terms in the routing ("auxiliary-loss-free" balancing).

In the lab's short run the router didn't collapse badly even without the auxiliary loss: the busiest expert handled 17% of routing slots (12.5% is uniform), and none was idle. With a small balancing weight, the busiest took 15%. Collapse is a bigger problem with many experts, top-1 routing and long training; at this scale the loss was cheap insurance, not a rescue. I'd rather show you that than a contrived collapse.

## Reading a model card

With this vocabulary, a model's config file is readable: `hidden_size` ($d$), `num_hidden_layers` ($L$), `num_attention_heads` and `num_key_value_heads` (GQA), `intermediate_size` (the SwiGLU hidden size), `rope_theta` (the RoPE base), `rms_norm_eps`, `vocab_size`, `tie_word_embeddings`, and for MoE `num_experts` and `num_experts_per_tok`. From those you can compute the parameter count (29.2), the KV cache per token (29.3), the training and inference FLOPs (33.1), and whether it fits on your hardware, before downloading anything.

## Things that will bite you

- **Porting weights between implementations** with different conventions: RoPE pairing (interleaved vs split halves), fused vs separate QKV projections, gate and up projections swapped in SwiGLU.
- **The wrong `rope_theta`** or RoPE scaling setting when loading a long-context model: fine on short prompts, garbage on long ones.
- **GQA head counts** mismatched when repeating K and V.
- **Attributing gains to the wrong change**: ablate one at a time.
- **MoE memory**: planning for active parameters when all of them must be loaded.
- **MoE fine-tuning** without the load-balancing loss (or with a different capacity factor than pretraining).

## Exercises

1. Show that SwiGLU with hidden size $\frac{8}{3}d$ has the same parameter count as a GELU MLP with hidden size $4d$ (no biases).
2. A model has 32 layers, 32 query heads and 8 KV heads of dimension 128. What's its KV cache per token in bf16, and how much larger would it be with full multi-head attention?
3. Why does RMSNorm work as well as LayerNorm despite not centering?
4. An MoE model has 64 experts, top-2 routing, and each expert has 100M parameters, plus 3B shared parameters. What are the total and active parameter counts?
5. Why does an MoE router tend to collapse without a balancing mechanism?
6. The lab's first comparison showed a 0.35-nat gain for the modern block. Why run the ablation, and what would you have concluded without it?

1. GELU MLP: $d \times 4d + 4d \times d = 8d^2$. SwiGLU: three matrices of $d \times \frac{8}{3}d$: $3 \times \frac{8}{3}d^2 = 8d^2$.

3. The model is pre-norm, so what matters is controlling the scale of the input to each sub-layer; the mean is largely irrelevant for that, and the following linear layer can absorb a shift. Empirically the re-centering contributes little, and removing it saves compute. (Zhang and Sennrich found the rescaling invariance is what matters.)

4. Total: $3\text{B} + 64 \times 100\text{M} = 9.4$B. Active per token: $3\text{B} + 2 \times 100\text{M} = 3.2$B (per MoE layer structure as stated).

5. A rich-get-richer loop: an expert that the router slightly prefers early on receives more tokens, gets more gradient updates, becomes better at those tokens, and is preferred even more. Rarely chosen experts stay undertrained and are chosen less. Nothing in the language modeling loss rewards using experts evenly, so the balancing has to be added.

6. Because the two models differed in five ways at once (normalization, positions, MLP, attention heads, biases), and the gain was much larger than published results at scale, which suggested one change dominated or something was off. Without the ablation you'd attribute the gain to "the modern block" as a whole, and perhaps adopt SwiGLU or GQA believing they matter a lot here; the ablation shows it was entirely RoPE (a head start on positions at a short budget), and the other changes were neutral at this scale.
