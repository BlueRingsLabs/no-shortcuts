Chat models get the attention; encoders do a lot of the work. The search box that understands what you meant, the spam filter, the model
that routes a support ticket, extracts a contract's parties, flags a transaction description or detects a toxic comment: very often a
fine-tuned BERT-family encoder with 100 to 400 million parameters, running for a fraction of a cent per thousand requests, on a CPU if needed,
with latency in milliseconds. When the task is "read this and label it" and you have a few thousand examples, an encoder is often more
accurate, far cheaper and far easier to control than prompting a large generative model. It's also the ancestor of the embedding models
behind every retrieval system (41.1).

## BERT: masked language modeling

GPT reads left to right and predicts the next token. **BERT** (Devlin et al., 2019) reads the whole sequence at once, with no causal mask, and is trained to reconstruct tokens that were hidden:

- Choose 15% of the positions. Of those, replace 80% with a `[MASK]` token, 10% with a random token, and leave 10% unchanged. Compute the loss only at the chosen positions.
- The random and unchanged cases matter: at fine-tuning time there are no `[MASK]` tokens, so the model must build good representations for every position, not only for masked ones.
- The original also had a **next sentence prediction** objective (is sentence B the real continuation of A?), which RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019) showed was useless; RoBERTa dropped it, trained longer on more data with bigger batches and dynamic masking, and did much better. Most of BERT's early gains over its successors were training recipe, not architecture.

Every position's output vector depends on the entire input, left and right: a **contextual embedding** (34.3's endpoint). A special `[CLS]` token at the start is conventionally used as a summary of the whole sequence for classification.

**What the lab found.** A tiny encoder and a tiny decoder of the same size, trained for the same steps on this course, then asked to fill in a hidden token in the middle of validation text: the encoder, which sees both sides, gets 28.6% accuracy on 2,000 windows against 29.5% for the decoder, which sees only the left side, and a higher loss. The paired difference is −0.9 points with a 95% interval of −3.2 to +1.4: no measurable advantage for twice the context. (An earlier run on 400 windows put the encoder three points ahead; that was noise, which is why the lab now computes the interval.) The reason is sample efficiency: in each step, the encoder learned from the 15% of tokens it masked, the decoder from every token. **ELECTRA** (Clark et al., 2020) fixed exactly this: a small generator replaces some tokens, and the encoder is trained to detect which tokens were replaced, at every position, reaching BERT's quality with a fraction of the compute.

Another finding from building it: my first encoder used learned position embeddings and never left the **unigram plateau** (validation loss stuck near 5.6 nats) in the lab's budget, while the decoder trained normally. A masked position carries no information about its own token, so the encoder must learn to read its neighbors, and learning where the neighbors are through learned absolute positions is slow. With RoPE (29.3, 35.5), the same encoder got past the plateau in a few hundred steps. Masked-LM training is known for a long initial plateau; the position scheme is part of it.

**Why BERT can't generate text**: it was never trained to produce a sequence token by token from the left. You can sample from it by iterative unmasking, badly. Generation is the decoder's job.

## T5: everything is text to text

**T5** (Raffel et al., 2020) is an encoder-decoder (29.2): the encoder reads the input bidirectionally, the decoder generates the output with cross-attention to the encoder. Every task is phrased as text in, text out: "translate English to German: ...", "summarize: ...", "cola sentence: ..." with the answer as a string. Its pretraining objective is **span corruption**: replace random spans of the input with sentinel tokens and train the decoder to produce the missing spans:

- input: `Thank <extra_id_0> me to your party last week , it was a great evening and <extra_id_1> excellent`
- target: `<extra_id_0> you for inviting <extra_id_1> the food was <extra_id_2>`

The targets are short, which makes pretraining cheaper than reconstructing the whole input. The T5 paper is also a large, careful empirical study of pretraining objectives, data (it introduced C4, 35.3), architectures and fine-tuning strategies, and still one of the best references for "what actually matters". Encoder-decoders remain strong for translation, summarization and other input-to-output tasks (Flan-T5 is a widely used instruction-tuned version), though decoder-only models have taken over general-purpose use.

## Fine-tuning for classification

Add a small head (a linear layer) on the `[CLS]` output, or on the mean of the token outputs, and train the whole network on labeled examples with a small learning rate (2e-5 to 5e-5 for base-size models; 27.3's advice about gentle fine-tuning applies), for 2 to 5 epochs, with warmup. That's it; the recipe is very robust.

The lab fine-tunes its tiny encoder on 34.2's task (which Part of this course a paragraph comes from): 0.53 accuracy when pretrained with masked LM, against 0.58 from random initialization, a difference of −0.06 with a paired 95% interval of −0.14 to +0.03 on 161 test paragraphs. At this toy scale, pretraining makes no measurable difference (an earlier version of this lab reported the opposite sign with the same code: 161 test items can't settle a five-point question). TF-IDF with logistic regression gets about 0.81, far better than both, because the tiny encoder was pretrained on 350,000 tokens and reads only the first 63 tokens of each paragraph. With a real pretrained encoder (billions of tokens), fine-tuning on a few hundred to a few thousand labels typically beats bag-of-words clearly on tasks where word order and context matter, and ties on keyword tasks. Always keep the linear baseline in the comparison.

Choices that matter in practice:

- **Model**: DeBERTa-v3 and ModernBERT are strong current encoders; distilled models (DistilBERT, MiniLM) for low latency; multilingual models (XLM-R, mDeBERTa) for other languages.
- **Maximum length**: most encoders handle 512 tokens (ModernBERT 8,192). Longer documents need chunking (classify chunks, then aggregate) or a long-context model.
- **Class imbalance**: weighted loss, and thresholds chosen on validation data (18.3).
- **Small data**: few-shot methods such as SetFit (contrastive fine-tuning of a sentence encoder, then a small head) work with dozens of labels.

## Token classification

For NER (34.2) and similar tasks, add a linear layer on *every* token's output. The complication is subwords: labels are per word, the model sees BPE pieces. The standard convention: give each word's label to its **first piece** and ignore the rest in the loss (label -100 in PyTorch), then read predictions from first pieces. The lab shows the alignment on a sentence where "Globex" (lowercased as "globex") becomes four pieces: one labeled B-ORG, three ignored. Giving all pieces the same B- tag, or misaligning labels after truncation, are classic bugs that quietly cost F1. A CRF on top (34.2) can help with small data.

## Sentence embeddings

A pretrained encoder's `[CLS]` vector or mean-pooled output is a poor sentence embedding out of the box: similarities are dominated by anisotropy (34.3). **Sentence-BERT** (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) fine-tunes the encoder so that similar sentences have similar pooled vectors, with pairs from NLI data; current embedding models (E5, BGE, GTE, and many others) are trained contrastively (30.1) on hundreds of millions of pairs (queries and passages, titles and bodies, questions and answers), with in-batch negatives and hard negatives. They're the retrieval half of RAG, covered properly in 41.1.

> **Scar tissue.** A friend finishing law school built a dataset of rental contracts and asked me for a clause classifier. The BERT-style
> model was great at most categories and blind to early termination clauses. The contracts were long, the tokenizer truncated them at 512
> tokens, and in that city's standard contracts the termination terms sat near the end. The model had never seen a single one. Chunking
> with a sliding window and pooling over chunks fixed it. When a model is inexplicably bad at one thing, check whether the thing ever
> reached it.

## Things that will bite you

- **Fine-tuning with a large learning rate**: wrecks the pretrained representations (27.3).
- **Truncation** silently dropping the part of the document that holds the answer.
- **Label misalignment** between words and subword pieces in token classification.
- **Using raw `[CLS]` vectors for similarity search** instead of a trained sentence-embedding model.
- **The wrong tokenizer** for the checkpoint (it must be the one it was pretrained with).
- **No linear baseline**: an expensive encoder that TF-IDF matches.
- **Evaluating on data the base model saw in pretraining** (public benchmarks).

## Exercises

1. Why does BERT replace only 80% of the chosen tokens with `[MASK]`?
2. Why is a masked-LM encoder less sample-efficient than a causal decoder, and how does ELECTRA address it?
3. Write the T5 input and target for masking "brown fox" and "the lazy" in "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog".
4. You fine-tune BERT-base on 3,000 labeled tickets and accuracy is worse than TF-IDF + logistic regression. List four things to check.
5. The word "Kowalski" is tokenized into three pieces. What labels do the pieces get for training, and how do you read the prediction?
6. When would you choose a fine-tuned encoder over prompting a large generative model, and vice versa?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. If every chosen position showed `[MASK]`, the model would learn that only `[MASK]` positions need a prediction and that visible tokens can be trusted as-is, so its representations of ordinary (visible) tokens would be weaker, and there would be a mismatch with fine-tuning, where `[MASK]` never appears. The random 10% forces the model to question every visible token; the unchanged 10% biases representations toward the actual observed word.

2. The encoder's loss comes only from the masked positions (15%), so each training step provides about one seventh of the prediction signal a decoder gets from the same text, where every position predicts its next token. ELECTRA trains a discriminator to classify every token as original or replaced (by a small generator), so the loss covers every position, and it reaches the same quality with much less compute.

3. Input: `the quick <extra_id_0> jumps over <extra_id_1> dog`; target: `<extra_id_0> brown fox <extra_id_1> the lazy <extra_id_2>`.

4. The learning rate and schedule (too high, no warmup), the number of epochs and early stopping; truncation (is the relevant text beyond 512 tokens?); preprocessing and tokenizer (the checkpoint's own tokenizer, the same text cleaning as the baseline); label quality and class imbalance (per-class metrics, thresholds); the evaluation split (identical for both models); and whether the task is keyword-driven, where TF-IDF is legitimately hard to beat. Also try several seeds: fine-tuning on small data is noisy.

5. The first piece gets the word's tag (B-PER, or I-PER if it continues a name), and the other two get -100 (ignored in the loss). At prediction time, take the predicted tag of the first piece as the word's tag and ignore the others.

6. Encoder: a stable labeling task, enough labeled data (hundreds to thousands of examples), high volume, tight latency or cost budgets, a need for calibrated scores and thresholds, on-premises or CPU deployment, or resistance to prompt injection (the input can't instruct a classifier). Generative model: no or few labels, frequently changing categories, tasks requiring reasoning, explanation or generation, long and varied inputs, or low volume where engineering time dominates. A common pattern: use the large model to label data, then train an encoder on those labels (after auditing them) for production.
