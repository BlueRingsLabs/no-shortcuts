# 39.1 Evaluating language models

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 38.1 (the fine-tuned model under test), 18.1 and 18.3 (honest evaluation and calibration), 05.1 (confidence intervals and the bootstrap). Lab: [`lab_39_1_evaluation.py`](labs/lab_39_1_evaluation.py).*

Every model release comes with a table of benchmark scores, bolded where they beat the competition. Most of those numbers are real.
Many of them don't mean what the reader assumes, and a few are close to meaningless. After fifteen years of watching vendors present
numbers, I read a benchmark table the way I read a penetration test report written by the vendor of the product under test: politely,
and with the question "what exactly was measured, and who chose it?" This lesson is about asking that question well, about your own
models as much as anyone else's.

The lab uses the course's fine-tuned model from 38.1 as the system under test. It's tiny, so every effect is exaggerated, which makes it
easy to see. Every one of these effects has been documented on large models too.

## What a benchmark number contains

A benchmark score is the output of a pipeline: a dataset, a prompt format, a decoding setup, a way of extracting the answer and a
scoring rule. Change any of them and the number changes, sometimes by more than the gap between the models being compared.

**The scoring rule.** The lab scores the same 150 answers four ways:

| scoring rule | score |
|---|---|
| exact match | 0.187 |
| normalized exact match (case, punctuation, articles) | 0.187 |
| token F1, SQuAD-style | 0.306 |
| gold answer contained in the model's answer | 0.227 |

Same model, same outputs, and "the accuracy" ranges from 0.19 to 0.31. Exact match punishes harmless variation; token F1 gives partial
credit for partial answers, including wrong ones that share words with the right one; "contains the gold answer" rewards answers that list
every candidate. None of them is wrong. A number without its scoring rule is not a number.

**The prompt.** The same 80 facts, asked four ways:

| phrasing | exact match |
|---|---|
| "What is lesson {id} about?" | 0.613 |
| "Lesson {id}: what's the topic?" | 0.613 |
| "Give me the title of lesson {id}." | 0.650 |
| "Tell me what lesson {id} covers." (never seen in training) | 0.062 |

The model knows the facts and can't retrieve them through an unfamiliar phrasing. Large models are much less sensitive than this one,
and not insensitive: published work has shown several-point swings on standard benchmarks from formatting changes as small as the
separator between few-shot examples (Sclar et al., 2024). Report results over several prompt variants, or report that you didn't.

**Multiple choice by likelihood.** Many evaluation tools don't let the model generate at all. They score each option by its
log-probability as a continuation and pick the highest, either summed over the option's tokens or averaged per token. When the model knows
the answer, both rules find it: the lab's model scores 1.000 with either rule on 60 questions. The rule matters when the model
doesn't know. Given four *wrong* options, where no option deserves to win, the per-token mean picks the shortest option only 2% of the time
(no preference would be 25%). Once a long title has started, its later tokens are easy to predict, which raises its average. The sum pays
for every extra token, and on this model it lands near chance (22%, against 25%). Neither rule is neutral in general, and they're biased in opposite
directions. Evaluation tools differ in exactly these details (summed or normalized, with or without a leading space, which tokens are
scored), which is why the same model gets different scores on the "same" benchmark from different tools. When you compare against a
published number, use the same evaluation code and version, or don't compare.

## How sure are you?

Benchmarks are samples, and small ones. The lab compares greedy decoding (0.187) with sampling at temperature 0.7 (0.087) on 150
questions. The 95% bootstrap intervals are [0.127, 0.247] and [0.047, 0.133], wide enough that a two- or three-point difference would
mean nothing. The paired difference is +0.100 with interval [+0.047, +0.160]: this one is real. The two systems disagree on 19 of the 150
questions, and those 19 are all the evidence there is; pairing uses exactly them, which is why its interval is narrower than the two
separate ones. (In an earlier version of this lab, with a different small model, the same comparison gave +0.020 with an interval
straddling zero. Same code, same questions: the conclusion depended on the model, which is the point of computing the interval every time.)

Three habits:

- **Report intervals.** For accuracy $p$ on $n$ items the standard error is about $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$: with $n = 500$ and $p = 0.7$ that's two
  points, so a 95% interval is about ±4 points. Many popular benchmarks have a few hundred items; many leaderboard gaps are smaller than
  that.
- **Pair comparisons.** Compare two systems on the same items and bootstrap the per-item difference (05.1). Pairing removes the variance
  of which items happened to be in the set, which is usually most of it.
- **Decide the size in advance.** If the improvement you care about is two points, you need thousands of items to see it reliably
  (18.1). A test set too small to detect the effect you're looking for is a way of generating noise with a straight face.

## Contamination

Benchmarks leak into training data: they're on the web, in papers, on GitHub, and paraphrased in forum posts. A model that saw the test
set scores well on it without being able to do the task. Detection methods:

- **N-gram overlap** between training data and test items (the approach of GPT-3's and Llama's reports; 35.3 built one). It needs the
  training data, and misses paraphrases.
- **Membership inference from the model alone**: texts the model trained on tend to get lower loss. The lab tests this on the base model,
  trained on 90% of the lessons of Parts I to III. Mean loss: 3.04 on training paragraphs, 3.69 on held-out lessons of the same parts,
  3.78 on Part IV lessons. The AUC for "was this paragraph in training?" is 0.846 against held-out lessons from the same parts, and
  0.921 against Part IV.

The second number is the trap. Against Part IV the test looks stronger, but most of the extra separation is topic shift: Part IV uses
different words, so its loss is higher whether or not anything was memorized. Published membership-inference results on LLMs often
compared training data with non-members from a different time period or source, and much of their apparent success disappeared when the
non-members came from the same distribution (Duan et al., 2024). The lab's model memorizes a lot because it trained for many epochs on a
small corpus; frontier models train for about one epoch on most of their data and memorize less, except for text duplicated many times,
which benchmarks often are.

The defenses are held-out evaluation sets that were never published (private test sets, freshly written items, questions about events
after the training cutoff) and dynamic benchmarks that keep adding new items (LiveBench, LiveCodeBench). For your own applications,
the defense is simpler: evaluate on your own data, which no model was trained on (43.1).

## Beyond benchmarks

Benchmarks measure what's easy to score. Most of what makes a model useful isn't.

- **LLM-as-judge**: a strong model grades outputs against a rubric, or compares two outputs. Cheap and scalable, and biased in known
  ways: it prefers longer answers, the first position in a pairwise comparison, and outputs that sound like itself. Use it only after
  measuring its agreement with human judgments on your task, randomize positions, and report agreement alongside the scores (43.1).
- **Human preference arenas** (LMArena, formerly Chatbot Arena): people compare two anonymous models on their own prompts, and an
  Elo-style (Bradley-Terry, 38.3) rating comes out. It measures what people prefer in chat, which correlates with style and length as
  much as with correctness, and can be optimized for directly.
- **Human evaluation** with trained raters and a rubric: expensive, slow, and the reference everything else is calibrated against.
- **Task-specific evaluation** of your application: the only evaluation that answers "is this model good for us?" Module 43 is about
  building it.

## Reading a model card

When a new model ships, ask:

1. Which evaluation code, prompt format, number of shots, decoding settings and scoring rule? Are they the same as for the models it's compared
   with, or are the competitors' numbers copied from elsewhere?
2. How big is each benchmark, and are the gaps larger than the confidence intervals?
3. Is there any contamination analysis? Were benchmark-adjacent data sources excluded from training?
4. Are the benchmarks saturated (most models above 90%), in which case differences mean little?
5. What's missing? The benchmarks a vendor doesn't report are informative.

> **Scar tissue.** In a university research group I worked with, our benchmark score jumped twenty points after a fine-tuning run, and we
> celebrated for about a day. The evaluation set lived in the same shared folder as the fine-tuning data, and someone's data-gathering
> script had globbed the whole folder. The model had trained on the test. Evaluation sets now live somewhere else, with different
> permissions, and an n-gram overlap check runs between every training set and every evaluation set before any score is reported.

## Things that will bite you

- **Comparing numbers from different evaluation tools**, prompts or scoring rules.
- **Single prompt, single run, no interval.**
- **Test sets too small** to detect the difference you claim.
- **Contamination**, including your own: tuning prompts on the test set is training on it.
- **Membership tests with shifted non-members.**
- **LLM judges used without measuring their agreement with humans.**
- **Optimizing for a benchmark** until it stops measuring what it was built to measure (Goodhart, 38.3).

## Exercises

1. A paper reports 71.2% vs 69.8% on a 1,000-item multiple-choice benchmark. Is the difference meaningful? What would you need to know?
2. Why can summed log-probability favor short answer options, and what can go wrong with averaging per token instead?
3. You suspect a model has seen a benchmark. You don't have its training data. What can you do?
4. Why does paired bootstrapping give tighter intervals than comparing two separate intervals?
5. The lab's membership test gets AUC 0.92 against Part IV and 0.85 against same-distribution held-out lessons. Which number is the
   right estimate of memorization, and why?
6. Design an evaluation for a model that summarizes support tickets, without any public benchmark.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The standard error of each score is about $\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3 / 1000} \approx 1.45$ points, so the unpaired difference of 1.4
   points is well inside the noise. A paired analysis could tighten that if the models agree on most items. You'd also need to know
   whether both were run with the same evaluation code, prompt and decoding, whether the number is from one run or several, and how many
   benchmarks and variants were tried before reporting this one.

2. The log-probability of a sequence is a sum of negative numbers, one per token, so each extra token lowers it. When the model is
   confident about the correct option, that dominates; when it isn't, length can decide. Per-token averaging removes that penalty but
   can overcorrect, favoring long options whose later tokens are predictable (as in the lab). Scoring only the option's letter (A, B,
   C, D) after showing all options avoids both, if the model handles that format.

3. Test with perturbed versions of the items (reworded, reordered options, changed numbers): a model that learned the task is barely
   affected, one that memorized the items drops. Compare its loss on the benchmark items with its loss on similar items written after its
   training cutoff. Check whether it can complete a test item verbatim from a prefix. Evaluate on a fresh set of items in the same format.

4. Items vary a lot in difficulty, and both systems see the same items. The difference per item cancels the shared difficulty, so its
   variance is much smaller than the sum of the two separate variances. Separate intervals ignore that correlation and overstate the
   uncertainty of the difference.

5. The 0.85. The Part IV comparison mixes two effects, memorization and the fact that Part IV's topics and vocabulary differ from the
   training data; the second would give a high AUC even for a model that memorized nothing. A membership test is only valid when members
   and non-members come from the same distribution.

6. A few hundred real tickets (anonymized), with reference summaries from support staff for a subset; a rubric (covers the customer's
   issue, the requested action, the urgency, no invented facts); automatic checks where possible (length, required fields, names and
   numbers present in the ticket); an LLM judge for the rubric, validated against staff ratings on 100 tickets; and a paired comparison
   of every candidate model or prompt against the current one, with intervals. Then add failures from production as they appear (43.1).
