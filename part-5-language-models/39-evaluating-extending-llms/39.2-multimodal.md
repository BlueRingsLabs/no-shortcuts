# 39.2 Multimodal models: vision-language and speech

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 30.2 (ViT and CLIP), 35.2 (a GPT), 38.1 (fine-tuning), 28.1 (recurrent networks). Lab: [`lab_39_2_multimodal.py`](labs/lab_39_2_multimodal.py).*

A language model reads tokens. Everything multimodal comes down to one question: how do you turn an image or a sound into something a
language model can read, and train the combination without starting from zero? The answers that won are almost embarrassingly simple:
encode the other modality with a network built for it, project the result into the language model's embedding space as a handful of
"soft tokens", and fine-tune. This lesson builds a miniature vision-language model and a small speech recognizer, and explains how the
large ones differ.

## Vision-language models

The recipe popularized by LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023) has three parts:

1. A **vision encoder**, usually a pretrained CLIP-style ViT (30.2), turns an image into a grid of patch embeddings.
2. A **projector** (a linear layer or a small MLP) maps each of those vectors into the language model's embedding space.
3. The **language model** receives them as a prefix of "image tokens" followed by the text, and is trained with the ordinary next-token
   loss on captions, answers and instructions.

Training happens in two stages. Stage 1 freezes both the encoder and the language model and trains only the projector on image-caption
pairs, so the projector learns to "write" in the language model's embedding space. Stage 2 unfreezes the language model (and sometimes the
encoder) and trains on instruction data: questions about images, descriptions, charts, documents. Other designs exist. Flamingo inserts
cross-attention layers into the language model, and natively multimodal models train on interleaved tokens of all modalities from the
start. But the prefix-of-soft-tokens design is the one you'll meet most in open models.

The lab's miniature: images of colored shapes (30.2's data), a small CNN encoder, a projector that produces 4 soft tokens, and the course's
own tiny GPT (35.2) as the language model. Captions are "a red circle." and so on. Four color-shape combinations are held out of training
entirely.

| stage | exact caption, seen combinations | never-seen combinations |
|---|---|---|
| 1: language model frozen, projector and encoder trained | 0.610 | 0.000 |
| 2: language model also tuned | 0.817 | 0.006 |

Stage 1 is the surprising result. A language model that has never seen an image, whose weights haven't changed, produces correct captions
61% of the time from four vectors it was never trained on. The projector learned to produce inputs that make the frozen model say the right
words. That's why stage-1 alignment is cheap, and why a strong language model can be given vision with relatively little data.

The last column is the other lesson. On the held-out combinations, stage 2 names the right color 100% of the time and the right shape 1% of
the time: shown a red circle, it says "a red plus" or "a red square", combinations it saw in training. It recognizes each attribute, then
falls back on familiar pairs. Compositional generalization doesn't come free. It comes from training data that covers combinations, and
it's a real weakness of large vision-language models too: counting, spatial relations and unusual attribute bindings ("a blue banana") are
where they fail most.

What large systems add:

- **Resolution.** More patches means more image tokens. High-resolution images are tiled into crops, each encoded separately, which is
  why an image can cost more than a thousand tokens (40.1).
- **Documents and charts.** OCR-free reading depends on resolution and on training data full of text in images.
- **Grounding**: predicting boxes or points, trained as text coordinates.
- **Video**: sampled frames as image tokens, compressed over time.

## Speech

Speech recognition has the same shape, with two twists: the input is a waveform, and the alignment between sound and text is unknown.

**The front end.** A waveform at 16 kHz is 16,000 numbers a second, too many and too raw. Nearly every system first computes a
**log-mel spectrogram**:

1. Cut the signal into overlapping frames (25 ms windows every 10 ms).
2. Take the power spectrum of each frame (FFT).
3. Pool the frequencies into bands spaced on the mel scale, which is roughly how human hearing resolves pitch: fine at low frequencies,
   coarse at high ones.
4. Take the log, because loudness perception is logarithmic, and it turns multiplicative effects into additive ones.

The lab implements each step with NumPy: 40 mel bands, 10 ms frames.

**The alignment problem.** A transcript says "5 7 5 1 5 9", and the audio has hundreds of frames. Which frames are which digit? Labeling
frames by hand doesn't scale. **Connectionist temporal classification** (CTC; Graves et al., 2006) sidesteps it. The model outputs, for
every frame, a distribution over the symbols plus a special *blank*. An output path maps to a transcript by merging repeats, then removing
blanks: the path "5 5 - 7 7 - 7" becomes "5 - 7 - 7" and then "5 7 7", the blank being what separates the two 7s. The CTC loss
sums the probability of every path that maps to the correct transcript, by dynamic programming. No frame labels needed.

The lab trains a small convolutional and recurrent model with CTC to read digit sequences from synthetic phone-keypad tones (DTMF: each
digit is a sum of two sine waves) in noise:

| step | CTC loss | exact sequence accuracy (200 new clips) |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 2.76 | 0.000 |
| 400 | 1.82 | 0.000 |
| 500 | 0.65 | 0.100 |
| 600 | 0.04 | 1.000 |

For hundreds of steps the model outputs only blanks, which is a cheap way to get a moderate loss, and then it finds the alignment, and
accuracy goes from 0 to 100% in about 100 steps. This plateau is typical of CTC training. In the lab it only resolves because the model
downsamples time by 4 with strided convolutions, so each digit spans a few output frames instead of 8 to 20. Without the downsampling, the
model was still stuck on blanks after 1,200 steps. Real systems downsample too (Whisper's encoder halves the frame rate; many CTC models go
to 40 to 80 ms per output frame).

**Modern systems.** Whisper (Radford et al., 2022) keeps the log-mel front end and replaces CTC with an encoder-decoder transformer, trained
on 680,000 hours of weakly labeled audio. The decoder generates text tokens like a language model, which handles punctuation,
capitalization, translation and timestamps as tokens too. CTC models remain popular where streaming and latency matter, since they emit
output frame by frame. And speech-native language models skip text altogether: they tokenize audio into discrete units (with neural
codecs) and model them directly, which is how real-time voice assistants handle tone, interruption and overlapping speech.

## Things that will bite you

- **Image token budgets**: high-resolution images are expensive, and downscaling loses the small text you needed.
- **Compositional and counting failures** in vision-language models, confidently described.
- **Train-serve mismatch in audio front ends**: sample rate, normalization and mel parameters must match exactly.
- **CTC plateaus** mistaken for a broken model, or sequences too long for the output frame rate.
- **Hallucinated transcripts**: encoder-decoder speech models can produce fluent text for silence or noise.

## Exercises

1. In the LLaVA recipe, why train only the projector first?
2. An image is encoded into 576 patch embeddings. What does that cost in a 4,000-token context, and how would you reduce it?
3. Compute the number of mel frames for 3 seconds of audio with a 10 ms hop. Why take the log of the mel energies?
4. Which of these CTC paths decode to "3 3 5": "3 3 - 3 5", "3 - 3 5 5", "- 3 3 3 5", "3 - 3 - 5"?
5. Why did the lab's captioner name colors correctly but not shapes on unseen combinations? What training data would fix it?
6. When would you choose a CTC model over an encoder-decoder model for speech?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The projector starts random; if the language model is unfrozen at the same time, its weights receive large, meaningless gradients
   from the random image tokens, damaging its language ability. Training the projector alone first teaches it to produce useful inputs
   cheaply (few parameters), so that stage 2 fine-tunes from a sensible starting point.

2. 576 of 4,000 tokens, about 14% of the context and of the prefill cost, per image. To reduce: pool or merge patches (a projector that
   outputs fewer tokens, as in resamplers like Q-Former or Perceiver), lower the resolution when detail isn't needed, or crop to the
   relevant region first.

3. About 300 frames (3 s / 10 ms, minus a few at the edges). Log because perceived loudness is roughly logarithmic, because energies span
   several orders of magnitude (the log compresses the range, which helps training), and because the channel's multiplicative effects become
   additive, which normalization can remove.

4. Merge repeats, then drop blanks. "3 3 - 3 5" → "3 - 3 5" → "3 3 5" ✓. "3 - 3 5 5" → "3 - 3 5" → "3 3 5" ✓. "- 3 3 3 5" → "- 3 5" →
   "3 5" ✗. "3 - 3 - 5" → "3 3 5" ✓. A blank is required between two identical symbols.

5. Colors are presumably easy for the encoder (a global statistic) and each color appeared with several shapes in training, so the model
   learned color as a separate attribute. Each held-out shape-color pair never appeared, and the language model has a strong prior on which
   shape word follows each color word in its training captions, which overrides weaker shape evidence. Training data where every color
   appears with every shape (or augmentations that recolor shapes) breaks the correlation.

6. For streaming, low-latency recognition (live captions, voice commands) where output must appear frame by frame, on small devices, and
   where hallucination on silence is unacceptable (CTC can't invent long text without acoustic evidence). Encoder-decoder models win on
   accuracy, punctuation, multilingual and translation tasks when latency is less critical.
