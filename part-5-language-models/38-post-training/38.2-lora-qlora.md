# 38.2 Parameter-efficient fine-tuning: LoRA and QLoRA

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 38.1, 37.3 (quantization), 02.4 (the SVD and low rank). Lab: [`lab_38_2_lora.py`](labs/lab_38_2_lora.py).*

Full fine-tuning of a 7B model needs the weights, their gradients and two Adam moments in memory: about 16 bytes per parameter, over
100 GB before activations. Most teams don't have that, and even those who do don't want a 14 GB copy of the model for every task and
every customer. **Parameter-efficient fine-tuning** trains a tiny number of new parameters and freezes the rest. **LoRA** is the method
that won, and **QLoRA** is what made fine-tuning large models possible on a single GPU. This lesson builds both from scratch and measures
what they cost, which is more interesting than the usual "matches full fine-tuning" claim.

## The idea

LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) bets that the *change* a fine-tune makes to a weight matrix has low rank. Instead of learning $\Delta W$
($d_\text{out} \times d_\text{in}$), learn two thin matrices, $B$ ($d_\text{out} \times r$) and $A$ ($r \times d_\text{in}$), with
$r$ between 1 and 64, and compute

$$h = W x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A x.$$

$W$ is frozen. $A$ starts small and random, $B$ starts at zero, so at step 0 the model is exactly the base model (the lab checks: identical
outputs). The trainable parameters per matrix drop from $d_\text{out} d_\text{in}$ to $r(d_\text{out} + d_\text{in})$: for a
$4096 \times 4096$ projection at $r = 16$, from 16.8M to 131k. The scale $\alpha / r$ keeps the update's size roughly independent of $r$,
so you can change the rank without retuning the learning rate much. LoRA is usually applied to the attention projections and, for
better results, to the MLP layers too (the lab does both).

Three practical consequences:

- **Memory**: gradients and optimizer states exist only for the adapters. The frozen base can even be stored in low precision.
- **Adapters are small files** (tens of megabytes for a 7B model), so you can keep one per task or customer and swap them over a single
  base model in memory (serving systems such as vLLM do this per request).
- **Merging**: after training, $W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA$ is an ordinary matrix. The lab merges the adapter and gets a plain model with
  the same outputs (maximum logit difference $3 \times 10^{-5}$) and zero inference overhead.

## What the lab measures

38.1's task: teach the course's small GPT (970k parameters) to answer questions about the course's lessons, evaluated on questions phrased
as in training ("seen") and phrased in ways it never saw ("unseen"), plus how many answers are well-formed, and the loss on the course text
as a measure of forgetting:

| method | trainable parameters | exact (seen) | exact (unseen) | well-formed (unseen) | course text loss (base: 3.524) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| full fine-tuning | 970,496 (100%) | 0.87 | 0.11 | 0.82 | 6.753 |
| LoRA $r = 1$ | 8,192 (0.8%) | 0.01 | 0.00 | 0.15 | 4.793 |
| LoRA $r = 4$ | 32,768 (3.4%) | 0.13 | 0.07 | 0.42 | 5.755 |
| LoRA $r = 16$ | 131,072 (13.5%) | 0.58 | 0.23 | 0.79 | 6.685 |
| LoRA $r = 4$, 30 epochs | 32,768 (3.4%) | 0.42 | 0.26 | 0.59 | 6.407 |

What to read in it:

- **Rank matters here.** Ranks 1 and 4 learn little in the same number of epochs. A tiny model is the worst case for LoRA: its matrices
  are only 128 or 512 wide, and a few directions are a large share of what they can express. On 7B models, ranks of 8 to 16 usually match
  full fine-tuning on instruction-following tasks, which is where LoRA's reputation comes from.
- **Budget matters too.** Rank 4 for 30 epochs learns much more than rank 4 for 12: low-rank updates learn more slowly, not only less.
- **LoRA generalized better.** Full fine-tuning memorized the training phrasings (0.87 seen, 0.11 unseen); rank 16 learned less of them
  and did twice as well on phrasings it never saw, and rank 4 trained for longer did better still. It's a regularizer.
- **LoRA forgets less, in proportion to how much it learns.** Ranks 1 and 4 barely damaged the course-text loss; rank 16, which learned
  the most, damaged it almost as much as full fine-tuning (6.685 against 6.753). The same trade-off was measured on
  large models by Biderman et al. (2024), whose title says it: "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less". When the goal is new *knowledge*
  (a new domain, a new language), LoRA falls short of full fine-tuning; when it's new *behavior* on top of what the model knows, it's
  usually enough, and safer.

The learning rate: LoRA wants roughly ten times the full fine-tuning rate (the lab uses $10^{-2}$ against $10^{-3}$). Its update starts at
zero and lives in a small subspace; a full-fine-tuning learning rate barely moves it.

## QLoRA

QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) combines LoRA with a **4-bit frozen base**. The base weights are stored in 4-bit NF4 (a data type whose levels
are placed for normally distributed weights), dequantized on the fly for each matmul, and never updated; the adapters train in 16-bit.
With paged optimizers for memory spikes and double quantization of the scales, it fine-tuned a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU.

The lab's miniature uses 37.3's int4 group quantizer for the base (loss on the course text: 3.543 against 3.524 in full precision), then
trains rank-16 adapters on top: exact match 0.70 seen and 0.38 unseen, against 0.58 and 0.23 on the full-precision base. That the 4-bit
version came out ahead is noise, not a finding: one run, 100 questions each, and a quantized base that changes the starting point of
training. What it does show is that a 4-bit base doesn't stop the adapters from learning, which is the published result at scale
(Dettmers et al., 2023): QLoRA matches 16-bit LoRA.

The memory arithmetic for a 7B model, before activations (the lab computes it):

| method | memory |
|---|---|
| full fine-tuning (bf16 weights and gradients, fp32 Adam states and master weights) | ~112 GB |
| LoRA, bf16 base | ~15 GB |
| QLoRA, 4-bit base | ~4 GB |

Activations come on top and grow with batch size and sequence length; gradient checkpointing (recomputing activations in the backward
pass) trades compute for that memory.

## Variants you'll meet

- **DoRA**: splits each weight into magnitude and direction and applies LoRA to the direction; often a little better at the same rank.
- **rsLoRA**: scales by $\alpha/\sqrt{r}$, which behaves better at high ranks.
- **LoRA+**: a higher learning rate for $B$ than for $A$.
- **Prefix tuning, prompt tuning, adapters (bottleneck layers)**: the earlier PEFT methods; LoRA displaced them because it adds no
  inference cost once merged.

Libraries: Hugging Face PEFT implements all of these; TRL and Axolotl wrap them into training recipes; Unsloth optimizes them for speed.

## Things that will bite you

- **The full fine-tuning learning rate** used for LoRA: nothing happens, and it looks like LoRA doesn't work.
- **Adapters only on the attention**, when the task needs the MLP layers too.
- **Rank chosen by folklore** rather than tried at two or three values on your task.
- **Evaluating on training phrasings only**, which hides both full fine-tuning's memorization and LoRA's advantage.
- **Merging into a quantized base**: merge into the full-precision weights, then quantize the merged model, and evaluate again.
- **The base model version changing** under a stored adapter: adapters are only valid for the exact weights they were trained on.

## Exercises

1. Compute the trainable parameters of LoRA with $r = 8$ on all four attention projections of a model with 32 layers and $d = 4096$.
2. Why is $B$ initialized to zero and not $A$? What would happen if both were random?
3. In the lab, full fine-tuning scored 0.87 on seen phrasings and 0.11 on unseen ones. What does that tell you, and what would you change?
4. When would you choose full fine-tuning over LoRA despite the memory cost?
5. You serve one base model with 200 customer adapters. How does a request get its adapter, and what does that cost in latency and memory?
6. Why does QLoRA dequantize the base weights for every matmul rather than training in 4-bit?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Each projection: $r(d_\text{in} + d_\text{out}) = 8 \times 8192 = 65{,}536$. Four per layer, 32 layers: $65{,}536 \times 128 \approx 8.4$M
   parameters, about 0.1% of a 7B model.

2. $B = 0$ makes $BA = 0$, so training starts exactly from the base model. If both were random, the first forward pass would add a random
   perturbation to every adapted layer and damage the model before learning started. $A$ can't be zero too, or the gradient of $B$ (which
   depends on $Ax$) and of $A$ (which depends on $B$) would both be zero: nothing would ever train.

3. It memorized the specific training templates rather than learning to answer questions about lessons in general. Options: more varied
   phrasings in the training data (the real fix), fewer epochs or early stopping on unseen-phrasing validation, a lower learning rate,
   or LoRA as a regularizer. And always evaluate on unseen phrasings.

4. When the model must learn a lot of new knowledge or a new domain or language (continued pretraining), when the task is far from the
   base model's distribution, when you have the compute and the best possible quality matters more than memory, and when you'll serve a
   single fine-tuned model rather than many.

5. The server keeps the base weights once and a cache of adapters in GPU memory (the rest in CPU memory or on disk); each request names
   its adapter, and batched kernels apply different adapters to different requests in the same batch (as in S-LoRA or vLLM's multi-LoRA).
   Cost: a small extra matmul per adapted layer (unmerged), adapter memory (tens of MB each), and latency to load an adapter on a cache miss.

6. Gradient-based updates need precision: 4-bit weights can only move in large discrete steps, and small updates would round to nothing.
   So the base stays frozen in 4-bit (it's only read), the matmul runs in 16-bit after dequantizing, and the trainable adapters are 16-bit.
