# 38.3 Preference optimization: RLHF and DPO

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 38.1, 46.5 (policy gradients), 04.2 (probability). Lab: [`lab_38_3_preferences.py`](labs/lab_38_3_preferences.py).*

SFT teaches a model to imitate demonstrations. But for most of what we want from an assistant (helpful, honest, not rambling, not
harmful) it's much easier for people to *compare* two answers than to *write* the ideal one. Preference optimization turns comparisons
into training signal. It's what made chat models feel like chat models, and it's also the part of the pipeline where you most clearly
get what you measured instead of what you wanted. This lesson covers reward models, RLHF, DPO, and the failure modes, with a lab where
nearly everything goes wrong in instructive ways.

## Preference data

A **preference pair** is a prompt $x$ with two responses, a preferred one $y_w$ and a rejected one $y_l$. Labelers see both and pick. The
model behind almost every method is **Bradley-Terry**: each response has a latent score $r(x, y)$, and

$$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big).$$

Who labels, and what they can check, decides everything downstream. Raters can check fluency, format, tone, apparent confidence and
length in seconds; they can check factual correctness only when they know the answer or spend time verifying it. The lab builds pairs
from two labelers: a **fact-checker** who prefers the correct answer, and a **form-checker** who can only tell a well-formed lesson title
from a garbled one. Real rating pipelines are mostly the second kind, however good the guidelines.

## Reward models

A **reward model** is the SFT model with its output layer replaced by a scalar head, trained to maximize
$\log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$ over the pairs. The lab trains one per labeler and measures accuracy on pairs it didn't train on, with 5-fold cross-validation:

| reward model trained on | cross-validated pair accuracy | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|
| the fact-checker's pairs (correct > wrong) | 0.53 | 0.46 to 0.61 |
| the form-checker's pairs (well-formed > garbled) | 0.76 | 0.70 to 0.81 |

The fact reward model can't be told apart from a coin; the form model clearly can do its job. (Why cross-validated: with a couple of
hundred pairs, a single 80/20 split leaves about 40 test pairs, and in development runs it put the fact model anywhere from 0.47 to
0.62. One split is an anecdote.) The fact model starts from the same small model that gets most facts wrong, and a reward model can't judge what
its base model doesn't know. Form is easy to learn. That's why real reward models are initialized from models as large as the policy (or
larger), and why they still end up rewarding what raters can check: confident, well-formatted, long.

## Optimizing against a reward model

The simplest optimizer is **best-of-n**: sample $n$ answers, return the one the reward model scores highest. No training, and it's what
you'd use at inference time as a baseline for anything fancier. The lab does it with the form reward model and with a deliberately bad
one trained on a labeler who likes long answers:

| $n$ | form RM: correct / well-formed / length | length-loving RM: correct / well-formed / length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.32 / 0.52 / 31 | 0.32 / 0.52 / 31 |
| 4 | 0.33 / 0.62 / 27 | 0.30 / 0.43 / 39 |
| 16 | 0.22 / 0.70 / 27 | 0.17 / 0.25 / 43 |

Neither makes the answers more correct, and more optimization makes both worse. The form reward model does what it was trained for (the
share of well-formed answers rises from 0.52 to 0.70), and correctness falls from 0.32 to 0.22: it's right on 76% of held-out pairs, and
taking the maximum over 16 samples selects exactly the samples where its errors are largest. This is **reward model over-optimization**
(Gao, Schulman and Hilton, 2023): as you optimize harder against a proxy, the true objective first rises (with a decent proxy), then
falls. With a weak proxy, as here, it only falls. The length-loving model is the pure case of **reward hacking**: length goes up with
$n$, everything else goes down. Optimizing a proxy delivers the proxy.

Real examples are less cartoonish and the same in kind: models that learned to be longer, more hedged or more sycophantic (agreeing with
the user's stated opinion), because raters rewarded it.

## RLHF with PPO

The InstructGPT recipe (Ouyang et al., 2022), which produced the first widely used chat models:

1. SFT on demonstrations (38.1).
2. Train a reward model on comparisons.
3. Optimize the policy with PPO (46.6) to maximize reward minus a **KL penalty** to the SFT model:
   $\max_\pi \mathbb{E}[r(x, y)] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_\text{SFT})$.

The KL term is the brake on over-optimization: the policy may move only as far from the SFT model as the reward gain justifies, which
keeps it in the region where the reward model was trained and is roughly right. It works, and it's heavy: four models in memory (policy,
reference, reward model, value network), generation inside the training loop, and PPO's sensitivity to hyperparameters.

## DPO

**Direct preference optimization** (Rafailov et al., 2023) observes that the KL-regularized objective above has a closed-form optimal
policy, $\pi^*(y \mid x) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x, y)/\beta}$, so the reward can be written in terms of the policy:
$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)} + \text{const}$. Substitute that into the Bradley-Terry likelihood and
the reward model disappears:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\log \sigma\Big(\beta \Big[\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w \mid x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l \mid x)}\Big]\Big).$$

A supervised loss on pairs: no reward model, no sampling, two models (policy and frozen reference). It's why DPO and its relatives became
the default for open models.

The lab runs DPO on 165 (correct, wrong) pairs sampled from the SFT model, at four settings, and measures what happened to the log-
probabilities of the chosen and rejected answers (against the reference) and to accuracy:

| setting | chosen log-prob change | rejected log-prob change | exact match, other questions | unseen phrasings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SFT model (reference) | 0 | 0 | 0.650 | 0.192 |
| DPO, lr $10^{-4}$ | −19.3 | −61.3 | 0.000 | 0.000 |
| DPO + NLL on chosen, lr $10^{-4}$ | −0.8 | −24.1 | 0.308 | 0.108 |
| DPO, lr $10^{-5}$ | −0.2 | −4.7 | 0.633 | 0.100 |
| DPO, lr $3 \times 10^{-6}$ | +0.05 | −1.1 | 0.642 | 0.167 |

Every row "succeeded" by the loss: the margin between chosen and rejected grew. Now look at which way each moved. At $10^{-4}$, the
chosen answers became $e^{19}$ times *less* likely, the rejected ones even less, and the model stopped answering anything correctly. The
loss only sees the difference. Pushing probability off the rejected answers is easiest, and the probability goes somewhere: to text
nobody chose. This is **likelihood displacement** (Razin et al., 2024), and it's the most common way DPO goes wrong in practice: the
training curves look great and the model gets worse.

Two fixes, both in the table. An **NLL term on the chosen answer** (as in RPO, Pang et al., 2024) keeps it likely while the margin grows,
which limits the damage. **Small learning rates** and few epochs keep the policy near the reference, which is what $\beta$ is supposed to do
and, with a strong optimizer, doesn't do alone. Neither made the model more accurate. Preference data can only reweight answers the model
already produces, and this model mostly produces wrong ones: DPO polishes a policy, it doesn't teach facts. Monitor the chosen
log-probability, not only the loss, the margin or the "reward accuracy" your training library reports.

## Where this goes next

- **Variants**: IPO (a bounded loss that over-fits less), KTO (works from thumbs-up/thumbs-down instead of pairs), ORPO and SimPO (no
  reference model), and iterative or online DPO, which samples new pairs from the current policy.
- **AI feedback**: preferences labeled by a model following written principles (Constitutional AI, RLAIF), cheaper and more consistent
  than people, with the judge's biases (43.1) instead.
- **Verifiable rewards**: where correctness can be checked in code (math, tests, formats), reward models are replaced by verifiers, and
  the over-optimization problem mostly disappears. That's 38.4.

## Things that will bite you

- **Reward models trusted beyond their accuracy**, and optimized hard.
- **Raters rewarding form** because they can't check substance.
- **Length, sycophancy and hedging** creeping up across post-training rounds, unmeasured.
- **DPO's margin read as success** without looking at which way each likelihood moved.
- **Evaluating preference-tuned models with the same reward model** used to train them.

## Exercises

1. Derive the Bradley-Terry reward model loss from $P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma(r_w - r_l)$ by maximum likelihood.
2. Why did the fact reward model stay at chance while the form one learned? What would you change to get a useful fact reward model?
3. Explain why best-of-16 made the answers less correct with a reward model that's right 76% of the time.
4. What does the KL penalty in RLHF protect against? What happens as $\beta \to 0$ and as $\beta \to \infty$?
5. Show that the DPO loss only depends on the difference between the chosen and rejected log-ratios. Why does that allow both
   likelihoods to fall?
6. Design a preference data collection for a customer-support assistant that won't reward confident wrong answers.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The likelihood of the data is $\prod \sigma(r_w - r_l)$; its negative log is $-\sum \log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$, the loss. The
   reward is only identified up to a constant per prompt, since only differences enter.

2. Judging correctness requires knowing the answer, and the reward model is the same small model, which gets most answers wrong: the
   information isn't in its weights. Form is a surface property it can see. A useful fact reward model needs a base that knows the facts
   (a larger model), or access to them (retrieval over the course, a verifier that checks the title against the table of contents,
   which turns it into 38.4's verifiable reward).

3. Each sample's reward-model score is its true quality plus the model's error. The maximum of 16 scores is disproportionately the sample
   with the largest positive error, not the best answer. With a reward model that's right 76% of the time, the errors dominate the
   selection, and the chosen answers are the ones the proxy likes most for the wrong reasons (here: short and title-shaped).

4. Drifting into regions where the reward model is wrong (over-optimization and reward hacking), and losing the SFT model's fluency and
   knowledge. As $\beta \to 0$, the policy maximizes the reward model alone and finds its exploits; as $\beta \to \infty$, it stays the SFT
   model and learns nothing from the preferences.

5. The loss is $-\log\sigma(\beta(\Delta_w - \Delta_l))$ with $\Delta = \log \pi_\theta - \log \pi_\text{ref}$, a function of
   $\Delta_w - \Delta_l$ only. Decreasing $\Delta_w$ by 10 and $\Delta_l$ by 60 lowers the loss as much as raising $\Delta_w$ by 50. Nothing
   in the loss prefers the second, and pushing the rejected answer down is usually easier, so both can fall, with the probability mass
   going to other text.

6. Pair comparisons where raters see the relevant ground truth (the order record, the policy text) next to the answers, a rubric that
   ranks "correct and says so" above "confident and wrong" and "I don't know" above wrong, an explicit option for "both wrong", checks
   of rater agreement on a gold subset with known answers, length-balanced pairs, and audits of what the resulting reward model prefers
   (43.1).
