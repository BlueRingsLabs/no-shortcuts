# 38.4 Reasoning models and RL with verifiable rewards

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 38.3, 46.5 (policy gradients), 37.1 (decoding). Lab: [`lab_38_4_reasoning_rlvr.py`](labs/lab_38_4_reasoning_rlvr.py).*

The models that "think before answering" (OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1 and their successors) rest on two ideas that are each simpler
than the marketing suggests. First, writing out intermediate steps gives a transformer more computation per problem, and makes each step
easy. Second, where the answer can be checked by a program (math with a known result, code with tests, a format with a validator), you can
run reinforcement learning against the checker instead of against a reward model, and most of 38.3's over-optimization problems go away.
This lesson demonstrates both on the smallest task that shows them, and measures what RL actually changes, which is less than the word
"reasoning" suggests.

## Steps are computation

A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per generated token: one forward pass through its layers. A problem that needs more
sequential computation than the layers provide can't be solved in one token, however large the training set. Each token it writes,
though, becomes input to the next forward pass. **Chain of thought** turns one hard step into many easy ones, each conditioned on text
the model already wrote (Wei et al., 2022; the theory is in Feng et al., 2023, and Merrill and Sabharwal, 2024).

The lab trains the same small transformer from scratch, with the same budget, on adding two 3-digit numbers, in two formats:

- direct: `123+489=612`
- with steps: `123+489:3+9=12,2+8+1=11,1+4+1=6=612` (digits right to left, with carries)

After 800 training steps of 64 problems each, on 500 new problems: **direct answers 0.4% correct, written-out steps 100%.** Same model,
same data, same compute for training. The direct format asks for all digits and carries at once, in the forward pass that produces the
first digit; the step format asks for one single-digit addition at a time. The direct model does get there with enough training (in
development runs, it jumped from 2% at 1,100 steps to 82% at 1,400: a sharp transition, as it finds an algorithm its layers can
represent), but writing out the steps made the problem easy.

This is test-time compute in its simplest form: more output tokens, more computation per problem. Reasoning models are trained to spend
it where it helps.

## RL with verifiable rewards

**RLVR**: sample answers, check them with a program, reward the right ones, update the policy with a policy gradient. The checker can't be
flattered by length or confidence, so optimizing hard against it is safe in a way optimizing a reward model isn't. What can still go
wrong is the checker itself: a unit test suite that passes a function returning a hard-coded value, a math grader that accepts any string
containing the right number. Reward hacking moves from the reward model to the verifier.

**GRPO** (group relative policy optimization, Shao et al., 2024) is the algorithm DeepSeek used, and 46.5's policy gradient with the
simplest good baseline:

1. For each prompt, sample a group of $G$ answers (8 to 64).
2. Score each with the verifier: $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$, or partial credit.
3. Advantage of each answer: $A_i = (r_i - \text{mean}(r)) / \text{std}(r)$, the group being the baseline. No critic network.
4. Update with the policy-gradient loss $-A_i \log \pi(\text{answer}_i)$, averaged over tokens, usually with PPO's clipping and a KL
   penalty to a reference model.

A group where every answer is right, or every one wrong, has zero advantage everywhere and teaches nothing. So RLVR needs a model that's
already right *sometimes* on the training problems, and curricula that keep problems at that difficulty. From a model that never gets
it right, there's no signal at all.

The lab starts GRPO from the direct-answer model trained for 1,400 steps, which is right most of the time, and runs 40 steps of 16
problems with 8 samples each, at a learning rate of $10^{-5}$. On 200 test problems, with 15 samples each:

| model | greedy | one sample at $T = 1$ | majority of 5 | majority of 15 | pass@15 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| direct, 1,400 SFT steps | 0.820 | 0.658 | 0.755 | 0.795 | 1.000 |
| direct + GRPO | 0.845 | 0.672 | 0.780 | 0.810 | 0.985 |
| written-out steps, 800 SFT steps | 1.000 | 0.951 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 |

The honest reading: RL nudged single-sample accuracy up by 1.4 points, with a paired 95% interval of −0.7 to +3.5. At this budget, that's
not distinguishable from noise. The learning rate is that small because larger ones did damage: in development runs, $3 \times 10^{-4}$
took greedy accuracy from 0.82 to 0.57, and $10^{-3}$ collapsed the model. Policy-gradient updates on a model's own samples are easily too
large, and an RL run that's quietly making the model worse looks, on a noisy reward curve, a lot like one that's working.

What didn't happen is the more robust finding: **pass@15 didn't rise.** Every problem was already solved by at least one of 15 samples
before RL. RL can only move probability toward answers the model already produces sometimes; large RLVR runs get their big pass@1 gains
the same way, over thousands of steps. And look at the last two columns of the table: **majority voting** (self-consistency, Wang et al.,
2023) gets more from the same model than RL did, by spending more samples at inference. The written-out steps beat everything, with
less training. On this task, the cheapest gain was the format of the answer.

## What reasoning models are

Put the two ideas together at scale: a strong pretrained model, a large set of problems with checkable answers, and RL that rewards
correct final answers while leaving the model free to write whatever intermediate text it likes. DeepSeek-R1 (2025) reported that long
chains of thought, self-verification and backtracking ("wait, let me check") emerged from this without being demonstrated, and that the
length of the reasoning grew during training. The models are then usually distilled: smaller models are fine-tuned on the large model's
reasoning traces (38.1), which transfers much of the ability far more cheaply than running RL on them.

What's still debated, and worth being skeptical about:

- **How much is new ability and how much is sharpening.** Yue et al. (2025) find that RLVR raises pass@1 but not pass@k for large $k$:
  the base model could already solve those problems in some fraction of its samples. The lab shows the same pattern.
- **Faithfulness of the reasoning text.** The written steps are not a guaranteed record of how the answer was computed. Models sometimes
  reach the answer by other means and write a plausible derivation (Turpin et al., 2023). Don't treat chains of thought as explanations
  for audit purposes without checking.
- **Transfer.** Training on math and code improves some other tasks and not others; verifiable domains are where the method is strong.
- **Cost.** Reasoning tokens are output tokens: slow and expensive (40.1). Many products route only hard queries to reasoning models
  (45.1).

## Things that will bite you

- **RL from a model that can't solve any training problems**: zero advantage everywhere, nothing learned.
- **Learning rates borrowed from SFT**: policy-gradient updates on a model's own samples are easily too large, and the model gets worse
  while the reward curve looks noisy but flat.
- **Verifiers with holes**: the policy will find them before you do.
- **Judging RL by greedy accuracy alone**: look at sampled accuracy and pass@k too, to see what actually changed.
- **Reasoning text read as a faithful explanation.**
- **Paying for reasoning tokens** on queries that didn't need them.

## Exercises

1. Why can't a fixed-depth transformer compute an $n$-digit sum in one output token for large $n$, and why do written-out steps help?
2. In GRPO, why is a group where all answers are right useless for training? What does that imply for choosing training problems?
3. Design a verifier for "write a Python function that sorts a list" and list three ways a policy could hack it.
4. A reasoning model scores 71% pass@1 and its base model 52%, while pass@64 is 90% for both. What did RL do?
5. How would you decide whether to route a customer-support query to a reasoning model?
6. Why does distilling a reasoning model's traces into a small model work better than running RL on the small model directly?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Each output token gets a fixed number of sequential layer computations. Carries propagate across all digits, a sequential dependency
   whose length grows with $n$; beyond some $n$, a fixed depth can't represent it in one pass (in general, fixed-depth transformers are
   limited to a restricted class of computations per token). Written steps store intermediate results in the context, so each token
   needs only one digit-plus-carry step, and depth is effectively multiplied by the number of tokens.

2. All rewards equal means all advantages are zero: no gradient. Useful problems are those the model solves sometimes, so curricula
   filter out problems it always or never solves, and raise the difficulty as it improves.

3. Run the function on many random lists (including empty, duplicates, negatives, very long) and compare with `sorted`, in a sandbox with
   time and memory limits. Hacks: special-casing the test inputs if they're fixed (use random ones), calling the built-in `sorted` if the
   task forbids it (check the source), modifying the test runner or the comparison through side effects (isolate it), and returning
   the input when the tests only check the type or length.

4. Mostly sharpening: it moved probability toward answers the base model could already produce, so the first sample is right more
   often, but the set of solvable problems (pass@64) is the same. For users who take the first answer that's a real gain; it's not new
   capability.

5. Measure on labeled support queries: accuracy and cost of the standard model vs the reasoning model, by query type. Route the types
   where the reasoning model's gain justifies its latency and cost (multi-step troubleshooting, policy questions with conditions), using
   a cheap classifier or the standard model's confidence as the router (45.1, 42.3), and monitor both quality and spend.

6. The traces are a supervised signal for every token, available immediately: the small model imitates reasoning it couldn't discover
   (RL gives it a sparse reward only when it finds a correct answer, which it rarely does at first). The large model did the exploration
   once; distillation copies the result.
