# 37.3 Quantization

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 07.2 (floating point), 25.3 (fp16, bf16), 33.1 (memory-bound decoding), 37.2 (the KV cache). Lab: [`lab_37_3_quantization.py`](labs/lab_37_3_quantization.py).*

A 70-billion-parameter model in bf16 is 140 GB of weights: two 80 GB GPUs before a single request. In 4 bits it's about 36 GB: one GPU,
with room for a cache. And because generating a token at small batch sizes means streaming every weight from memory (33.1), a model a quarter
of the size also runs up to four times faster. That's why most open models you'll run locally, and many you'll serve, are quantized, and why
"which quantization?" is now a question in every deployment review.

The catch is that quantization is lossy, and the loss is invisible until you measure it on the right thing. This lesson covers the
formats, the methods, where the quality goes, and how to check it.

## Numbers with fewer bits

**Quantization** maps floating-point values to a small set of integers plus a **scale** (and sometimes a zero point): $q = \text{round}(w / s)$, clamped to the integer range, and $\hat w = s \cdot q$ when used. With symmetric int8, $q \in [-128, 127]$ and $s = \max|w| / 127$. The rounding error is at most $s/2$ per weight, so everything depends on how large $s$ is, which depends on **how many weights share it**:

- **Per tensor**: one scale for the whole matrix. The single largest weight sets the step size for millions of others.
- **Per channel** (per output row): one scale per row. The standard for int8.
- **Per group**: one scale per block of 32 to 128 consecutive weights in a row. The standard for 4-bit formats; the scales add 0.125 to 0.5 bits per weight.

**Floating-point formats with few bits** are the alternative: fp8 (E4M3 and E5M2, supported by recent GPUs for both training and inference), and 4-bit floating formats with shared block scales (NF4 in QLoRA, MXFP4 and NVFP4 in the newest hardware). They spend their few bits on a nonuniform grid that suits bell-shaped weight distributions.

## What the lab measures

The lab quantizes all the linear layers of the course's small GPT (the embeddings stay in full precision, as is common) and measures **validation loss**, not weight error:

| weights | bits per weight | validation loss | increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| fp32 | 32 | 3.5236 | |
| int8, per tensor | 8 | 3.5239 | +0.0003 |
| int8, per channel | 8 | 3.5237 | +0.0001 |
| int4, per channel | 4 | 3.5547 | +0.031 |
| int4, groups of 32 | 4.5 | 3.5434 | +0.020 |
| int3, per channel | 3 | 3.7314 | +0.208 |
| int3, groups of 32 | 3.5 | 3.6500 | +0.126 |

Int8 is free. At 4 bits the cost is visible and at 3 bits large; per-group scales buy back more than a third of the damage for half a bit per weight. Larger models are typically *more* robust to 4-bit quantization than this tiny one (there's more redundancy per layer), which is part of why 4-bit 70B models are practical. Always measure on the model you'll deploy.

## Outliers

Two kinds, with different consequences:

- **Weight outliers**: one weight 20 times larger than the rest of its matrix multiplies the int8 error of a per-tensor scale by 400 in the lab (it rounds everyone else toward zero), and barely affects per-channel scales. Finer-grained scales contain them.
- **Activation outliers**: large language models develop a few feature dimensions with activations tens of times larger than the rest ("massive activations"; Dettmers et al., 2022, found them emerging systematically beyond about 6.7B parameters). Quantizing activations per tensor then wrecks every other feature; in the lab, adding three such features raises the int8 error of a layer's activations 24-fold. **LLM.int8()** handles them by computing the outlier dimensions in fp16 and the rest in int8; **SmoothQuant** moves the difficulty from activations to weights with a per-channel rescaling.

That's why the common case is **weight-only quantization** (int4 weights, bf16 activations, dequantized on the fly inside the matmul kernel): weights are fixed and can be quantized carefully offline; activations change with every input.

## Better rounding: GPTQ and AWQ

Round-to-nearest (RTN) treats every weight independently. But what matters is the layer's *output*, $WX$, for the inputs $X$ the layer actually sees, and rounding errors in different weights can cancel.

**GPTQ** (Frantar et al., 2023) quantizes a layer's input columns one at a time, and after rounding each column, adjusts the not-yet-quantized columns to compensate for its error, using the inverse of the Hessian $H = X^\top X$ computed from a small **calibration set** (a few hundred sequences). It's the Optimal Brain Surgeon idea (1993), made fast enough for billions of weights. The lab implements it in twenty lines: on one layer at 3 bits, the relative output error drops from 0.017 with RTN to 0.005; on the whole model, validation loss at 4 bits goes from 3.543 (RTN) to 3.540, and at 3 bits from 3.650 to 3.603. Same format, same bits, better choices of which way to round. The gain grows as bits shrink.

**AWQ** (Lin et al., 2024) observes that a small fraction of weights matter most (those multiplying large activations) and scales them up before quantization, and the activations down, protecting them without mixed precision. Both need calibration data, and both are sensitive to it: calibrate on text that looks like your use case (code for a code model, the right languages for a multilingual one).

## Formats you'll meet

- **GPTQ and AWQ checkpoints**: 4-bit weights with group size 128, for GPU serving engines (vLLM, TGI, TensorRT-LLM).
- **GGUF** (llama.cpp): a file format with many quantization types (Q8_0, Q4_K_M, Q5_K_S...), using blocks of weights with per-block scales and "k-quants" that mix bit widths across layers; the standard for CPU and laptop inference.
- **bitsandbytes**: int8 (LLM.int8()) and NF4 on the fly in PyTorch, and the base of QLoRA fine-tuning (38.2).
- **fp8**: native on recent GPUs for weights, activations and the KV cache; usually near-lossless and fast.
- **KV cache quantization** (int8 or fp8, sometimes 4-bit): shrinks the cache (37.2), which at long contexts is bigger than the weights.

## What it buys

For decoding at batch size 1, tokens per second are bounded by memory bandwidth divided by the bytes read per token (33.1):

| model | format | size | H100 (3.35 TB/s) | laptop (about 100 GB/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8B | bf16 | 16 GB | ≤ 209 tokens/s | ≤ 6 tokens/s |
| 8B | int4 (g128) | 4.1 GB | ≤ 812 tokens/s | ≤ 24 tokens/s |
| 70B | bf16 | 140 GB | doesn't fit on one | doesn't fit |
| 70B | int4 (g128) | 36 GB | ≤ 93 tokens/s | ≤ 2.8 tokens/s (if it fits) |

These are upper bounds; kernels reach a good fraction of them at small batches. At large batch sizes, serving becomes compute-bound and the speed advantage of weight-only quantization shrinks (the dequantization costs arithmetic), while the memory savings still buy more concurrent requests.

## Measuring the damage

Perplexity on held-out text is the first check and a weak one: small perplexity changes can hide large drops on specific capabilities. Quantization hurts unevenly: long-context retrieval, math and multi-step reasoning, code, low-resource languages and rare knowledge tend to degrade first, while chat on common topics looks fine. Evaluate the quantized model on your own task suite (39.1, 43.1), compare against the unquantized model on identical prompts, and read samples. Also check that the serving stack's kernels match the format (a mismatched group size or zero point is a silent quality bug).

## Things that will bite you

- **Judging quantization by weight error or perplexity alone.**
- **Per-tensor scales at low bit widths.**
- **Calibration data unlike the deployment data.**
- **Quantizing activations** of a large model naively (outlier features).
- **Comparing "4-bit" models** with different group sizes, formats and methods as if they were the same.
- **Forgetting the KV cache** in the memory plan after quantizing weights.
- **Assuming speedups at large batch** from weight-only quantization.

## Exercises

2. Why do per-group scales help more at 4 bits than at 8 bits?
3. Explain in your own words what GPTQ does that round-to-nearest doesn't.
4. Why is weight-only quantization common, while quantizing activations is harder?
5. A 70B model in int4 with group size 128: estimate its size, and whether it fits on one 48 GB GPU with an 8k-token KV cache for one sequence (80 layers, 8 KV heads of 128, bf16 cache).
6. Your quantized model has perplexity within 1% of the original, but users report worse answers to math questions. What would you do?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\max|w| = 0.8$; int4 symmetric has $q_{\max} = 7$, so $s = 0.8/7 \approx 0.114$. The largest rounding error is $s/2 \approx 0.057$ (for values within range).

2. With 8 bits there are 255 levels, so even a coarse scale leaves a fine grid (step $\max|w|/127$); outliers cost little. With 4 bits there are only 15 levels, so the step is $\max|w|/7$, and a single large weight in the row makes the step huge for everyone; smaller groups give each block its own, smaller maximum, which matters much more when levels are scarce.

3. RTN rounds each weight to the nearest level independently, ignoring how errors combine in the output. GPTQ quantizes one input column at a time and, after each, updates the remaining (still full-precision) weights so that the layer's output on real calibration inputs changes as little as possible, using the inverse Hessian of that output error. Errors are steered into directions the actual inputs rarely excite.

4. Weights are fixed: they can be quantized offline, with calibration and careful methods, and dequantized inside the kernel. Activations change with every token and contain outlier features with huge magnitudes in large models, so a static scale either clips the outliers or wastes precision for every other feature; handling them needs per-token or per-channel scales, mixed precision, or rescaling (SmoothQuant). And at small batch sizes, weight bytes dominate memory traffic, so weight-only quantization captures most of the speed gain.

5. Weights: $70 \times 10^9 \times (4 + 16/128)/8$ bytes $\approx 36$ GB. KV cache: $2 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 8192 \times 2$ bytes $\approx 2.7$ GB. Total about 39 GB plus activations and runtime overhead: it fits in 48 GB for one or a few sequences.

6. Build a math evaluation set (from your users' questions, 39.1) and compare the quantized and original models on it, to confirm and measure. Then try less aggressive options: a larger bit width or smaller groups, GPTQ/AWQ instead of RTN, calibration data that includes math, keeping sensitive layers (often the first and last, or attention) in higher precision, or fp8. Choose based on the task metric, not perplexity.
