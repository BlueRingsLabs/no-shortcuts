# 37.1 Decoding strategies

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 35.2 (sampling from a GPT), 28.2 (beam search, teacher forcing), 06.1 (entropy). Lab: [`lab_37_1_decoding.py`](labs/lab_37_1_decoding.py).*

A language model doesn't produce text. It produces a probability distribution over the next token, and something else, the **decoding
strategy**, turns a sequence of those distributions into words. That something is a few lines of code with more influence on the output than
many architecture changes: the same model sounds dull, unhinged, or reasonable depending on it. It's also where you enforce that an output
parses as JSON, matches a schema or is one of five allowed labels, which is the difference between a demo and a system that other code can
depend on.

The lab uses a small GPT trained on this course (shared by the labs of this module and the next, trained once and cached), and runs every
strategy on the same prompt.

## Greedy decoding

Take the most likely token at every step. Deterministic and cheap, and it loops: once a phrase has been generated, repeating it becomes likely, and greedy never takes the less likely exit. The lab's greedy continuation ends in "for the same source of the same source of the same source", with 10% of its 4-grams repeated. Larger models loop less but still do, especially on long outputs.

## Beam search

Keep the $B$ best partial sequences by total log-probability, extend each by its top candidates, keep the best $B$ again (28.2). It finds sequences with higher total probability than greedy: the lab's beam of 4 reaches a mean log-probability of $-1.21$ per token against greedy's $-1.65$. What it found was a stack of short bolded list headings ("Classification.", "Comparing the data."), the most common shape in these lessons, saying nothing.

That's the general lesson (Holtzman et al., 2020): for open-ended generation, **the most probable text is not good text**. Human writing isn't the maximum-likelihood sequence; it has a spread of surprising word choices, and maximizing probability produces bland, repetitive output. Beam search remains right where there's one correct output to find, such as translation, speech recognition or structured extraction, with two details:

- **Length normalization**: summing log-probabilities penalizes every extra token, so raw beam search prefers ending early. Compare finished beams by average log-probability per token, or with a length penalty $\text{score}/\text{length}^\alpha$.
- **Diminishing returns**: widths of 4 to 10 are typical; wider beams sometimes produce *worse* translations (the "beam search curse", Koehn and Knowles, 2017), another sign that the model's probability and quality diverge.

## Sampling

Draw the next token from the distribution. With the unmodified distribution, the long tail of unlikely tokens gets sampled often enough to derail the text (each individual tail token is rare, but together they carry real mass). The fixes reshape or truncate the distribution:

- **Temperature** $T$: divide the logits by $T$ before the softmax. $T < 1$ sharpens (more confident, more repetitive); $T > 1$ flattens (more diverse, more nonsense); $T \to 0$ is greedy.
- **Top-k**: keep only the $k$ most likely tokens (Fan et al., 2018). A fixed $k$ is too many when the model is sure and too few when it isn't.
- **Top-p (nucleus sampling)**: keep the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches $p$ (Holtzman et al., 2020). Adapts to the model's confidence: after "Thank you very", the nucleus is one token; after "My favorite food is", it's hundreds.
- **Min-p**: keep tokens whose probability is at least $p_{\min}$ times the top token's (Nguyen et al., 2024). Also adaptive, and it holds up better at high temperatures.

The lab measures diversity (distinct bigrams across four samples of 50 tokens) against the model's own average log-probability of what it sampled:

| setting | distinct bigrams | mean log-prob |
|---|---|---|
| temperature 0.5 | 0.88 | −2.05 |
| temperature 1.0 | 0.92 | −3.01 |
| temperature 1.5 | 0.99 | −4.77 |
| top-k 20 | 0.91 | −2.41 |
| top-p 0.9 | 0.89 | −2.68 |
| min-p 0.1 | 0.83 | −2.08 |
| temperature 1.5 + min-p 0.1 | 0.92 | −2.72 |

Truncation buys diversity at a fraction of the cost in confidence: temperature 1.5 with min-p is as diverse as temperature 1.0 and more confident than it, while temperature 1.5 alone wanders into nonsense. That's why production APIs expose temperature and top-p (and increasingly min-p) together.

Practical defaults: temperature 0 to 0.3 for extraction, classification, code and anything with a right answer; 0.7 to 1.0 with top-p 0.9 to 0.95 for conversational and creative text. And **set a seed** when you need reproducibility, knowing that many hosted APIs don't guarantee determinism even at temperature 0 (batching and floating-point nondeterminism, 37.2).

## Repetition penalties

Directly discourage tokens that already appeared: the CTRL rule divides positive logits of seen tokens by a penalty (around 1.1 to 1.3); OpenAI-style **frequency and presence penalties** subtract terms proportional to how often, or whether, a token appeared. In the lab, a penalty of 1.3 all but removes greedy's loops (repeated 4-grams from 0.10 to 0.01). It's a blunt instrument: it also penalizes tokens that *should* repeat (function words, names, variable names in code, JSON keys), and it's easy to degrade structured output with it.

## Constrained decoding

When the output must follow a format, don't ask nicely and hope; **mask the logits** of every token that would violate it, at every step. The model can then only choose among valid continuations, so the output is valid by construction:

- The lab constrains a continuation to digits: every output parses as a number, 4 out of 4. And every one is "7", including for "The lab uses a batch size of": this model's favorite number. A valid format is not a correct answer; constraints fix syntax, not knowledge.
- **Grammar-constrained decoding** generalizes this with a finite-state machine or a context-free grammar tracked alongside generation: JSON schemas, regular expressions, SQL, function-call arguments. Libraries (Outlines, XGrammar, llguidance, llama.cpp's GBNF) compile the grammar against the tokenizer's vocabulary, which is fiddly because tokens don't align with grammar symbols (a single token can contain a closing quote and a comma). Hosted APIs expose it as "structured outputs" (40.2).
- **Scoring a closed set of options**: for classification into fixed labels, compute each option's total log-probability after the prompt and pick the best. It never produces an invalid label and gives a score for each. In the lab, "To join two tables you write a query in" scores SQL at $-7.0$, Kafka $-10.5$ and gradient $-10.6$. It's how many multiple-choice benchmarks are scored (39.1), and it's sensitive to how options are tokenized: longer options accumulate more negative log-probability, so normalize by length or compare per-token scores, deliberately.

One caution from practice: forcing a format can lower answer quality if the model would have "reasoned" before answering; constraining the whole output to a JSON object with an answer field removes that room. Let the model write its reasoning in a field first, or constrain only the final part (40.2, 38.4).

> **Scar tissue.** A Telegram bot I run on a small VPS summarizes the feeds I follow, and for its first month about a fifth of the
> summaries stopped mid-sentence. I assumed the model was dumb. The serving library's default `max_new_tokens` was small, my request
> didn't set it, and generation simply stopped. The response even said so, with `finish_reason: length`, in a field I never read. Check
> the stop reason on every generation, and treat `length` as an error unless you asked for it.

## Things that will bite you

- **Greedy or beam search for open-ended text**: loops and blandness.
- **Pure sampling at temperature 1 without truncation**: occasional nonsense from the tail.
- **High temperature for tasks with a right answer**.
- **Repetition penalties on code or structured output**.
- **Parsing free-form output with regexes** instead of constraining it.
- **Assuming temperature 0 is deterministic** on a hosted API.
- **Length bias** when scoring options by total log-probability.
