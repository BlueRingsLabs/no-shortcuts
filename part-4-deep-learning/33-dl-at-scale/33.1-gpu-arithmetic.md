# 33.1 GPU arithmetic: memory, FLOPs and bandwidth

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 24.2 (why backprop stores activations), 25.3 (GPUs, mixed precision), 29.2 (parameter and FLOP counts). Lab: [`lab_33_1_gpu_arithmetic.py`](labs/lab_33_1_gpu_arithmetic.py).*

At some point somebody will ask you three questions before approving a GPU budget: will it fit, how long will it take, and how much will it
cost. The wrong answers are "let's try it and see" (a week of out-of-memory errors), "the paper did it in two days" (on hardware you don't
have), and a number with no arithmetic behind it. The right answer is a page of multiplication, and this lesson is that page. None of it needs
a GPU to learn, which is convenient, since the lab runs on a CPU: memory accounting, FLOP counting and arithmetic intensity are the same
calculations everywhere, and the lab checks each formula against what PyTorch actually does.

## The hardware, in numbers

A data-center GPU is a large number of parallel cores with small, very fast on-chip memory (registers, shared memory/SRAM, L2 cache) in front of a large, much slower **high-bandwidth memory** (HBM). Matrix multiplies run on specialized **tensor cores**, at speeds that depend on precision. Rough figures for an NVIDIA H100 SXM (check the current datasheet; these move every generation):

| | H100 SXM |
|---|---|
| HBM capacity | 80 GB |
| HBM bandwidth | 3.35 TB/s |
| bf16/fp16 dense tensor-core throughput | ~989 TFLOP/s |
| fp8 dense | ~1,979 TFLOP/s |
| fp32 (non-tensor-core) | ~67 TFLOP/s |
| NVLink (GPU to GPU within a node) | 900 GB/s |

Two things to take from the table. Low precision is not a detail: bf16 on tensor cores is about 15 times fp32 on regular cores, which is why mixed precision (25.3) is the default. And arithmetic is cheap next to data movement: the GPU can do about 295 bf16 operations in the time it takes to read one byte from HBM. That ratio organizes everything below.

## Memory: will it fit?

Training memory has four parts.

**Weights, gradients and optimizer state.** With mixed-precision training and Adam, per parameter: bf16 weights (2 bytes), bf16 gradients (2), an fp32 master copy of the weights (4), and Adam's two fp32 moment estimates (4 + 4). **16 bytes per parameter**, before a single activation. (Some setups keep fp32 gradients, 18 bytes.) In pure fp32 it's also 16: weights 4, gradients 4, Adam 8, which the lab measures on a real model: exactly 16.0 bytes per parameter.

So full fine-tuning of a 7B model with Adam needs about 112 GB for the model state alone, more than one 80 GB GPU. A 70B model needs 1.1 TB. Inference in bf16 needs only the weights: 14 GB and 140 GB. That gap is why the parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, 38.2), sharding (FSDP/ZeRO, 33.2), 8-bit optimizers and quantization (37.3) exist.

**Activations.** Everything the forward pass saves for the backward pass (24.2). It scales with batch size × sequence length × width × layers, and, for plain attention, with the $n \times n$ score matrices on top. For a transformer, Korthikanti et al. (2022) estimate about $s\,b\,h\,(34 + 5as/h)$ bytes per layer in 16-bit precision, for sequence length $s$, batch $b$, hidden size $h$ and $a$ heads: a linear part and a part quadratic in $s$. The lab counts every byte autograd saves on a small GPT: 84 kB per token at context 64, 109 kB at 256. The growth is the attention matrices.
At long contexts and large batches, activations exceed everything else, and they're the part you control most directly:

- **Activation checkpointing** stores only some activations (each block's input) and recomputes the rest during the backward pass: about one extra forward pass (roughly 33% more compute) for a large memory saving. The lab's checkpointed model saves 6 to 8 times fewer bytes.
- **FlashAttention** (29.3) doesn't store the $n \times n$ matrices at all.
- **Gradient accumulation** (25.1) runs several small micro-batches before each optimizer step: the same effective batch in less activation memory.
- **Sequence packing** avoids spending memory on padding.

**Temporary buffers and fragmentation**: workspaces for kernels, communication buffers, and memory that the allocator can't reuse because it's fragmented. Budget 10% to 20% extra. `torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated()` after a few steps is the ground truth; estimate first, then measure.

**For inference**, add the **KV cache** (29.3, 37.2): $2 \times$ layers $\times$ KV heads $\times$ head size $\times$ tokens $\times$ bytes, per sequence. At long contexts and high concurrency it's larger than the weights.

## FLOPs: how long will it take?

A matrix multiply of $(m \times k)$ by $(k \times n)$ costs $2mkn$ FLOPs (a multiply and an add per term). For a transformer with $N$ parameters in its matrices, that makes about **$2N$ FLOPs per token** for the forward pass, plus the attention scores, $4 \cdot n \cdot d$ per token per layer (two $n \times d$-by-$d \times n$-shaped products). The backward pass costs about twice the forward (gradients with respect to both inputs and weights), so training is about **$6N$ FLOPs per token**.
The lab counts with PyTorch's `FlopCounterMode`: 16.08 GFLOPs for the forward pass against 16.14 from the formula (the difference is biases and embedding lookups), and forward plus backward is exactly 3 times the forward.

Training time is then total FLOPs over *achieved* FLOP/s:

$$\text{time} = \frac{6 N D}{\text{GPUs} \times \text{peak FLOP/s} \times \text{MFU}},$$

for $D$ training tokens. **Model FLOPs utilization** (MFU, from the PaLM paper) is the fraction of peak you actually get, counting only the model's necessary FLOPs (recomputation for checkpointing doesn't count, though it costs time). Well-tuned large training runs reach 35% to 55%; a first attempt often gets 10% to 20%. Example: a 7B model on 2 trillion tokens is $6 \times 7 \times 10^9 \times 2 \times 10^{12} = 8.4 \times 10^{22}$ FLOPs; on 1,024 H100s at 40% MFU that's about 2.4 days. Multiply by the GPU-hour price and add 30% for failed runs, evaluation and the experiments before it, and you have a budget.

## Bandwidth: why FLOPs aren't the whole story

The **arithmetic intensity** of an operation is FLOPs per byte of memory traffic. The **roofline model** (Williams, Waterman and Patterson, 2009): achieved throughput is the minimum of peak compute and (intensity × memory bandwidth). Below the **ridge point** (peak FLOP/s ÷ bandwidth; about 295 FLOP/byte for an H100 in bf16) an operation is **memory-bound**: the arithmetic units wait for data.

- A large square matmul of size $n$ does $2n^3$ FLOPs on $3n^2$ numbers: intensity grows with $n$, and big matmuls are compute-bound. That's where the tensor cores earn their keep.
- **Elementwise operations** (activations, residual adds, dropout), **normalizations** and **softmax** do a handful of FLOPs per element loaded: intensity well below 1. Always memory-bound, however big the tensor.
- **LLM decoding** with batch size 1 reads every weight once per generated token and does about 2 FLOPs per weight: about 1 FLOP per byte in bf16. Hopelessly memory-bound. The GPU spends its time streaming weights; batching many requests together (37.2) reuses each weight load across sequences, and quantization (37.3) makes each load smaller.

The lab measures this machine's roofline. Matmuls go from 34 GFLOP/s at 32×32 to about 470 at 512 and above, while an elementwise add reaches 7 GFLOP/s at any size, moving about 80 GB/s: pure memory bandwidth. On a GPU the numbers are 100 times larger and the shape is the same.
The consequence for engineering is **fusion**: combine consecutive memory-bound operations into one kernel so the data is read once and written once. That's what `torch.compile` (25.3, 33.3), fused optimizers, fused LayerNorm and FlashAttention do.

## Communication

Once training spans several GPUs (33.2), a third resource enters: interconnect bandwidth. NVLink within a node (hundreds of GB/s per GPU), InfiniBand or Ethernet between nodes (tens of GB/s per GPU). Gradient synchronization for data parallelism moves about $2 \times$ the gradient size per step per GPU (ring all-reduce); tensor parallelism communicates activations inside every layer and needs NVLink-class bandwidth. The same page of arithmetic tells you which parallelism strategy the hardware can sustain.

## Things that will bite you

- **Forgetting the optimizer state**: "7B parameters in bf16 is 14 GB, it fits on my 24 GB card" is true for inference and false for training by a factor of 8.
- **Activations at long context**, estimated with the linear formula.
- **Peak FLOP/s used as achieved FLOP/s**: budget with a realistic MFU.
- **Sparse or fp8 peak numbers** from the datasheet quoted for dense bf16 training.
- **Profiling a small model and extrapolating** to a large one: small models are overhead- and memory-bound in ways large ones aren't.
- **The KV cache** left out of an inference memory plan.
