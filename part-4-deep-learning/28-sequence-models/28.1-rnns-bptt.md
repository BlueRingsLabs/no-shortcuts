Transformers replaced recurrent networks for most sequence work around 2018, and I could skip this module and nobody would complain for about a
year. Then you'd meet a streaming problem (a sensor, a keystroke, an audio frame arriving every 10 ms, a device with 256 KB of RAM) and discover
that "attend to the whole history" is a bad plan when the history never ends. You'd also meet state space models and linear RNNs, which came back
as serious contenders for long sequences, and they're recurrences wearing a new jacket. And vanishing gradients, the problem that shaped every
architecture since, is easiest to see here.

So: what a recurrent network computes, how its gradient flows backward through time, why that gradient dies or explodes, and what practitioners
did about it before and after gating (28.2).

## The recurrence

A sequence $x_1, \dots, x_T$ of vectors (word embeddings, sensor readings, characters as one-hot vectors). A **recurrent neural network** keeps a hidden state $h_t$ and updates it with the same weights at every step:

$$h_t = \tanh(W_{hh} h_{t-1} + W_{xh} x_t + b), \qquad y_t = W_{hy} h_t + c,$$

with $h_0 = 0$ (or learned). The state is a fixed-size summary of everything seen so far; the output at each step reads from it.

Two ideas, both familiar:

- **Weight sharing across time**, the same way a convolution shares weights across space (27.1). The model has the same number of parameters for sequences of length 5 and 5,000, and it can process lengths it never saw in training (whether it does that *well* is another question).
- **Unrolling**: for a given sequence, the recurrence is just a deep feedforward network with $T$ layers that share weights. Everything in 24.2 applies to that unrolled graph.

The shapes of problems it covers:

- **Many-to-one**: read a sequence, output one thing at the end (sentiment, a fraud score for a session, "did this machine fail").
- **Many-to-many, aligned**: an output per input step (tagging each word, predicting the next character).
- **Sequence-to-sequence**, unaligned: read one sequence, write another of a different length (translation, summarization). That's the encoder-decoder of 28.2.

**Bidirectional** RNNs run one recurrence forward and one backward and concatenate their states, so each position sees both sides. Useful for tagging a finished sentence; useless for anything that must answer before the sequence ends.
**Stacked** RNNs feed one layer's states as the next layer's inputs.

## Backpropagation through time

**BPTT** is backprop on the unrolled graph. The loss is usually a sum over steps, $L = \sum_t L_t$. Since $W_{hh}$ is used at every step, its gradient is a sum of contributions from every step, and each contribution travels backward through the chain of hidden states:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{hh}} = \sum_t \sum_{k \le t} \frac{\partial L_t}{\partial h_t}\,\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k}\,\frac{\partial^+ h_k}{\partial W_{hh}},$$

where $\partial^+$ is the "immediate" derivative (holding $h_{k-1}$ fixed). The factor that matters is the Jacobian between distant states:

$$\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k} = \prod_{i=k+1}^{t} \operatorname{diag}\!\left(1 - h_i^2\right) W_{hh}.$$

A product of $t - k$ matrices. The lab implements BPTT by hand in NumPy, checks it against autograd, and then looks at that product.

## Vanishing and exploding gradients

Take norms. The tanh derivative is at most 1, so $\|\partial h_t / \partial h_k\| \le (\sigma_{\max}(W_{hh}))^{t-k}$, where $\sigma_{\max}$ is the largest singular value (Bengio, Simard and Frasconi, 1994; Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013).

- If $\sigma_{\max} < 1$, the bound shrinks **exponentially** in the distance $t - k$: the gradient from an error at step 100 carries essentially nothing back to step 1. The network can't learn that step 1 mattered. Saturated tanh units (derivative near 0) make it worse.
- If the largest eigenvalues are above 1 (a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, because tanh can squash), the product can grow exponentially: a gradient of $10^{12}$, a parameter update that throws the model into nonsense, NaNs.

This isn't a bug in an implementation; it's what repeated multiplication by the same matrix does. The same analysis for a deep feedforward network is 26.2's initialization story; the difference is that an RNN multiplies by the **same** matrix at every step, so there's no averaging out.
The lab measures $\|\partial h_T / \partial h_t\|$ as a function of $T - t$ for different scales of $W_{hh}$: straight lines on a log axis. With random weights of spectral radius about $g$, a linear recurrence follows $g^{T-t}$ exactly. A tanh recurrence vanishes at $g = 0.8$, at $g = 1$ and even at $g = 1.5$, because saturated units contribute derivatives near zero.
At $g = 3$ it becomes chaotic and explodes anyway ($10^{10}$ after 100 steps, against $10^{53}$ for the linear one). Vanishing is the default; exploding is the occasional disaster.

The consequence that matters in practice: a vanilla RNN trained by gradient descent learns short-range dependencies and **fails silently on long ones**. The loss goes down (the short-range structure is learned), so nothing looks broken. The lab trains one on a task where the answer depends on the first input and only the first input, and increases the delay until it can't: perfect at 20 steps, 91% at 50, a coin flip at 100, with the same model and the same training budget.

## What people did about it

- **Gradient clipping** (Pascanu et al., 2013): if the gradient norm exceeds a threshold, rescale it to the threshold. It fixes the *exploding* case (the direction is kept, the step size is bounded) and does nothing for vanishing. In PyTorch: `torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)` between `backward()` and `step()`. Cheap enough that everyone uses it for recurrent models, and for transformers too.
- **Initialization**: an orthogonal $W_{hh}$ (all singular values 1) keeps the linear part of the product norm-preserving, at least at the start; the IRNN (Le, Jaitly and Hinton, 2015) used ReLU with an identity recurrent matrix.
- **Gating** (the LSTM, 1997, and the GRU, 2014): an additive path through time whose gradient doesn't have to pass through a squashing nonlinearity and a matrix product at every step. That's 28.2, and it's what actually worked.
- **Shortcuts through time**: attention (28.2, then 29) lets any step read any earlier step directly, so the path length between two positions is 1 instead of $t - k$. That's the real reason transformers won.

## Truncated BPTT

Full BPTT on a sequence of length $T$ stores $T$ hidden states and costs a backward pass through all of them. For long streams (a novel, a year of sensor data) that's impossible, so you **truncate**: split the stream into chunks of $k$ steps, carry the hidden state forward from chunk to chunk (the model still *sees* the whole history through the state), but stop the gradient at chunk boundaries (`h = h.detach()` in PyTorch).

The consequence to understand: **gradients never cross a chunk boundary**, so the model gets no training signal for dependencies longer than $k$ steps. It can still carry information further by accident, but nothing teaches it to. The lab makes this concrete: a GRU asked to output the bit it saw 8 steps earlier, trained on the same number of time steps with windows of 4 and 32. With 32 it's perfect; with 4 it stays at chance, although the hidden state could carry the bit either way.

Forgetting the `detach()` is the classic bug: the graph grows with every chunk, memory climbs until the process dies, and each backward pass gets slower. Detaching in the wrong place (before the state is used) is the other one: the state stops carrying information at all.

## Training a language model, and sampling from it

A **character-level language model** predicts the next character from the previous ones: many-to-many, targets are the inputs shifted by one. It's the classic RNN demo (Karpathy, 2015), and it teaches three things that carry straight into large language models (Part V):

- **Teacher forcing**: during training, the input at step $t$ is the *true* previous character, not the model's prediction. Fast and stable. The cost is **exposure bias**: at generation time the model conditions on its own outputs, a distribution it never trained on, and one early mistake can compound.
- **Sampling**: generate by feeding the model its own sample, one step at a time. **Temperature** divides the logits before the softmax: below 1 sharpens (safer, more repetitive), above 1 flattens (more varied, more nonsense). Greedy decoding (always the argmax) tends to loop.
- **Perplexity**, $\exp(\text{mean cross-entropy per token})$, is the standard metric: the effective number of equally likely choices the model is hesitating between. A uniform guess over 80 characters has perplexity 80.

The lab trains one on the text of this course. In under a minute on a CPU it gets to a validation perplexity of about 5 (unigram frequencies: 29). It learns to spell my headings long before it learns to make a point, which is also true of some consultants.

## Practicalities

- **Batching variable lengths**: pad and mask (25.2), or pack. PyTorch's `pack_padded_sequence` lets the built-in RNNs skip the padding entirely, so the final hidden state is the one at each sequence's true end, not after 40 steps of padding. Taking `h[:, -1]` of a padded batch is a quiet, common bug.
- **Use the built-in modules** (`nn.RNN`, `nn.LSTM`, `nn.GRU`): they're fused and far faster than a Python loop over time steps, especially on GPUs (cuDNN).
- **`batch_first`**: PyTorch's recurrent modules default to (time, batch, features). Set `batch_first=True` or be careful; a transposed input often runs without error.
- **Dropout** goes between stacked layers (the `dropout` argument), not on the recurrent connection, unless you use the same mask at every step (variational dropout, Gal and Ghahramani, 2016).
- **Where recurrence still earns its keep**: streaming and on-device inference, where each step costs $O(1)$ time and memory regardless of history length (a transformer's per-token cost grows with the context). The modern **state space models** (S4, Mamba) and linear-recurrence models (RWKV) are recurrences designed so that training is parallel over time and inference is a cheap recurrence. They are the reason this module isn't only history.

> **Scar tissue.** An LSTM sentiment model I built on forum posts classified long posts much better than short ones, and I spent a while
> theorizing about why short posts were harder. They weren't. The batches were padded to the longest post, and the model read the final
> hidden state, which for short posts came after dozens of padding tokens that washed out everything it had read. Packing the sequences
> (or taking the state at each sequence's real last token) fixed it. The theory was more elegant than the bug.

## Things that will bite you

- **Silent failure on long-range dependencies**: the loss decreases, the model learned only the short-range part.
- **No gradient clipping**, and a loss that goes to NaN at step 30,000.
- **Truncated BPTT** with a window shorter than the dependency you need.
- **A missing `detach()`** between chunks (memory grows forever) or one in the wrong place.
- **Reading the last time step of a padded batch** instead of each sequence's true last step.
- **`batch_first` confusion**: time and batch swapped, no error.
- **Bidirectional models in a streaming product**: they need the future.

## Exercises

1. An RNN has input size 10, hidden size 20 and output size 5 (with biases). How many parameters does it have, and how does that change for sequences of length 1,000 instead of 10?
2. Write the Jacobian $\partial h_t / \partial h_{t-1}$ for $h_t = \tanh(W_{hh} h_{t-1} + W_{xh} x_t + b)$.
3. $W_{hh} = 0.9 I$ and the tanh units are in their linear range. By how much is the gradient scaled going back 50 steps? And with $W_{hh} = 1.1 I$?
4. Why does gradient clipping help with exploding gradients but not with vanishing ones?
5. You train a character model with truncated BPTT, windows of 50 characters, and it never learns to close a parenthesis opened 80 characters earlier. Why, and what would you change?
6. A model's validation cross-entropy is 1.2 nats per character. What's its perplexity, and how does it compare with uniform guessing over 80 characters?

1. $W_{xh}$: $20 \times 10 = 200$; $W_{hh}$: $20 \times 20 = 400$; $b$: 20; $W_{hy}$: $5 \times 20 = 100$; $c$: 5. Total 725, independent of the sequence length: the same weights are reused at every step.

2. $\partial h_t / \partial h_{t-1} = \operatorname{diag}(1 - h_t^2)\, W_{hh}$, since $\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z)$.

3. In the linear range each step multiplies by $W_{hh}$: $0.9^{50} \approx 0.005$ (the gradient shrinks about 200 times) and $1.1^{50} \approx 117$ (it grows over 100 times). Over 200 steps: $0.9^{200} \approx 7 \times 10^{-10}$ and $1.1^{200} \approx 2 \times 10^{8}$.

4. Clipping rescales the whole gradient when its norm is too large: it bounds a step that would otherwise be catastrophic, keeping its direction. When gradients vanish, the contributions from distant steps are tiny *relative to* the short-range ones inside the same gradient; clipping (or any rescaling of the whole vector) doesn't change that ratio, so the long-range signal is still lost.

5. With 50-step windows, the gradient never flows more than 50 steps back, so no update ever connects the closing position with the opening 80 characters earlier; the state might carry the information, but nothing trains it to. Use longer windows (memory permitting), a gated model (28.2) that keeps information more easily, or attention; and check with a targeted evaluation (accuracy on closing brackets by distance), because the average loss won't show it.

6. $e^{1.2} \approx 3.3$: the model is, on average, as uncertain as a uniform choice among about 3.3 characters, versus 80 for uniform guessing.
