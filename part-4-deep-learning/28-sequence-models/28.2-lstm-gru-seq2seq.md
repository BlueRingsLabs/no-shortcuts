# 28.2 LSTM, GRU and seq2seq with attention

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 28.1. Lab: [`lab_28_2_lstm_seq2seq.py`](labs/lab_28_2_lstm_seq2seq.py).*

The vanilla RNN of 28.1 forgets because every step squeezes the whole history through a matrix and a tanh. The fix, invented in 1997 and ignored
for about fifteen years, was to give the network a second state that is updated by **addition** and controlled by **gates**: learned switches that
decide what to keep, what to write and what to show. LSTMs then ran speech recognition, machine translation, handwriting recognition and
keyboard prediction on a billion phones, until attention made them optional.

This lesson covers the LSTM and the GRU (what each gate does and why the gradient survives), the encoder-decoder for sequence-to-sequence problems,
the bottleneck that broke it, and the fix, **attention**, which is the bridge to transformers (29). The lab builds an LSTM cell by hand, repeats
28.1's delay experiment with gates (and finds that gates alone aren't enough), and trains a small seq2seq model with and without attention on a task where the difference is obvious.

## The LSTM

The **long short-term memory** (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; the forget gate added by Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins, 2000) keeps a **cell state** $c_t$ next to the hidden state $h_t$. At each step, from $x_t$ and $h_{t-1}$, it computes three gates (sigmoids, values in $(0, 1)$) and a candidate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_t &= \sigma(W_f [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f) && \text{forget: how much of the old cell to keep} \\
i_t &= \sigma(W_i [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i) && \text{input: how much of the candidate to write} \\
o_t &= \sigma(W_o [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o) && \text{output: how much of the cell to expose} \\
\tilde c_t &= \tanh(W_c [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c) && \text{candidate content} \\
c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde c_t \\
h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(c_t)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why the gradient survives**: along the cell state, $\partial c_t / \partial c_{t-1} = \operatorname{diag}(f_t)$ (plus smaller terms through the gates' dependence on $h_{t-1}$). No weight matrix, no tanh derivative, just the forget gate. When the network wants to remember, it sets $f_t \approx 1$ and the gradient passes back almost unchanged,
for hundreds of steps. That's the "constant error carousel" of the original paper, and it's the same idea as the residual connection (26.4): an additive path the gradient can travel without being multiplied by something small at every step.

Two details that matter in practice:

- **Initialize the forget gate bias to 1 or more** (Jozefowicz, Zaremba and Sutskever, 2015), so the network starts by remembering and learns to forget, rather than the reverse. PyTorch initializes it like every other bias (uniform around 0); setting it is one line, and the lab measures the difference.
  It's bigger than I expected. On 28.1's delay task, an LSTM with the default bias can't even learn a 20-step delay in the training budget where a *vanilla RNN* learns it perfectly, because with $f \approx \sigma(0) = 0.5$ the cell halves its memory every step. With a bias of 2 it learns 20 steps at once, and fails at 100: $\sigma(2)^{100} \approx 3 \times 10^{-6}$ of the memory survives 100 steps.
  With a bias of 4, $\sigma(4)^{100} \approx 0.16$ survives, and it gets 99.8%. The rule of thumb "1 or 2" is for typical dependencies; the right value depends on how far back the signal is, and the gradient through the cell decays exactly as fast as the memory.
- The four gate computations are one matrix multiply producing $4H$ outputs, split four ways: that's why `nn.LSTM`'s weight matrices have $4H$ rows, in the order input, forget, cell, output.

## The GRU

The **gated recurrent unit** (Cho et al., 2014) merges the cell and hidden state and uses two gates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= \sigma(W_z [h_{t-1}, x_t]) && \text{update} \\
r_t &= \sigma(W_r [h_{t-1}, x_t]) && \text{reset} \\
\tilde h_t &= \tanh(W_h [r_t \odot h_{t-1}, x_t]) \\
h_t &= (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde h_t
\end{aligned}
$$

The update gate interpolates between keeping the old state and writing a new one: the same additive path, with one fewer gate. (PyTorch's convention swaps the roles of $z$ and $1 - z$ and applies the reset gate after the matrix multiply; the math is equivalent, and it will confuse you exactly once when you port weights.)
Empirically, LSTMs and GRUs perform about the same on most tasks (Chung et al., 2014; Greff et al., 2017, who also found that the forget gate and the output activation are the parts that matter). The GRU is a little cheaper; pick either, tune the rest.

## Sequence to sequence

Translation maps a sequence to another of a different length and order. The **encoder-decoder** (Sutskever, Vinyals and Le, 2014; Cho et al., 2014):

1. An **encoder** RNN reads the source and ends in a final state: a fixed-size vector meant to summarize the whole input.
2. A **decoder** RNN starts from that state and generates the target one token at a time, each step conditioned on the previous token (teacher forcing in training, its own samples at inference, 28.1), until it emits an end-of-sequence token.

It worked better than anyone expected (Sutskever et al. found that reversing the source sentence helped, a hint that short paths between related words matter). And it has an obvious flaw: **the whole source must squeeze through one vector**. Quality drops as sentences get longer, because a 50-word sentence and a 5-word one get the same few hundred numbers.
The lab trains one to reverse strings of 4 to 20 digits: it gets 4-digit strings right 99.8% of the time, 12-digit strings 18% of the time, and 20-digit strings never.

## Attention

**Attention** (Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio, 2015) removes the bottleneck: keep **all** the encoder states $s_1, \dots, s_n$, and at each decoder step compute a weighted average of them, with weights chosen by the decoder's current state:

$$e_{tj} = \text{score}(h_{t-1}, s_j), \qquad \alpha_{tj} = \frac{\exp(e_{tj})}{\sum_k \exp(e_{tk})}, \qquad \text{context}_t = \sum_j \alpha_{tj}\, s_j.$$

The context vector goes into the decoder step along with the previous token. The score can be a small MLP (Bahdanau's "additive" attention) or a dot product $h^\top W s$ (Luong, Pham and Manning, 2015).

What it changes:

- **No bottleneck**: the decoder can look up the relevant source positions at each step, so long inputs stop degrading quality so badly.
- **Short gradient paths**: every source position is one step away from every decoder step, instead of $n$ steps back through the encoder recurrence. That's the fix for 28.1's vanishing gradients, by a different route than gating.
In the lab, the same model with attention reverses 20-digit strings exactly 96% of the time.

- **Interpretability, sort of**: the weights $\alpha_{tj}$ form an alignment matrix. For translation it looks like a soft word alignment; for the lab's task you can read the algorithm off it: the weights sit on the anti-diagonal (output step $t$ reads input position $L - 1 - t$), which nobody told the model. Attention weights are a view of the computation, not an explanation of the decision (29.1 and 22.3 argue about this).

Then someone asked what happens if you drop the recurrence and keep only the attention, which is 29.

## Decoding

Generating from a seq2seq model (or any autoregressive model) is a search problem: the most probable output sequence is not the sequence of most probable tokens.

- **Greedy**: the argmax at each step. Fast; commits early and can't recover.
- **Beam search**: keep the $k$ best partial sequences at each step, extend each, keep the best $k$ again. Standard for translation ($k$ around 4 to 10); needs a **length penalty**, because summing log-probabilities favors short outputs.
- **Sampling** with temperature, top-$k$ or nucleus (top-$p$): for open-ended generation, where the most probable text is bland and repetitive (37.1).

## Where this leaves RNNs

Transformers (29) beat LSTMs on nearly every benchmark with enough data, because attention gives short paths between all positions and trains in parallel over the sequence (an RNN is sequential by definition). LSTMs and GRUs remain reasonable for **small data**, **streaming** and **low-latency on-device** inference, and as strong baselines for time series (though check against gradient-boosted features, 23.1, which often win). The ideas outlived the architecture: gating is in every modern MLP block (SwiGLU, 29.2), additive paths are residual connections, and encoder-decoder attention is cross-attention.

## Things that will bite you

- **Forget gate bias at 0**: the network starts by forgetting everything.
- **Gate order** when porting weights between frameworks (input, forget, cell, output in PyTorch; other orders elsewhere; PyTorch's GRU convention).
- **The seq2seq bottleneck** on long inputs, blamed on "not enough training".
- **Attention without masking the padding** in the source: the decoder attends to pad tokens (29.1 does masks properly).
- **Teacher forcing at evaluation time** (feeding the true previous token when measuring quality): impressive numbers, useless model.
- **Beam search without a length penalty**: short, truncated outputs.

1. $c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde c_t$, so with the gates and candidate held fixed, $\partial c_t / \partial c_{t-1} = \operatorname{diag}(f_t)$. With $f_t = 1$ (and in practice close to 1), the gradient passes through unchanged; over $k$ steps it's scaled by $\prod f$, which the network controls, instead of a product of weight matrices and tanh derivatives.

2. LSTM: four gates, each with input weights $64 \times 32$, recurrent weights $64 \times 64$, and PyTorch's two bias vectors of 64: $4 \times (2048 + 4096 + 128) = 25{,}088$. GRU: three gates: $3 \times 6272 = 18{,}816$.

3. With a bias near 0, $f_t \approx \sigma(0) = 0.5$ at the start, so the cell state halves every step and both information and gradient decay like $0.5^k$: the vanilla-RNN problem again. A bias of 1 to 2 gives $f_t \approx 0.73$ to $0.88$; the cell initially remembers, gradients flow back further, and the network learns long-range dependencies before learning when to forget.

4. The decoder only sees the encoder's final state: one fixed-size vector, whatever the input length, so long inputs lose information. Attention gives the decoder access to every encoder state at every step, weighted by relevance, so the information available grows with the input.

5. $0.7(1, 0) + 0.2(0, 1) + 0.1(1, 1) = (0.8, 0.3)$.

6. Teacher forcing feeds the true previous token at each step, so the model never has to deal with its own mistakes; at deployment it conditions on its own outputs, and errors compound (exposure bias). Evaluate with free-running decoding (greedy or beam) and a sequence-level metric (exact match, BLEU or chrF for translation, human evaluation where it matters). The lab's no-attention model shows the gap: on 20-digit inputs, 68% token accuracy with teacher forcing and 39% free-running.
