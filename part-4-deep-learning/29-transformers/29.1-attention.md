Every large language model, most modern vision models, the protein structure predictors, the speech recognizers and the diffusion models
that draw your conference slides are built around one operation that fits on a single line. People who use these models daily often can't
write that line, which is fine until they have to debug a mask that lets a model read the answer, explain why a 128k context costs what it
costs, or read a paper that changes one term. So this lesson writes the line, derives every piece of it, and breaks it a few times on purpose.

28.2 met attention as an add-on to a recurrent decoder. Here it stands on its own.

## A soft dictionary lookup

A Python dictionary maps a query to the value whose key matches exactly. **Attention** does the same thing softly: compare a **query** with every **key**, turn the similarities into weights that sum to 1, and return the weighted average of the **values**.

For one query $q \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$, keys $k_1, \dots, k_n \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ and values $v_1, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$:

$$\alpha_j = \frac{\exp(q^\top k_j / \sqrt{d_k})}{\sum_{j'} \exp(q^\top k_{j'} / \sqrt{d_k})}, \qquad \text{output} = \sum_j \alpha_j v_j.$$

Stack the queries as rows of $Q$ ($m \times d_k$), keys as $K$ ($n \times d_k$), values as $V$ ($n \times d_v$), and all queries run at once:

$$\operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V,$$

with the softmax applied to each row. That's the line: **scaled dot-product attention** (Vaswani et al., 2017). Two matrix multiplies and a softmax. The output for each query is a convex combination of the values, so attention can only *mix* information that's already in $V$; transforming it is the job of the rest of the block (29.2).

If the kernel view from 19.3 rings a bell, it should: with a fixed similarity function, this is the **Nadaraya-Watson kernel smoother** (1964), a weighted k-nearest-neighbors (19.1) with an exponential kernel. What's new is that queries, keys and values are **learned** projections of the input, so the model learns what "similar" means. The lab fits a Nadaraya-Watson regression and shows it's the same formula.

## Where Q, K and V come from

In a network, the three come from the input by learned linear maps. For a sequence of $n$ token vectors $X$ ($n \times d$):

$$Q = X W_Q, \qquad K = X W_K, \qquad V = X W_V.$$

- **Self-attention**: $Q$, $K$ and $V$ all come from the same sequence. Each token looks at all the others (and itself) and collects what it needs. This is the transformer's core.
- **Cross-attention**: queries from one sequence, keys and values from another. The decoder reading the encoder in 28.2 is cross-attention; so is a diffusion model reading a text prompt (31.4), and a VLM's text reading image features.

Separate projections matter: "what am I looking for" (query), "what do I contain, for matching" (key) and "what do I hand over if selected" (value) are different questions, and a token's answers to them needn't be the same.

## Why divide by the square root of $d_k$

Suppose the components of $q$ and $k$ are independent with mean 0 and variance 1 (roughly what initialization and normalization give you). Then $q^\top k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i$ is a sum of $d_k$ terms with mean 0 and variance 1 each, so it has **variance $d_k$** (04.3): for $d_k = 128$, a standard deviation of about 11.

A softmax of numbers spread that widely is nearly one-hot: one weight close to 1, the rest close to 0. That's bad twice. The model can't average, and the softmax's gradient, $\operatorname{diag}(\alpha) - \alpha\alpha^\top$, is close to zero when $\alpha$ is one-hot, so learning stalls: the **saturation** problem of 24.1 in a new place.
Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ brings the variance back to 1, whatever the head size. The lab measures softmax entropy and the gradient reaching the scores against $d_k$, with and without the scaling.
Scaled, nothing moves. Unscaled, the average largest weight goes from 0.57 at $d_k = 16$ to 0.94 at $d_k = 1024$, and the typical query's gradient first grows (sharper softmax) and then collapses to about a fortieth of the scaled one. A model whose gradient scale depends on the head size is a model whose learning rate depends on it too.

(The factor is a convention, not a law. Some models use learned temperatures, and some normalize queries and keys before the dot product, "QK-norm", to stop the logits growing during training, 35.5.)

## Masks

Two kinds, same mechanism: set the score to $-\infty$ **before** the softmax, so the weight comes out exactly 0.

- **Padding mask**: sequences in a batch have different lengths (25.2); real tokens must not attend to padding.
- **Causal mask**: in a language model predicting token $t+1$, position $t$ must not see positions after $t$, or training is trivial and generation is impossible. The mask is upper-triangular: position $i$ attends to $j \le i$.

Getting this wrong is the most expensive bug in the module: a causal mask that leaks one position produces a model with a spectacular training loss that generates garbage, and nothing crashes. The lab plants it in a tiny character model: validation loss 0.04 nats per character against 1.96 for the correct mask (a unigram model gets 3.4), 5.7 at a position with no future to peek at, and greedy generation that prints "eeeeeeee" forever. Two smaller traps: multiplying the weights by a 0/1 mask **after** the softmax (the remaining weights no longer sum to 1), and a row where **everything** is masked (softmax of all $-\infty$ is NaN, 0/0), which happens with left-padding and some packing schemes.

## Multi-head attention

One attention computes one weighting per query: one notion of "relevant". Language needs several at once (the subject of this verb, the antecedent of this pronoun, the previous token, the matching bracket). **Multi-head attention** runs $h$ attentions in parallel on lower-dimensional projections and concatenates the results:

$$\text{head}_i = \operatorname{Attention}(XW_Q^{(i)}, XW_K^{(i)}, XW_V^{(i)}), \qquad \operatorname{MHA}(X) = \operatorname{concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h)\, W_O,$$

with each head of size $d/h$, so the total cost is about the same as one head of size $d$. In code there's no loop over heads: project to $d$, reshape to $(batch, h, n, d/h)$, run batched attention, reshape back. The lab writes it and matches `nn.MultiheadAttention`.

Heads do specialize, and some are easy to name (previous-token heads, and the "induction heads" that copy patterns, which 29.2's lab builds). Many heads can also be pruned with little loss (Michel, Levy and Neubig, 2019). Specialization is real and redundancy is too.

## Properties worth knowing

- **Permutation equivariance**: shuffle the input tokens and self-attention's outputs are shuffled the same way, nothing else changes. Self-attention treats its input as a **set**. Word order has to be injected separately: **positional encodings**, 29.3. The lab checks this numerically.
- **Every pair of positions is one step apart.** The path between tokens 1 and 1,000 has length 1, versus 999 recurrent steps (28.1). That's what fixed long-range learning, and what makes training parallel over the sequence.
- **Cost is quadratic in length**: the score matrix is $n \times n$, so time is $O(n^2 d)$ and naive memory is $O(n^2)$ per head. At $n = 1{,}000$ that's nothing; at $n = 100{,}000$ it's the reason for FlashAttention, sparse and linear attention, and a whole research area (29.3).
- **At generation time**, each new token's query attends to all previous keys and values, which are cached (the **KV cache**, 37.2): per-token cost grows linearly with the context, and the cache, not the weights, is often what fills GPU memory.

## Attention weights are not explanations

It's tempting to read an attention matrix as "the model looked here because this mattered". Sometimes that's roughly right, and 28.2's reversal alignment is a clean example. In general it isn't reliable: different attention patterns can produce the same output, weights can be permuted or adversarially altered without changing predictions in many settings (Jain and Wallace, 2019), and the values being mixed have already been transformed by earlier layers, so "position 7" in layer 20 may carry information from everywhere.
Wiegreffe and Pinter (2019) answered that attention *can* be informative under proper tests. The practical rule: use attention maps to generate hypotheses, and test them with interventions (ablate, patch, perturb, 22.3), not with heatmaps in a slide deck.

## In PyTorch

- `F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, attn_mask=None, is_causal=False)`: the fused implementation, which dispatches to FlashAttention or memory-efficient kernels when it can (29.3). Use it in real code. Note that a boolean `attn_mask` means **True = may attend**, the opposite of `key_padding_mask` in `nn.MultiheadAttention`, where True means **ignore**. Yes, really.
- `nn.MultiheadAttention(embed_dim, num_heads, batch_first=True)`: defaults to `batch_first=False` (time first, like the RNNs); by default it returns weights averaged over heads (`need_weights=True`, which also disables the fast path).

## Things that will bite you

- **A leaky causal mask**: a wonderful training loss, garbage generations.
- **Masking after the softmax** instead of before.
- **Fully masked rows** producing NaNs.
- **Opposite boolean mask conventions** between PyTorch functions.
- **`batch_first=False`** in `nn.MultiheadAttention`, silently.
- **Forgetting the $\sqrt{d_k}$** in a hand-written attention: it trains, badly, and worse with larger heads.
- **Reading attention maps as explanations.**
- **Quadratic memory** at long sequence lengths, discovered in production.
