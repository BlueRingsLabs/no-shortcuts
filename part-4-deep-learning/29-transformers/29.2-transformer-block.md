# 29.2 The transformer block, built from scratch

*About 12 hours. Prerequisites: 29.1, 26.2 (warmup, initialization), 26.4 (LayerNorm, residual connections), 24.1 (GELU). Lab: [`lab_29_2_transformer_block.py`](labs/lab_29_2_transformer_block.py).*

A transformer is attention (29.1) plus four boring things: a small MLP, two normalization layers, two residual connections, and a lot of
repetition. The boring things are where the engineering is. Put the normalization in the wrong place and a deep model sits at the loss of a
unigram counter no matter how long you wait; tie two matrices that look like they should be tied and a simple circuit never forms; count
parameters wrong and your compute budget is off by a third. This lesson assembles the block, counts what's in it, trains it, and looks inside
at one of the few mechanisms people have actually reverse-engineered.

## The block

A decoder block in the **pre-norm** arrangement used by GPT-2 and nearly every model since:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &\leftarrow x + \operatorname{MHA}(\operatorname{LN}(x)) \\
x &\leftarrow x + \operatorname{MLP}(\operatorname{LN}(x))
\end{aligned}
$$

with a causal mask inside the attention for a language model. Stack $L$ of them between a token embedding (plus positions, 29.3) at the bottom and a final LayerNorm and a linear **unembedding** to vocabulary logits at the top. That's GPT.

Two ways to read it:

- **Communication, then computation**: attention is the only place where positions exchange information; the MLP works on each position independently. Every block lets tokens talk, then lets each think about what it heard.
- **The residual stream** (Elhage et al., 2021): the vector $x$ at each position is a shared bus. Every attention layer and MLP *reads* from it (through a LayerNorm) and *adds* its result back. Nothing overwrites anything; information written by layer 2 is still there, as a direction in the stream, for layer 20 to read. It's a useful picture for interpretability and for understanding why depth works at all (26.4's residual argument, at scale).

## The MLP

$\operatorname{MLP}(x) = W_2\,\phi(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2$, expanding from $d$ to $4d$ and back, with $\phi$ = GELU (24.1). About two thirds of a block's parameters live here, and much of what people call a model's "knowledge" seems to be stored in these layers (Geva et al., 2021, read them as key-value memories).

Modern LLMs use a **gated** variant, **SwiGLU** (Shazeer, 2020): $\operatorname{MLP}(x) = W_2\left(\operatorname{Swish}(W_1 x) \odot W_3 x\right)$, three matrices instead of two, with the hidden size cut to about $\frac{8}{3}d$ to keep the parameter count equal. It trains to a slightly better loss for the same compute, which at pretraining scale is worth a lot of money. Gating again (28.2), in a place nobody expected it to matter.

## Where the normalization goes

The 2017 transformer was **post-norm**: $x \leftarrow \operatorname{LN}(x + f(x))$. Every residual addition is followed by a normalization, so the residual stream itself is normalized at every layer, and the gradient reaching early layers has to pass through $L$ LayerNorms on the way down. Post-norm transformers need a **learning rate warmup** to train at all, and get harder to train as they get deeper (Xiong et al., 2020, showed why: at initialization the gradients near the output are large, and warmup keeps early steps from wrecking the model).

**Pre-norm** moves the normalization inside the branch, $x \leftarrow x + f(\operatorname{LN}(x))$. The residual stream is never normalized, so there's an identity path from the loss to every block (26.4), and training is stable without careful warmup. It needs one extra LayerNorm before the unembedding, since the stream's scale grows with depth.

The lab trains an 8-block model at a high learning rate (1e-2): pre-norm trains; post-norm sits on the unigram plateau (loss 3.42) for all 200 steps, with or without a 100-step warmup. At 3e-3 the story is less clean and more instructive: in my development runs over three seeds, post-norm without warmup got stuck on one and trained on two, **and on those two it was ahead of pre-norm** after 200 steps.
That matches the literature: post-norm often reaches a better model when it trains (Liu et al., 2020), and fails unpredictably when it doesn't. For a model that costs millions to train, "usually trains, slightly better" loses to "always trains". Pre-norm won. Some recent models add normalization back on the branch outputs too ("sandwich" norms, 35.5), trying to have both.

Use **RMSNorm** (26.4) instead of LayerNorm in new code; it's what Llama-style models use, and it's cheaper.

## Three shapes

- **Decoder-only** (GPT, Llama, most LLMs): causal self-attention only. Trained to predict the next token; generates by feeding its output back in.
- **Encoder-only** (BERT, 36.1): bidirectional self-attention, no mask. Every position sees the whole input; trained by masking tokens and predicting them. Produces representations for classification, retrieval and tagging, not text.
- **Encoder-decoder** (the original transformer, T5): an encoder over the input, a causal decoder over the output, and **cross-attention** in each decoder block reading the encoder's output (28.2's seq2seq, without the recurrence). Natural for translation and other input-to-output tasks.

Decoder-only won for general-purpose models mostly because it's simple and scales: one stack, one objective, and any task can be written as text in, text out.

## Embeddings, and tying

The input side maps token IDs to vectors with an embedding matrix $E$ ($V \times d$); the output side maps the final vector to logits with an unembedding $U$ ($V \times d$), logits $= U x$. **Weight tying** sets $U = E$ (Press and Wolf, 2017): it saves $Vd$ parameters (39M of GPT-2 small's 124M are embeddings) and often helps small models. GPT-2 and many small models tie; many large ones (Llama) don't, since at their size the embeddings are a small fraction and separate matrices are slightly better.

The lab found a sharp edge. In an attention-only model, the direct path from input token to output logits is $U E^\top$; tied, it's $E E^\top$, symmetric and (up to the final normalization) positive semidefinite, so every token's own logit gets a boost from its own embedding. An MLP can learn to cancel it; an attention-only model can't. The lab's two-layer attention-only model learns to copy repeated sequences with 93.5% accuracy untied, and 25% tied. Tying is a modeling assumption ("the input and output meaning of a token are the same vector"), not a free compression.

## Counting parameters and compute

Per block: attention has $W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O$, four $d \times d$ matrices, so $4d^2$; the MLP has $d \times 4d$ and $4d \times d$, so $8d^2$. Total **$12d^2$ per block** (plus biases and norms, under 1%). A model with $L$ blocks has about $12Ld^2$ non-embedding parameters $N$. GPT-2 small, $d = 768$, $L = 12$: 85M in the blocks, 39M in the embeddings, 124M total. The lab counts them.

Compute, in the approximation everyone uses (Kaplan et al., 2020): a forward pass costs about **$2N$ FLOPs per token** (a multiply and an add per parameter), and training (forward plus backward, which costs about twice the forward) about **$6N$ per token**, plus the attention scores, $2 \cdot n \cdot d$ per layer per token for context length $n$, which matters once $n$ is comparable to $d$ or more. So a 7B-parameter model trained on 2 trillion tokens costs about $6 \times 7 \times 10^9 \times 2 \times 10^{12} \approx 8.4 \times 10^{22}$ FLOPs. That arithmetic is the basis of scaling laws and of every training budget (35.4).

## The training recipe

What GPT-style models converged on, and what the lab uses at small scale:

- **Initialization**: normal with standard deviation 0.02, and the output projections of each residual branch (attention's $W_O$ and the MLP's second matrix) scaled by $1/\sqrt{2L}$ (GPT-2), so that the sum of $2L$ branch outputs starts with the same scale as one.
- **AdamW** with $\beta_2 = 0.95$ (not the default 0.999, which reacts too slowly to the occasional huge gradient), weight decay around 0.1 on matrices but not on biases, norms or embeddings.
- **Warmup then cosine decay** of the learning rate (26.2), **gradient clipping** at norm 1.0 (28.1).
- **Mixed precision** in bf16 (25.3).
- **Dropout** 0.1 for small models or fine-tuning; usually 0 for large pretraining runs, where one pass over the data leaves nothing to overfit.

## Looking inside: induction heads

Give a trained language model a sequence with a repeat in it, "... Mr and Mrs Dursley ... Mr and Mrs", and it predicts "Dursley" with confidence, although it may have never seen the name. It's copying from context, and Olsson et al. (2022) found the circuit that does it: an **induction head**.

It takes two attention layers:

1. A **previous-token head** in the first layer: each position attends to the one before it and writes "the token before me was A" into its residual stream.
2. An **induction head** in a later layer: at the current position, whose token is A, it queries for positions whose *previous* token was A (the key reads what the previous-token head wrote), attends to that position, and copies *its* token, B, to the output. "Last time A appeared, B came next."

One layer can't do it: a single attention layer can find earlier occurrences of the current token, but those hold A, not B. The lab trains attention-only models on sequences made of a random segment repeated until the sequence is full: two layers reach 93.5% accuracy on the repeats in 500 steps, one layer gets 21% (it learns to hedge over the offsets it has seen).
Olsson et al. argue that induction heads are a large part of how in-context learning works, and that their sudden formation during training shows up as a visible bump in the loss curve. It's one of the clearest cases of a mechanism in a neural network that was found, explained and then confirmed by intervention.

## A benchmark I'm not proud of

The lab trains a 4-block transformer (222k parameters) and lab 28.1's RNN (122k) on the same characters for the same 30 seconds of CPU time. The RNN wins, clearly (on my machine, validation perplexity 5.3 vs 8.5; wall-clock results move with the hardware): it's cheaper per step and gets twice as many. That's a true result about 30 seconds on a CPU, and it says nothing about what wins with a GPU, long contexts and a real budget, which is transformers, by a margin that ended the argument around 2018.
I keep it in the lab because the opposite mistake is common: choosing an architecture from a benchmark whose conditions look nothing like yours.

## Things that will bite you

- **Post-norm without warmup** (or with a learning rate it can't take): a model stuck at the unigram loss.
- **Forgetting the final LayerNorm** in a pre-norm model.
- **Weight tying** assumed harmless.
- **Counting parameters without the embeddings** (or with them twice, when tied).
- **Weight decay on LayerNorm gains and biases.**
- **AdamW's default $\beta_2 = 0.999$** in large transformer training: slow to react to loss spikes.
- **Architecture decisions from toy benchmarks**, including the one in this lesson.

5. To predict B after the current A, the model must find a position whose *preceding* token was A, and read the token *at* that position. Attention compares the current query only with each position's own key, which is computed from what's in that position's residual stream. At initialization a position's stream contains only its own token (and position), not the previous token. So one layer must first move "previous token = A" into each position's stream (the previous-token head); a later layer can then match the current A against it. One layer can match "positions holding A", which returns A, not B.

In a model with MLPs and plenty of layers, the network can transform its representation so the assumption costs nothing, and the parameter savings and shared gradient signal help, especially for rare tokens. In an attention-only model the direct path contributes $EE^\top$ to the logits, which favors predicting the current token itself; there's no MLP to cancel it, and the copying circuit has to fight it.
