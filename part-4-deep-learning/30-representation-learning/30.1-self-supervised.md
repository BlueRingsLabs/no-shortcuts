# 30.1 Self-supervised and contrastive learning

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 27.3 (transfer, linear probes), 26.4 (BatchNorm), 17.4 (softmax cross-entropy), 21.3 (what a representation is). Lab: [`lab_30_1_self_supervised.py`](labs/lab_30_1_self_supervised.py).*

Labels are the expensive part of machine learning. Somebody has to draw the boxes, read the scans, listen to the calls, and they get tired,
disagree with each other and invoice by the hour. Meanwhile the unlabeled data is practically free: every camera, every log, every document
store has more of it than you'll ever use. **Self-supervised learning** invents a training task from the data itself, so that solving it forces
a network to learn features that turn out to be useful for the tasks you actually care about, which then need far fewer labels.

You've already met the most successful example without the name: a language model predicting the next token (29.2) is self-supervised, and
it's how every LLM is pretrained. This lesson is about the vision side, where the question "what task?" had a less obvious answer, and about
the failure that haunts all of it: collapse.

## Pretext tasks

The first generation invented puzzles: predict how much an image was rotated (Gidaris et al., 2018), solve a jigsaw of shuffled patches, colorize a grayscale image. Each needs *some* understanding of objects to solve. Each also admits shortcuts (a jigsaw can be solved from chromatic aberration at the patch edges, a real finding), and the features learned were tied to the puzzle. They worked; they were overtaken.

## Contrastive learning

The idea that took over: **two views of the same image should have similar representations, and views of different images should not.** A view is an augmented copy: cropped, flipped, color-jittered, blurred.

**SimCLR** (Chen et al., 2020) is the cleanest version:

1. Take a batch of $N$ images; make two random augmentations of each: $2N$ views.
2. Encode each with a network $f$ (a ResNet) and a small **projection head** $g$ (an MLP): $z = g(f(x))$.
3. For each view, its partner is the positive and the other $2N - 2$ views are negatives. The loss, **InfoNCE** (van den Oord et al., 2018), is cross-entropy for picking the partner out:

$$\ell_i = -\log \frac{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i, z_{j(i)})/\tau)}{\sum_{k \ne i} \exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i, z_k)/\tau)},$$

with cosine similarity and a temperature $\tau$ (around 0.1 to 0.5). It's a $(2N-1)$-way classification problem whose classes are the other images in the batch. At chance the loss is $\log(2N - 1)$; the lab's run goes from 6.17 (chance at $N = 256$ is 6.24) to 3.5. InfoNCE is also a lower bound on the mutual information between the views, which is where the theory lives.

Details that turned out to matter:

- **Augmentations define the task.** SimCLR's ablations found that the combination of random cropping and **color distortion** was essential. Without color distortion, two crops of the same image can be matched by their color histograms alone, and the network learns that instead of anything about objects. The lab reproduces it: SimCLR without color jitter learns *no* shape information (linear probe 0.26, chance 0.25), because color was enough to win the contrastive game.
- **The invariances you ask for are the information you lose.** The same lab shows the flip side: the model trained *with* color jitter can barely tell which color tint an image has (0.55, chance 0.33), while a random encoder gets 0.79. If color were your label, you'd have trained it away. Choose augmentations for the downstream tasks you expect.
- **The projection head is thrown away**: features *before* the head (the output of $f$) transfer better, because the head is where the network discards whatever the contrastive task didn't need.
- **Many negatives**: larger batches help (SimCLR used 4,096 on TPUs). **MoCo** (He et al., 2020) decoupled the number of negatives from the batch size with a **queue** of past embeddings and a slowly updated **momentum encoder** to keep them consistent.

## Collapse, and how non-contrastive methods avoid it

Why negatives at all? Because the objective "make the two views similar" has a trivial solution: map every image to the same vector. Perfect agreement, zero information. That's **collapse**, and every self-supervised method needs something to prevent it. Negatives do it by pushing different images apart. The surprise of 2020 was that you can do without them:

- **BYOL** (Grill et al., 2020): an online network with an extra **predictor** head learns to predict the output of a **target** network, which is an exponential moving average of the online one. No negatives, and it doesn't collapse, for reasons that took a while to understand.
- **SimSiam** (Chen and He, 2021) simplified it to the essential piece: two branches with shared weights, a predictor on one, and a **stop-gradient** on the other. Remove the stop-gradient and it collapses immediately. The lab does exactly that: with the stop-gradient, outputs stay spread out (per-dimension standard deviation 0.10, against 0.125 for perfectly spread unit vectors); without it, the loss reaches its minimum of $-1$ and every image maps to essentially the same point (0.007).
  An aside from building it: with a BatchNorm on the projector's output, the no-stop-gradient model collapsed only partially in my runs (std 0.074). BatchNorm normalizes across the batch, which smuggles in a weak form of comparison between images; that is one of the explanations that was proposed for why BYOL works at all.
- **Barlow Twins** (Zbontar et al., 2021) and **VICReg** (Bardes, Ponce and LeCun, 2022) prevent collapse explicitly: make the cross-correlation matrix of the two views' embeddings close to the identity (Barlow), or add terms that keep each dimension's variance up and decorrelate dimensions (VICReg). No negatives, no asymmetry tricks, just a loss that says what you want.
- **DINO** (Caron et al., 2021): self-distillation between a student and a momentum teacher, with centering and sharpening of the teacher's outputs to avoid collapse. On vision transformers (30.2) its attention maps segment objects without ever seeing a mask. **DINOv2** (Oquab et al., 2023) scaled it with curated data into the best general-purpose visual features available, and a good default backbone.

## Masked image modeling

The vision version of BERT (36.1): hide parts of the input and predict them. **MAE** (He et al., 2022) masks 75% of the patches of an image, runs the encoder only on the visible 25% (cheap), and a light decoder reconstructs the missing pixels. The high masking ratio is the point: with only a quarter of the image visible, interpolation from neighbors doesn't work, and the network has to model objects. MAE features are excellent after **fine-tuning** and mediocre under a **linear probe**, the opposite of contrastive features, which is a reminder that how you evaluate changes which method looks best.

## Evaluating representations

- **Linear probe**: freeze the encoder, train a linear classifier on its features (27.3). Measures how linearly accessible the information is. Favors contrastive methods.
- **k-NN**: classify by nearest neighbors in feature space (19.1). No training, similar conclusions.
- **Fine-tuning**: train everything on the downstream task. Favors MAE-style methods.
- **Low-shot**: 1% or 10% of the labels. This is where self-supervision pays off most, and it's the regime you're usually in.

The lab's numbers, on its tiny setup (3,000 unlabeled 32-pixel images, 20 epochs on a CPU): with 40 labels, a linear probe on SimCLR features gets 0.49, against 0.31 for supervised training from scratch on the same 40 images and 0.29 for a probe on a random encoder. With 400 labels, supervised training wins, 0.84 against 0.66.
That's an honest picture of small-scale self-supervision: a large win when labels are scarce, overtaken once they aren't, because 3,000 images and 20 epochs make a weak pretraining. At real scale (millions of images, hundreds of epochs), self-supervised features match or beat supervised ImageNet pretraining for transfer; DINOv2's linear probes compete with fine-tuned supervised models.

## In practice

- **Don't train your own** for general images: take a pretrained self-supervised backbone (DINOv2, or CLIP from 30.2) and probe or fine-tune it (27.3). Self-supervised pretraining is expensive: big batches, long schedules, careful augmentation.
- **Do consider it for unusual domains** where public backbones transfer poorly (medical scans, satellite and radar imagery, microscopy, industrial sensors) and you have lots of unlabeled data. Pretrain on your own data, starting from a public checkpoint if possible.
- **Choose augmentations deliberately**: they're the specification of what the features should ignore. Flips are wrong for text in images and for many medical images (left vs right); color jitter is wrong when color is diagnostic.
- **Watch for collapse**: track the standard deviation of the normalized embeddings (the lab's metric) or the rank of the embedding covariance during training. A loss that looks great while the embeddings shrink to a point is the signature.

## Things that will bite you

- **Collapse**, with a loss curve that looks like success.
- **Shortcuts** in the pretext task: color histograms, compression artifacts, patch edges, watermarks.
- **Augmentations that destroy the information your downstream task needs.**
- **Evaluating with the projection head's output** instead of the encoder's.
- **Comparing methods under one evaluation protocol** (linear probe vs fine-tuning) and generalizing.
- **Too small a batch** for SimCLR-style negatives, or too few epochs: self-supervision is slow.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $2N = 256$ views; each has 1 positive and $2N - 2 = 254$ negatives. At chance, all $2N - 1 = 255$ candidates are equally likely: loss $\log 255 \approx 5.54$.

2. The temperature sets how peaked the softmax over similarities is. As $\tau \to 0$, the loss concentrates on the hardest negative (the most similar wrong image), which can be a near-duplicate or a same-class image, making training unstable and pushing apart things that should be close. As $\tau$ grows, all negatives count about equally and the gradients get weak and uninformative. Values around 0.1 to 0.5 work; it's a hyperparameter worth tuning, and some methods learn it (CLIP, 30.2).

3. The contrastive task only asks the network to recognize which views came from the same image. If two crops of an image share color statistics that differ between images (they usually do: each photo has its own lighting and palette), the color histogram alone identifies the partner, and a network finds that much easier than learning shapes. It minimizes the loss without learning anything about objects. In the lab, SimCLR without color jitter reached a linear-probe shape accuracy of 0.26 (chance 0.25). Color distortion removes the shortcut, forcing the network to use content.

4. Collapse: the encoder maps all inputs to the same (or a very low-dimensional) output, which trivially satisfies "views agree". Prevention: negatives that push different images apart (SimCLR, MoCo); asymmetry between branches, with a predictor and a stop-gradient or a momentum target (SimSiam, BYOL, DINO with centering and sharpening); explicit variance and decorrelation terms (Barlow Twins, VICReg).

5. MAE is trained to reconstruct pixels, so its features keep a lot of low-level detail and the semantic information is present but not linearly separable; a linear probe can't reorganize it. Fine-tuning adapts all the layers and can reshape the representation, and the features are an excellent starting point for that. Contrastive objectives, by contrast, explicitly organize the space by image identity, which tends to be linearly aligned with classes.

6. Self-supervised pretraining on the 2 million X-rays, starting from a general backbone (DINOv2 or an ImageNet model) rather than from scratch, with a method that suits the data (MAE or DINO-style). Augmentations: crops, small rotations, brightness and contrast changes, noise. Avoid horizontal flips (the heart is on the left; laterality matters), heavy color jitter (irrelevant for grayscale, and intensity can be diagnostic), and crops so aggressive they cut out the finding. Then fine-tune on the 3,000 labeled images with a linear-probe baseline for comparison (27.3), evaluate per condition and per hospital or scanner, and check for shortcuts (scanner tags, text markers, and portable-vs-fixed scanner, which correlate with severity).
