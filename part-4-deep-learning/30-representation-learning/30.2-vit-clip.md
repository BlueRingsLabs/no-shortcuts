# 30.2 Vision transformers and CLIP

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 29.1 to 29.3, 30.1 (InfoNCE), 27.2 (CNN architectures), 27.3 (linear probes). Lab: [`lab_30_2_vit_clip.py`](labs/lab_30_2_vit_clip.py).*

Two ideas from 2021 that changed computer vision more than the preceding five years of architecture papers. The first: cut an image into
patches, call them tokens, and run the transformer from 29.2 on them, unchanged. The second: stop labeling images with a fixed list of
classes, and train on the 400 million image-caption pairs the internet already wrote, so that "class" becomes whatever you can describe in a
sentence. Together they're the visual half of every multimodal model you've used, the text conditioning of image generators, and the reason
"zero-shot" stopped being a research curiosity.

The lab builds both at toy scale, and the toy scale shows the first idea's cost very clearly.

## The vision transformer

ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021):

1. Split an $H \times W$ image into non-overlapping $P \times P$ patches: $N = HW/P^2$ of them (a 224-pixel image with 16-pixel patches gives 196).
2. Flatten each patch and project it linearly to $d$ dimensions. (This is exactly a convolution with kernel size and stride $P$; the lab checks it.)
3. Prepend a learned **[CLS] token**, add learned **position embeddings**, and run a standard transformer encoder: bidirectional attention, no mask.
4. Classify from the final [CLS] vector (or the mean of all tokens).

That's the whole architecture: no convolutions after the first layer, no pooling, no pyramid. Its strength and weakness are the same fact: it has **almost no inductive bias** for images. A CNN assumes locality (a pixel's neighbors matter most) and translation equivariance (27.1); a ViT has to learn from data that neighboring patches are related, and that a circle at the top left is the same as one at the bottom right. Its attention can relate any two patches from the first layer, so there's nothing it *can't* learn, given enough data.

**How much data?** The original ViT underperformed ResNets when trained on ImageNet's 1.3 million images and beat them when pretrained on 300 million (Google's internal JFT-300M). The lab shows the same effect at toy scale, with both models given the same augmentation and the same 600 optimizer steps:

| | 500 training images | 4,000 training images |
|---|---|---|
| CNN (94k parameters) | 0.95 | 0.99 |
| ViT (208k parameters) | 0.53 | 0.76 |

With enough data, the gap closes and then reverses; below that, inductive bias is worth a lot of data. **DeiT** (Touvron et al., 2021) made ViTs trainable on ImageNet alone with heavy augmentation, regularization and distillation from a CNN teacher, and self-supervised pretraining (DINO, MAE, 30.1) removed much of the data hunger.

Variants worth knowing: **Swin** (Liu et al., 2021) brings back a hierarchy and local windows, which suits detection and segmentation (27.4, 27.5); **ConvNeXt** (27.2) was the CNN side's reply, showing that a modernized CNN matches ViTs at the same scale. At large scale, plain ViTs dominate, largely because they plug straight into the transformer tooling and scaling knowledge of 29 and 35.

Practical details:

- **Patch size** sets the sequence length: halving $P$ quadruples the tokens and multiplies attention's cost by about 16 (29.3). 14 and 16 are common; small patches are expensive and help dense tasks.
- **Changing resolution** changes the number of patches, so the learned position embeddings must be **interpolated** (2D, bicubic) to the new grid, followed by fine-tuning, a small version of 29.3's length extension.
- **Registers**: large ViTs develop a few high-norm "artifact" tokens in background patches that they use as scratch space; adding a few extra learned tokens ("registers", Darcet et al., 2024) gives them somewhere else to put it and cleans up the attention maps.

## CLIP

CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) trained two encoders, one for images (a ViT or a ResNet) and one for text (a transformer), on **400 million image-text pairs** from the web, with the contrastive objective of 30.1 applied across modalities:

- A batch of $N$ pairs gives an $N \times N$ matrix of cosine similarities between every image embedding and every text embedding, scaled by a learned temperature.
- The loss is cross-entropy along the rows (each image should pick its own caption) and along the columns (each caption should pick its image), averaged: a **symmetric InfoNCE**. CLIP used $N = 32{,}768$.

The result is a **shared embedding space** where an image and a sentence describing it land close together. Which gives you, for free:

- **Zero-shot classification**: embed a caption for each class ("a photo of a dog", "a photo of a cat"), embed the image, pick the closest caption. No training on the target classes. CLIP matched the original ResNet-50's ImageNet accuracy without using any of ImageNet's 1.28 million labeled images for training.
- **Prompt engineering and ensembling**: the class name alone is a worse prompt than "a photo of a {class}", because captions on the web look like the latter; averaging the embeddings of many templates helps further. The CLIP authors report almost 5 points of ImageNet accuracy from the two together.
- **Retrieval**: text-to-image and image-to-text search by nearest neighbors in the shared space (41.1, 41.2).
- **Robustness**: CLIP's zero-shot accuracy dropped much less than ImageNet-trained models' on distribution-shifted test sets (sketches, renditions, adversarially filtered images). Training on diverse data, not a clever architecture, was the reason.

The lab trains a tiny CLIP on synthetic captioned shapes ("a red circle", "the plus is magenta", six templates, six colors, four shapes), with **four color-shape combinations removed from training entirely**. Zero-shot accuracy over all 24 combinations is 1.00 on combinations it saw and 0.98 on the ones it never saw, as images or captions: the text encoder composes "red" and "circle" even though "red circle" never appeared.
Retrieval for "a blue square", also never seen, returns five blue squares. The toy is too easy to show prompt ensembling doing anything (one template already scores 0.98 to 1.00), and I'd rather tell you that than invent a gap.

One practical wrinkle from building it: CLIP's loss treats every other caption in the batch as a negative, which is fine when captions are unique web text. The lab's captions repeat constantly (24 combinations), so a naive implementation would push "a red circle" away from other red circles. The lab's loss treats all pairs with the same content as positives. Real datasets have milder versions of the same problem (near-duplicate images, generic captions like "image"), and it's one reason data deduplication matters (35.3).

**Descendants**: ALIGN (1.8 billion noisy pairs), OpenCLIP (open reproductions on LAION data), and **SigLIP** (Zhai et al., 2023), which replaces the batch-wide softmax with an independent sigmoid per pair, removing the need for huge batches and all-to-all communication. SigLIP encoders are common in current open vision-language models (39.2).

## Where CLIP-style models are used

- The image encoder of most **vision-language models** (39.2): a ViT trained CLIP- or SigLIP-style, whose patch embeddings are projected into an LLM's input space.
- The text encoder that conditions **text-to-image diffusion** (31.4).
- **Open-vocabulary detection and segmentation** (27.4, 27.5): detect "the thing named X" by comparing region features with text embeddings.
- **Semantic search** over image collections, **deduplication**, **content filtering**, and data curation for training other models.

## Limitations to know before you rely on it

- **Binding and composition**: with several objects, CLIP-style models often behave like a bag of words: "a red cube and a blue sphere" and "a blue cube and a red sphere" embed nearly the same (Yuksekgonul et al., 2023). The lab's success at composition is on images with exactly one object, which is the easy case; don't generalize from it.
- **Counting, spatial relations and negation** ("a table with no chairs") are weak.
- **Typographic attacks**: a model that learned to read text in images can be fooled by it. An apple with a paper label saying "iPod" gets classified as an iPod (Goh et al., 2021). For anyone who has done security work, this is an attack surface, not a curiosity: any system that makes decisions from images can be steered by text an attacker places in the scene. In VLMs the same mechanism carries **prompt injection through images** (44.1).
- **Biases** from web data: associations between demographics and categories that you'd never accept in a labeled dataset are present, and zero-shot classifiers built on them inherit them (54.1). LAION audits found much worse, which is why curated datasets and data filtering matter.
- **Prompt sensitivity**: small wording changes move accuracy. Evaluate on your own data, with your own prompts, and calibrate thresholds for retrieval.

> **Scar tissue.** The friend who sells used hardware wanted his listing photos sorted by category automatically, and a zero-shot CLIP
> classifier went from 61% to 78% accuracy when I changed the prompt from the bare category name to "a product photo of a {category}".
> Then it dropped five points on one category when he renamed "graphics card" to "GPU" on his site. Nothing about the model had changed;
> the text encoder simply associated the two wordings with different images. Zero-shot means the prompt is a hyperparameter, and it
> deserves a validation set like any other.

## Things that will bite you

- **Training a ViT from scratch on a small dataset**: use a pretrained one, or a CNN.
- **Changing input resolution** without interpolating the position embeddings.
- **Unnormalized embeddings** in similarity search (CLIP embeddings are compared by cosine similarity; normalize).
- **The raw class name as the prompt**.
- **False negatives** in a contrastive batch with duplicate captions.
- **Trusting zero-shot accuracy on benchmarks** as a prediction for your domain.
- **Text in the image** steering the prediction.

6. Why can SigLIP train well with smaller batches than CLIP?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $(224/16)^2 = 196$ patches + 1 = 197 tokens. At 448: $28^2 = 784 + 1 = 785$. Attention's cost scales with the square of the sequence length: about $(785/197)^2 \approx 16$ times more for the attention scores (the MLPs and projections scale linearly, about 4 times).

2. CNNs build in locality and translation equivariance, so they don't have to learn them; a ViT's attention treats patches as an unordered set with learned positions and must discover spatial structure from data, which takes many examples. Large-scale pretraining (JFT-300M), strong augmentation, regularization and distillation (DeiT), and self-supervised pretraining (DINO, MAE) made ViTs practical on smaller labeled datasets; in practice, you start from a pretrained ViT.

3. `logits = I @ T.T / tau; labels = torch.arange(len(I))` and `loss = (F.cross_entropy(logits, labels) + F.cross_entropy(logits.T, labels)) / 2`.

4. Write a caption per class ("a photo of a {class}"), embed all ten with the text encoder, embed each image with the image encoder, normalize both, and pick the class with the highest cosine similarity. Improve without training: better templates matched to the domain ("a product photo of a {class}", "a satellite image of {class}"), ensembles of templates averaged per class, more descriptive class names (disambiguate "crane" the bird from "crane" the machine), and calibration of a rejection threshold on a small validation set.

5. Examples: text on packaging steering the tag (typographic effect: the brand name on the box vs the product); multiple products in one photo (binding: colors and attributes assigned to the wrong item); fine-grained categories CLIP can't distinguish (two models of the same shoe); attribute errors (color names under different lighting); bias in tags involving people in lifestyle photos; and distribution shift (studio photos vs user-uploaded ones). Test each with a labeled sample, and measure per category.

6. CLIP's softmax normalizes each image's similarities over all the captions in the batch (and vice versa), so the loss and its gradients depend on the whole batch, and it works best with very large batches to provide many negatives, which also requires gathering all embeddings across devices. SigLIP treats each image-text pair as an independent binary classification (match or not) with a sigmoid, so each pair's loss doesn't depend on normalizing over the batch; it works well at smaller batch sizes and is cheaper to distribute.
