# 26.2 Initialization, learning rate schedules and training dynamics

*About 9 hours. Prerequisites: 24.1 (vanishing gradients), 24.2, 26.1, 04.3 (variance of sums). Lab: [`lab_26_2_init_schedules.py`](labs/lab_26_2_init_schedules.py).*

Two numbers decide whether a network trains at all: the scale of its initial weights and the size of its steps. Get the first wrong and the signal
vanishes or explodes before training begins. Get the second wrong and training either crawls or blows up, sometimes at step 30,000 when you're asleep.
This lesson derives the initialization rules instead of stating them, treats the learning rate as the most important hyperparameter (because it is),
covers the schedules people actually use, and teaches you to read training curves, which is the skill that separates people who can train networks
from people who can run training scripts.

## Initialization

**Not zero, not equal.** If all weights in a layer start equal, every unit computes the same thing and receives the same gradient, forever: the layer is effectively one unit wide. Random initialization breaks the symmetry.
The lab shows zero-initialized hidden units staying identical through training.

**Not too small, not too big.** Consider a layer $z = Wx$ with $n_{\text{in}}$ inputs, weights with variance $\sigma_w^2$, and inputs with variance $\sigma_x^2$, all independent and zero-mean. Then (04.3)

$$\operatorname{Var}(z_j) = n_{\text{in}}\,\sigma_w^2\,\sigma_x^2$$

After $L$ layers the variance is multiplied by $(n_{\text{in}}\sigma_w^2)^L$ (times the activation's effect). If that factor is 0.8, the signal shrinks by $0.8^{50} \approx 10^{-5}$ over 50 layers; if it's 1.2, it grows by $10^4$. The same happens to
gradients on the way back, with $n_{\text{out}}$ in place of $n_{\text{in}}$. The fix is to choose $\sigma_w^2$ so the factor is 1:

- **Xavier / Glorot** (Glorot and Bengio, 2010), for tanh and linear layers (roughly linear near 0): $\sigma_w^2 = 1/n_{\text{in}}$ keeps the forward variance; $1/n_{\text{out}}$ keeps the backward one; the compromise is $\sigma_w^2 = 2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})$.
- **He / Kaiming** (He et al., 2015), for ReLU: ReLU zeroes half of its inputs, halving the second moment, so compensate with $\sigma_w^2 = 2/n_{\text{in}}$.

The lab pushes a signal through 50 layers with each choice and plots its standard deviation: vanishing, exploding, or steady.

Other tools: **orthogonal initialization** (preserves norms exactly for linear layers; popular for RNNs, 28.1); **zero-initializing the last layer of each residual branch** so a deep residual network starts as the identity (26.4), and scaling residual
branches by $1/\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ (used in GPT-2 and later, 35.5). PyTorch's default for `nn.Linear` (a uniform distribution giving variance $1/(3n_{\text{in}})$) is on the small side for deep ReLU networks without normalization; with
normalization layers (26.4) the initialization matters much less, which is a large part of why those layers exist.

## The learning rate

If you tune one hyperparameter, tune this one. Too small and training is slow and may stall in a poor region; too large and the loss oscillates, spikes or diverges to NaN. The best learning rate for a fixed training budget is usually
**close to the largest one that's still stable**, which makes the edge of instability a place you work near on purpose.

The **learning rate range test** (Smith, 2017) finds it cheaply: train for a few hundred steps while increasing the learning rate exponentially from tiny to huge, record the loss, and pick a value somewhat below where the loss
starts climbing (often by a factor of 3 to 10). The lab runs it.

## Schedules

A good schedule uses a large learning rate early (to make progress and explore) and a small one late (to settle into a minimum without bouncing around it). The standard shapes:

- **Step decay**: divide by 10 at fixed epochs (the classic ResNet recipe).
- **Cosine annealing**: $\eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})(1 + \cos(\pi t/T))$. Smooth, one less hyperparameter than step decay, the default in many modern recipes.
- **Linear warmup**: start near zero and increase linearly over the first few hundred or thousand steps. Why: early in training, gradients are large and poorly aligned, and Adam's second-moment estimates are based on very few
  steps (26.1), so a full-size step can throw the network into a bad region it never recovers from. Warmup is standard for transformers and for large-batch training. **Warmup + cosine** is the default recipe for most
  deep learning today.
- **One-cycle** (Smith and Topin, 2019): warm up to a high peak, then anneal to very low, often with momentum cycled inversely. Trains fast with a fixed budget.
- **Warmup-stable-decay** (WSD): warm up, hold constant for most of training, then decay quickly at the end. Used in recent LLM training because you can branch off a decayed checkpoint at any point without deciding the total
  length in advance (35.4).

In PyTorch, `torch.optim.lr_scheduler` has all of them; call `scheduler.step()` at the granularity the schedule was defined for (per step for warmup and cosine over steps, per epoch otherwise), and save its state in checkpoints (25.2).

## Batch size and learning rate

The mini-batch gradient is a noisy estimate of the full gradient, with variance roughly proportional to $1/B$. Doubling the batch halves the noise, so you can take larger steps: the **linear scaling rule** (Goyal et al., 2017) scales the
learning rate with the batch size (with warmup), and trained ImageNet in an hour with it. It holds up to a **critical batch size** (McCandlish et al., 2018) beyond which more batch buys almost no speedup per example; for Adam, the
scaling is closer to the square root. The lab trains with different batch sizes and shows why the learning rate must move with it.

## Reading training curves

Log training loss per step (smoothed), validation loss and metrics per epoch, the learning rate, and the **gradient norm**. Then:

- **Loss flat from the start**: learning rate far too low, a bug (frozen parameters, 25.2's list; wrong labels; a detached graph), or dead units from a bad init.
- **Loss decreasing then exploding to NaN**: learning rate too high, missing warmup, a numerical issue (log of 0, float16 overflow, 25.3). Gradient clipping helps with rare spikes; it doesn't fix a learning rate that's too high.
- **Occasional loss spikes that recover**: common in large-scale training (often near the edge of stability); watch that they recover and don't become more frequent.
- **Training loss falling, validation loss rising**: overfitting (26.3). **Both high and flat**: underfitting or a bug; try overfitting a single batch (26.5).
- **Noisy, then drops sharply when the learning rate decays**: normal; the decay removes the noise floor.

Two diagnostics worth logging:

- **The update-to-weight ratio** $\|\Delta\theta\|/\|\theta\|$ per layer: around $10^{-3}$ per step is a common healthy range. Much bigger: unstable. Much smaller: that layer barely learns.
- **Per-layer gradient norms**: vanishing gradients show up as norms falling by orders of magnitude toward the input layers.

A more recent insight on why the learning rate works the way it does: with gradient descent, the **sharpness** of the loss (its largest Hessian eigenvalue) tends to rise until it reaches $2/\eta$ and then hovers there, the "edge of stability"
(Cohen et al., 2021). The optimizer effectively picks regions whose curvature matches its step size, which is part of why large learning rates find flatter regions.

## Things that will bite you

- **Initializing everything to zero** (or to the same value).
- **Default initialization** on a deep network without normalization or residuals.
- **One learning rate** for every batch size.
- **No warmup** for Adam on transformers or large batches.
- **A schedule stepped at the wrong granularity** (a per-step cosine stepped per epoch never decays).
- **Tuning everything except the learning rate.**
- **Looking only at the final number** instead of the curves.

2. A 30-layer tanh network is initialized with $\sigma_w^2 = 0.5/n_{\text{in}}$. What happens to activations and gradients, and what would you change?
3. Why does Adam in particular benefit from warmup?
4. You double the batch size and keep the learning rate. What happens to the number of steps per epoch, the gradient noise, and the progress per epoch? What would you change?
