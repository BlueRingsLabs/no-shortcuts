# 26.3 Regularization: weight decay, dropout, augmentation

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 16.2 (bias-variance, double descent), 17.2 (L2), 18.3 (calibration), 26.1 (AdamW), 26.2. Lab: [`lab_26_3_regularization.py`](labs/lab_26_3_regularization.py).*

A modern network has far more parameters than training examples and can memorize its training set outright: Zhang et al. (2017) trained standard
image networks to 100% training accuracy on ImageNet with *random labels*. And yet the same networks generalize well on real labels. Part of the
explanation is implicit (the optimizer prefers some solutions over others, 16.3), and part is the explicit regularization in this lesson: techniques that
make memorization harder or less attractive than learning the pattern.

The ranking I'd give from experience, for most problems: **more data** beats everything; **data augmentation** (a way of manufacturing more data) is next;
then **weight decay** and **early stopping** (cheap, almost always on); then **dropout** and **label smoothing** (useful in specific places). The lab
starts from a network that memorizes 100 training images and adds each one, and it also tests on shifted
images, which is where the ranking above shows up.

## Weight decay

Covered in 26.1: shrink weights toward zero every step, decoupled from Adam's scaling (AdamW), not on biases or normalization parameters. Typical values: $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$ depending on the optimizer (AdamW uses larger values,
like 0.01 to 0.1, because its effective shrinkage is $\eta\lambda$ per step).

A subtlety worth knowing: in layers followed by batch or layer normalization (26.4), the output doesn't depend on the scale of the weights, so weight decay can't be regularizing the *function* in the usual sense. What it does
instead is keep the weight norm small, which increases the **effective learning rate** of the scale-invariant weights. It still helps, for a different reason than in 17.2 (van Laarhoven, 2017).

## Dropout

During training, set each unit's output to zero with probability $p$, independently per example and per step, and scale the survivors by $1/(1 - p)$ (**inverted dropout**) so the expected activation is unchanged. At evaluation, use all units
with no scaling (25.2's `eval()` switch). (Srivastava et al., 2014.)

Why it works, in three views: the network can't rely on any single unit, so it learns redundant, more robust features (no "co-adaptation"); each step trains a different thinned sub-network, so the final network approximates an
**ensemble** of exponentially many networks sharing weights (20.2's variance reduction); and it injects multiplicative noise, which is a regularizer in its own right.

Practice: rates of 0.1 to 0.5, typically on large fully connected layers and in transformer blocks (on attention weights and residual branches, 0.1 is common, 29.2), less often in convolutional layers (where **stochastic depth**, dropping
whole residual blocks, works better). Large language models trained on huge datasets often use **no dropout at all**: with one pass over trillions of tokens, overfitting isn't the problem. Keeping dropout *on* at test time and averaging
several passes (**MC dropout**) gives a cheap uncertainty estimate (23.4), of debated quality.

## Data augmentation

Generate new training examples by transforming existing ones in ways that **don't change the label**: the model learns the invariance instead of memorizing the particular example.

- **Images**: random crops and flips, small rotations and scalings, color jitter, blur; stronger policies (RandAugment); **mixup** (train on convex combinations of two images and their labels) and **CutMix** (paste a patch of one image into another,
  mix the labels by area). 27.3 uses them.
- **Text**: back-translation, synonym replacement, token dropout; for LLMs, mostly more and better data (35.3).
- **Audio**: time shifts, noise, SpecAugment (masking time and frequency bands).
- **Tabular**: rarely useful; noise injection at most. Tabular problems get their robustness from features and regularized trees (20.4).

The rule that matters: **the transformation must preserve the label**. Flipping a photo of a cat is fine; flipping a "6" or a "b", rotating a "9", or cropping out the tumor are not. Augment the training set only (validation measures performance on
real data), inside the data loader so each epoch sees different variants (25.2's worker seeding applies). **Test-time augmentation** (average predictions over several augmented copies) often adds a little accuracy for free.

## Label smoothing

Replace the one-hot target with $(1 - \varepsilon)$ on the true class and $\varepsilon/K$ spread over all $K$ classes (Szegedy et al., 2016). The cross-entropy can then never be driven to zero by making the correct logit infinitely large, so logits stay
bounded and the model is less overconfident. $\varepsilon = 0.1$ is typical. Effects: often a small accuracy gain, tighter clusters in the penultimate layer, and less confident probabilities. Whether that improves
calibration (18.3) depends on where you started: a large network that was overconfident gets better calibrated; a model that was already calibrated becomes *underconfident*, which is what
happens in the lab (its expected calibration error triples). Check a reliability diagram either way. A known
cost: it erases information in the logits about which wrong classes are plausible, which hurts knowledge distillation from the smoothed model (Müller, Kornblith and Hinton, 2019). In PyTorch: `cross_entropy(..., label_smoothing=0.1)`.

## Early stopping

Monitor the validation loss (or metric) every epoch, keep the best checkpoint, and stop after it hasn't improved for a **patience** window. The cheapest regularizer there is, and a real one: for linear models trained by gradient
descent from zero, stopping early is approximately equivalent to L2 regularization, with the number of steps playing the role of $1/\lambda$ (Bishop, 1995). Two cautions: the validation set used to choose the stopping point is now part
of model selection (18.1), and with modern schedules (26.2) the validation loss can get worse and then better again (**epoch-wise double descent**, Nakkiran et al., 2020), so a short patience can stop too early.

## Things that will bite you

- **Augmentations that change the label.**
- **Augmenting the validation or test set** (other than deliberate test-time augmentation).
- **Dropout left on** at evaluation, or **`train()` forgotten** after evaluating (25.2).
- **Weight decay on norm and bias parameters.**
- **Stacking every regularizer** at once on a model that was underfitting.
- **Patience too short** for a noisy validation curve.
- **Label smoothing on a teacher** you plan to distill from.

## Exercises

1. Show that inverted dropout keeps the expected value of each activation unchanged. What's the variance of a dropped-out activation $h\,m/(1 - p)$ with $m \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1 - p)$?
2. For each task, name an augmentation that's safe and one that isn't: handwritten digit recognition, chest X-ray diagnosis of a left-sided condition, sentiment analysis of reviews.
3. With label smoothing $\varepsilon$ and $K$ classes, what's the minimum achievable cross-entropy, and what logit gap achieves it?
4. Your training loss is still falling and validation loss has been flat for 5 epochs with a cosine schedule that has 30 epochs left. Stop or continue?
5. Why do large language models often train without dropout while small vision models on small datasets need a lot of regularization?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\mathbb{E}[h\,m/(1 - p)] = h(1 - p)/(1 - p) = h$. Variance: $h^2\operatorname{Var}(m)/(1 - p)^2 = h^2p(1 - p)/(1 - p)^2 = h^2\frac{p}{1 - p}$. With $p = 0.5$, the variance equals $h^2$: a lot of noise, which is the point.

2. Digits: small shifts, small rotations and elastic distortions are safe; horizontal flips and large rotations aren't (they change 2/5, 6/9, 7/1-like shapes). Chest X-rays for a left-sided condition: small rotations, contrast changes and crops that
   keep the lungs are safe; horizontal flips aren't (they move the condition to the right side; the label changes). Reviews: synonym replacement and back-translation are mostly safe; dropping or replacing negations ("not good" to "good")
   isn't.

3. The target puts $1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K$ on the true class and $\varepsilon/K$ on each other. The cross-entropy is minimized when the predicted distribution equals the target, giving its entropy $H = -(1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K)\log(1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K) - (K - 1)\frac{\varepsilon}{K}\log\frac{\varepsilon}{K} > 0$.
   The softmax matches it when the true logit exceeds the others by $\log\frac{1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K}{\varepsilon/K}$, a finite gap ($\ln(0.91/0.01) = \ln 91 \approx 4.5$ for $\varepsilon = 0.1$, $K = 10$): logits stay bounded.

4. Continue (keeping the best checkpoint). With a schedule that still has most of its decay ahead, validation loss often improves when the learning rate drops (26.2), and it can even worsen before improving (double descent). Use a patience longer
   than the schedule's noisy phase, or evaluate the decision once the learning rate is small.

5. LLMs see each training token roughly once, on datasets far larger than they can memorize, so the gap between training and validation loss stays near zero and there's nothing for dropout to fix; dropout would only slow learning. Small
   vision datasets are seen for many epochs by models that can memorize them, so without augmentation, weight decay and dropout the model overfits.
