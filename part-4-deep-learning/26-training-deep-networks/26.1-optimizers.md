# 26.1 Optimizers: SGD, momentum, Adam, AdamW

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 03.3 (gradient descent, conditioning, SGD), 17.2 (L2 regularization), 25.2. Lab: [`lab_26_1_optimizers.py`](labs/lab_26_1_optimizers.py).*

03.3 gave you gradient descent and its enemy, the condition number: when some directions of the loss are steep and others shallow, a single step size
is too large for the steep ones and too small for the shallow ones, and progress crawls. Deep networks are that problem at scale, with noise on top
(mini-batch gradients, 03.3's SGD) and a loss surface nobody can see. Every optimizer in this lesson is an answer to "how do I make progress on a
badly conditioned, noisy objective using only first derivatives?".

You'll implement each one in a few lines, check them against PyTorch's, watch them on landscapes where you can see the trajectory, and then look closely
at the detail this module's self-check asks about: why "Adam with L2 regularization" and "AdamW" are different algorithms, and why the difference
matters enough that it took a paper to point it out.

## SGD and momentum

**SGD**: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,g$, with $g$ the mini-batch gradient. Simple, memory-free, and for many vision tasks, with momentum and a good schedule, still the best-generalizing choice.

**Momentum** (Polyak, 1964) keeps a velocity, an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients:

$$v \leftarrow \mu v + g, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta v$$

with $\mu \approx 0.9$. In a consistent direction the steps add up (effective step size $\eta/(1 - \mu)$, ten times larger); in directions where the gradient oscillates (the steep walls of a narrow valley), successive gradients cancel.
It damps oscillation and accelerates along the valley floor, exactly the fix for ill-conditioning, and it averages out some mini-batch noise. For convex quadratics it improves the convergence rate from depending on $\kappa$ to depending
on $\sqrt\kappa$ (03.3).

**Nesterov momentum** evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point $\theta - \eta\mu v$ instead of at $\theta$, which corrects overshoot earlier. In PyTorch: `SGD(..., momentum=0.9, nesterov=True)`, implemented in an equivalent form
that doesn't need the extra evaluation.

## Adaptive methods: a step size per parameter

Different parameters see gradients of very different scales (embeddings of rare words vs frequent ones, the first layer vs the last). A per-parameter step size, set from the gradient's own history, helps.

- **AdaGrad** (Duchi et al., 2011): divide by the square root of the **sum** of all past squared gradients. Great for sparse features; the sum only grows, so the step size shrinks toward zero and training stalls.
- **RMSProp** (Hinton, 2012, in a lecture slide, never a paper): use an exponential **moving average** of squared gradients instead: $s \leftarrow \beta s + (1 - \beta)g^2$, $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,g/(\sqrt{s} + \epsilon)$.
- **Adam** (Kingma and Ba, 2015): momentum on the gradient *and* RMSProp's scaling, with **bias correction**:

$$m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1 - \beta_1)g, \quad s \leftarrow \beta_2 s + (1 - \beta_2)g^2, \quad \hat m = \frac{m}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \quad \hat s = \frac{s}{1 - \beta_2^t}, \quad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s} + \epsilon}$$

  Defaults $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. The moving averages start at zero, so early on they're biased toward zero; dividing by $1 - \beta^t$ removes that bias (without it, the first steps would be tiny for $m$
  and, because $s$ is even more biased with $\beta_2 = 0.999$, *huge* in the ratio). The update $\hat m/\sqrt{\hat s}$ is roughly a **signal-to-noise ratio** per parameter, bounded in magnitude by about 1, so $\eta$ is close to the
  maximum step per parameter, in parameter units. That's why Adam's learning rate transfers across problems better than SGD's, and why "3e-4" became folklore.

Costs and caveats: Adam stores two extra tensors per parameter (25.3's 16 bytes per parameter), and its generalization on some tasks (image classification with CNNs) is often slightly worse than well-tuned SGD with momentum. For
transformers, Adam-type methods are close to mandatory: SGD trains them badly, partly because of the very uneven gradient scales across their parameters.

## L2 regularization vs decoupled weight decay (the self-check)

With plain SGD, adding $\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2$ to the loss (L2 regularization, 17.2) and shrinking the weights directly each step (**weight decay**, $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\lambda\theta$) are **the same thing**: the gradient of the penalty is
$\lambda\theta$, and SGD subtracts $\eta\lambda\theta$.

With Adam they're **not** the same (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019). If you add the penalty to the loss, its gradient $\lambda\theta$ goes into $g$, and then gets **divided by $\sqrt{\hat s}$** along with everything else. Parameters with large or noisy
gradients (large $\hat s$) get their decay divided by a large number: they're barely regularized. Parameters with small gradients get strongly regularized. The regularization strength ends up coupled to the gradient
statistics, which is not what anyone intended.

**AdamW** decouples it: do the Adam update on the loss gradient alone, and apply weight decay separately, $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\lambda\theta$. Every parameter shrinks at the same relative rate, independent of its gradient history. It
generalizes better, its $\lambda$ is easier to tune, and it's the default for training transformers. In PyTorch, `Adam(weight_decay=...)` is the coupled L2 version; `AdamW(weight_decay=...)` is decoupled. The lab shows them decaying
the same weights by very different amounts.

Practical details: don't apply weight decay to **biases and normalization parameters** (it fights their job and rarely helps); in PyTorch, pass two parameter groups. And weight decay interacts with the learning rate
schedule: the effective shrinkage per step is $\eta\lambda$, so it fades as the learning rate decays (26.2).

## Newer optimizers, briefly

- **Lion** (Chen et al., 2023): the sign of a momentum-like average. Less memory (one state tensor), competitive on some large models.
- **Shampoo**, **SOAP**, **Muon**: approximate second-order methods using per-layer preconditioners (Shampoo) or orthogonalized updates (Muon), showing real speedups in recent large-scale training. An active area; the claims move
  fast, and many "Adam killers" haven't survived careful tuning of the Adam baseline.
- **8-bit optimizers** store Adam's states in 8 bits to save memory (25.3's arithmetic), with little quality loss.

My practical advice: AdamW for transformers and most new architectures; SGD with momentum and a schedule for CNNs on vision if you have time to tune; spend the tuning budget on the **learning rate** and its **schedule**
(26.2) before trying exotic optimizers.

## Things that will bite you

- **`Adam(weight_decay=...)`** when you meant AdamW.
- **Weight decay on biases and norm layers.**
- **Adam's $\epsilon$** at $10^{-8}$ in float16 or with tiny gradients: the denominator can underflow or dominate; some setups need $10^{-6}$ or larger.
- **Comparing optimizers** with one learning rate each. Tune each optimizer's learning rate before declaring a winner.
- **Resuming training** without the optimizer state (25.2): Adam restarts with zero moments and a huge bias-corrected first step.
- **Momentum with a learning rate tuned for plain SGD**: the effective step is $1/(1 - \mu)$ times larger.

## Exercises

1. Show that with a constant gradient $g$, momentum's velocity converges to $g/(1 - \mu)$. What's the effective step size for $\mu = 0.9$ and $\mu = 0.99$?
2. Why does Adam need bias correction? Compute $m_1$ and $\hat m_1$ after one step with $g_1 = 1$ and $\beta_1 = 0.9$.
3. Show that SGD with L2 regularization and SGD with weight decay give identical updates, and that Adam with L2 and AdamW don't.
4. What's the approximate magnitude of an Adam update when the gradient is consistently $g$ (the same every step)? When it's pure noise with zero mean?
5. Why should the weight decay not be applied to LayerNorm's gain parameters?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $v_t = \sum_{k<t}\mu^kg \to g/(1 - \mu)$. Effective step $\eta/(1 - \mu)$: $10\eta$ for $\mu = 0.9$, $100\eta$ for $\mu = 0.99$.

2. $m_0 = 0$, so $m_1 = (1 - \beta_1)g_1 = 0.1$: a tenth of the gradient, biased toward the zero initialization. $\hat m_1 = m_1/(1 - \beta_1^1) = 0.1/0.1 = 1 = g_1$. Similarly $s$ with $\beta_2 = 0.999$ starts at a thousandth of $g^2$; without correcting it,
   $m/\sqrt{s}$ would be $0.1/\sqrt{0.001} \approx 3.2$ times too large in the first step, and the correction factors differ between $m$ and $s$ for many steps.

3. SGD + L2: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta(g + \lambda\theta)$. SGD + decay: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta g - \eta\lambda\theta$. Identical. Adam + L2: the $\lambda\theta$ term enters $m$ and $s$, and the update is $\eta\,\widehat{m}(g + \lambda\theta)/\sqrt{\widehat{s}(g + \lambda\theta)}$, so the decay is
   rescaled per parameter by the adaptive denominator. AdamW: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\hat m(g)/\sqrt{\hat s(g)} - \eta\lambda\theta$: the decay isn't rescaled.

4. Consistent $g$: $\hat m = g$, $\hat s = g^2$, so the update is about $\eta\,\text{sign}(g)$: magnitude $\eta$, whatever the scale of $g$. Pure zero-mean noise with standard deviation $\sigma$: $\hat m$ averages toward 0 (magnitude about
   $\sigma\sqrt{(1 - \beta_1)/(1 + \beta_1)} \approx 0.23\sigma$) while $\sqrt{\hat s} \approx \sigma$, so updates are about $0.23\eta$ in random directions: Adam takes smaller steps where the gradient is mostly noise.

5. The gain's job is to set the scale of normalized activations; its natural value is around 1, not 0. Decaying it toward 0 pushes activations toward zero and fights the normalization, for no regularization benefit (the network's
   function is not "simpler" with smaller gains in any useful sense). Biases likewise have no reason to be pulled to 0.
