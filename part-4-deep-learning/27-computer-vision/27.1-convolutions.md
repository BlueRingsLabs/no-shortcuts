# 27.1 Convolutions

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 24.1, 24.2 (backprop as vector-Jacobian products), 25.1 (strides and views), 26.3 (augmentation and invariance). Lab: [`lab_27_1_convolutions.py`](labs/lab_27_1_convolutions.py).*

Feed a 224×224 color image to an MLP and the first layer alone, with 1,000 hidden units, has 150 million weights. Worse, it has to learn separately
that a cat in the top-left corner and a cat in the bottom-right corner are both cats, from examples of each. That's absurd, and the fix is two
assumptions about images that happen to be true: **nearby pixels matter together (locality)**, and **a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears
(translation)**. Build those into the layer and you get the convolution: a small filter slid across the image, with the same weights at every position.

Convolutional networks carried computer vision from 2012 until vision transformers arrived (30.2), and they're still everywhere: on phones, in cars,
in medical imaging, inside diffusion models (31.3). This lesson is the operation itself: shapes, parameter counts, receptive fields, how it's computed,
and what the built-in assumptions buy and cost. 27.2 builds architectures from it.

## The operation

A 2-D convolution layer takes an input of shape $(C_{\text{in}}, H, W)$ and $C_{\text{out}}$ filters, each of shape $(C_{\text{in}}, k, k)$. Output channel $o$ at position $(i, j)$ is

$$y_{o,i,j} = b_o + \sum_{c=1}^{C_{\text{in}}}\sum_{u=0}^{k-1}\sum_{v=0}^{k-1} w_{o,c,u,v}\;x_{c,\,s i + d u - p,\;s j + d v - p}$$

with stride $s$, padding $p$ (zeros around the border) and dilation $d$ (gaps between filter taps). Strictly, this is cross-correlation (a true convolution flips the kernel); since the weights are learned, nobody cares, and every framework
computes this.

**Output size** along each spatial dimension:

$$H_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor\frac{H + 2p - d(k - 1) - 1}{s}\right\rfloor + 1$$

The module's self-check: a 3×3 convolution with stride 2 and padding 1 on a 64×64 input gives $\lfloor(64 + 2 - 2 - 1)/2\rfloor + 1 = 31 + 1 = 32$, so 32×32. "Same" padding for odd $k$ is $p = (k - 1)/2$ with stride 1 (the output keeps the input's size).

**Parameters**: $C_{\text{out}}(C_{\text{in}}k^2 + 1)$, independent of the image size. A 3×3 layer from 64 to 128 channels has 73,856 parameters, whether the image is 32×32 or 4,000×4,000. **Compute** does depend on the image: about
$2\,C_{\text{out}}C_{\text{in}}k^2H_{\text{out}}W_{\text{out}}$ FLOPs, which is why high-resolution images are expensive and why networks shrink the spatial size as they go deeper.

## Building blocks

- **Pooling**: max or average over small windows (2×2, stride 2) to halve the resolution. Parameter-free. Max pooling gives a little local translation *invariance*. Modern networks often use strided convolutions instead.
- **Global average pooling**: average each channel over the whole spatial extent, turning $(C, H, W)$ into a $C$-vector for the classifier head. It makes the network accept any image size and replaced the huge fully connected layers of early CNNs.
- **1×1 convolutions**: a linear map across channels at each position, a per-pixel MLP layer. Used to change the number of channels cheaply (bottlenecks, 27.2).
- **Grouped and depthwise convolutions**: split the channels into groups convolved separately. Depthwise ($\text{groups} = C_{\text{in}}$: each channel gets its own $k \times k$ filter) followed by a 1×1 pointwise convolution is the **depthwise separable** convolution of MobileNet: roughly $k^2$ times fewer parameters and FLOPs than a full convolution, for a small accuracy cost.
- **Dilation** enlarges the filter's reach without more parameters (segmentation, audio).
- **Transposed convolution** ("deconvolution", a bad name): the operation whose forward pass is a convolution's backward pass with respect to its input. It upsamples (27.5). With stride larger than 1 and kernels not divisible by the stride it
  produces checkerboard artifacts; resize-then-convolve is often better.

## Receptive fields

The **receptive field** of a unit is the input region that can affect it. For a stack of layers with kernel sizes $k_\ell$ and strides $s_\ell$:

$$r_L = 1 + \sum_{\ell=1}^{L}(k_\ell - 1)\prod_{m<\ell}s_m$$

Three 3×3 stride-1 layers see 7×7; with a stride-2 layer early, everything after it grows twice as fast. A network needs receptive fields comparable to the objects it must recognize, which is one reason for downsampling. Two stacked 3×3
convolutions see as far as one 5×5, with fewer parameters ($2 \cdot 9 = 18$ vs 25 per channel pair) and an extra nonlinearity; that observation drove VGG's all-3×3 design (27.2). The **effective** receptive field (the region with
substantial influence, measured by gradients) is much smaller than the theoretical one and roughly Gaussian (Luo et al., 2016); the lab measures both.

## Equivariance, invariance, and what the assumptions buy

A convolution is **translation-equivariant**: shift the input, and the output shifts the same way (away from the borders, and exactly only for shifts that are multiples of the total stride). Pooling and global averaging add some **invariance** (the output
doesn't change). That's an **inductive bias**: an assumption built into the architecture that makes learning from limited data far more efficient when it's true (images) and harmful when it isn't (a tabular row has no "neighboring
columns"). The lab compares a CNN and an MLP with similar parameter counts on digits and on shifted digits (26.3's test): with no augmentation, the CNN loses far less accuracy on
shifted digits than the MLP, though it still loses some. It's not fully shift-invariant, though: strided
downsampling breaks exact equivariance, and small shifts can change CNN predictions noticeably (Zhang, 2019).

What the bias doesn't give you: rotation or scale invariance (learn them from augmentation), or long-range relationships early in the network (receptive fields grow slowly; attention, 29.1, relates distant positions in one step).

## How it's computed

A direct loop is correct and hopelessly slow in Python. The standard trick is **im2col**: copy every $k \times k \times C_{\text{in}}$ patch into a column of a big matrix (a strided view, 25.1, then a copy), and the convolution becomes one matrix multiplication
with the $(C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}k^2)$ weight matrix, which BLAS or the GPU executes at full speed (07.3). Libraries like cuDNN also use FFT and Winograd algorithms and pick the fastest per layer (`torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark`). The backward pass
is itself a pair of convolutions (with respect to the input: a transposed convolution; with respect to the weights: a correlation of the input with the output gradient). The lab implements the loop and the im2col versions and matches
PyTorch, including stride, padding, dilation and groups.

## Things that will bite you

- **Off-by-one output sizes**: use the formula, or run a dummy tensor through the network and print shapes.
- **Channels-first vs channels-last**: PyTorch is $(N, C, H, W)$; NumPy images and most image libraries are $(H, W, C)$.
- **Forgetting that a convolution's parameter count doesn't grow with the image**, while its compute and activation memory do.
- **Expecting rotation or scale invariance** for free.
- **Transposed-convolution checkerboards.**
- **Assuming exact shift invariance** in a strided network.
- **Input normalization** different at training and inference (26.5).
