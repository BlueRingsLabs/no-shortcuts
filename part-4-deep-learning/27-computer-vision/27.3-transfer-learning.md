# 27.3 Transfer learning

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 27.1, 27.2, 26.2 (learning rates), 26.3, 26.4 (BatchNorm's statistics). Lab: [`lab_27_3_transfer.py`](labs/lab_27_3_transfer.py).*

You will almost never train a vision model from random weights. Someone has already spent thousands of GPU-hours teaching a network to see edges,
textures, parts and objects on millions of images; you take that network, keep what it learned, and adapt it to your problem with a few hundred or
a few thousand labeled examples. That's **transfer learning**, and it's the single biggest practical lever in applied deep learning: it routinely
turns a problem that needs 100,000 labels into one that needs 1,000. The same pattern, pretrain big and adapt small, is how every language model
application works (Part V).

It also fails in specific, recognizable ways, and the lab shows two of them: fine-tuning that destroys the features it was supposed to use, and a
source task too narrow to transfer anywhere.

## Why it works

Networks trained on large, diverse datasets learn **general features in early layers** (oriented edges, color blobs, textures: nearly the same across datasets and even across architectures) and **increasingly task-specific features in
later layers** (Yosinski et al., 2014). The general ones are useful for almost any image task; the later ones are useful when your task resembles the source task. A pretrained network is a feature extractor whose features were paid for by
someone else's data.

## The options, from least to most adaptation

1. **Feature extraction / linear probe**: freeze the whole pretrained backbone, replace the classification head with a new one for your classes, and train only the head. Fast (compute the features once and train a logistic regression, 17.4, if
   you like), impossible to overfit badly, and a strong baseline. It measures how good the pretrained features are *as they are*.
2. **Partial fine-tuning**: unfreeze the last stage or two plus the head; keep the early, general layers frozen.
3. **Full fine-tuning**: train everything, starting from the pretrained weights. Usually the best accuracy when you have enough target data, and the one with the most ways to go wrong.

Choosing among them depends on two things: **how much labeled data** you have and **how similar** your images are to the pretraining data.

| | Similar domain | Different domain |
|---|---|---|
| **Little data** | linear probe, or fine-tune gently | probe features from *earlier* layers; fine-tune gently; get more data |
| **Lots of data** | full fine-tuning | full fine-tuning (pretraining still helps optimization); maybe train from scratch |

## Fine-tuning without breaking it

The failure the lab reproduces: fine-tune everything with a normal learning rate on 40 examples, and accuracy ends up *below* the linear probe. Large early gradients from a randomly initialized head flow back into the backbone and wreck
features that were already good; with few examples, the network then overfits what's left. The fixes:

- **A smaller learning rate for the backbone** than for the head (**discriminative learning rates**: e.g. head at $10^{-3}$, backbone at $10^{-4}$ or $10^{-5}$), in PyTorch via parameter groups. Warmup (26.2) helps too.
- **Linear probe, then fine-tune (LP-FT)** (Kumar et al., 2022): train the head first with the backbone frozen, so that when you unfreeze, the gradients reaching the backbone come from a sensible head instead of a random one. Simple,
  and it reliably helps, especially when the target data differs from the source.
- **Freeze BatchNorm statistics** (keep BN layers in eval mode, or freeze them entirely) when target batches are small or from a different distribution; otherwise the running statistics drift to your small batches' noise (26.4).
  The lab shows this matters as much as the learning rate.
- **Gradual unfreezing** (ULMFiT's idea): unfreeze from the top down, one stage per epoch.
- **Regularize and stop early** (26.3): augmentation, weight decay, and validation-based early stopping, since the target dataset is small.

## Practicalities

- **Match the pretraining preprocessing**: the same normalization constants (ImageNet's mean and standard deviation for torchvision models), color order (RGB vs BGR), and roughly the same resolution. A mismatch doesn't raise an error; it just
  quietly costs accuracy. The lab removes the normalization at transfer time and measures the damage.
- **Where to get backbones**: `torchvision.models` (with a `weights=` argument), `timm` (hundreds of backbones with benchmarks), Hugging Face. Check the **license** of both the weights and the data they were trained on before shipping anything
  commercial.
- **Foundation models as feature extractors**: self-supervised and image-text models (DINOv2, CLIP, 30.1 and 30.2) produce features that transfer more broadly than supervised ImageNet ones, and CLIP-style models can even classify **zero-shot** from
  class names. For many tasks today, the right first step is a linear probe on such a model.
- **Always compare** with a from-scratch model and a linear probe. If fine-tuning doesn't beat the probe, something is wrong (usually the learning rate or BatchNorm).

## When transfer doesn't help

Features are only as general as the task that produced them. A network pretrained on a narrow task (the lab's four synthetic shapes) learns features for that task, and they transfer poorly to a genuinely different one (digits): the linear probe
is barely better than chance, and from-scratch training does as well as fine-tuning. ImageNet features transfer widely because ImageNet is broad, not because pretraining is magic. For very different domains (medical scans, satellite
images, spectrograms), expect smaller gains, and consider domain-specific pretraining, including self-supervised pretraining on your own unlabeled data (30.1).

> **Scar tissue.** A classmate's plant disease classifier hit 98% on his test set and failed on every photo I took in the university
> greenhouse. The diseased leaves in his dataset had been photographed in the lab under warm lamps; the healthy ones outdoors, in
> daylight. The model had learned the color temperature. Grad-CAM heatmaps lit up the background, not the leaves. Balanced photos from
> both places and some color jitter turned the lamp detector into a disease detector. His thesis defense was a week later. It was a long
> week.

## Things that will bite you

- **Full fine-tuning with a from-scratch learning rate** on a small dataset.
- **BatchNorm statistics** updating on tiny or unrepresentative batches.
- **Preprocessing** different from pretraining.
- **Evaluating only the fine-tuned model**, without a linear-probe baseline.
- **Forgetting to replace the head** (or its bias initialization, 26.5) for the new number of classes.
- **Licenses** of weights and pretraining data.
- **Assuming any pretrained model transfers** to any domain.

## Exercises

1. You have 300 labeled images of a new product line, similar to everyday photos. Which approach do you try first, and what would make you move to full fine-tuning?
2. Why can full fine-tuning with a large learning rate end up worse than a linear probe on a small dataset?
3. Write the PyTorch optimizer setup for discriminative learning rates: head at $10^{-3}$, backbone at $10^{-4}$.
4. What happens to BatchNorm's running statistics when you fine-tune on batches of 8 medical images with a backbone pretrained on ImageNet, and what are two remedies?
5. A colleague reports that transfer learning "didn't help" on a thermal-camera dataset. What would you check before accepting that?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. A linear probe on a strong pretrained backbone (a supervised ImageNet model or DINOv2/CLIP features), with augmentation, as the baseline. Move to fine-tuning (gently: LP-FT, low backbone learning rate, frozen BatchNorm) if the probe's
   validation accuracy is below what's needed and there's evidence the features aren't quite right (confusions between classes that differ in fine details), or once more labels arrive.

2. The new head starts random, so its early gradients are large and essentially noise with respect to the backbone; a large learning rate lets them overwrite pretrained features faster than the head learns to use them. With few examples,
   the backbone then fits those examples instead of retaining general features, so both the representation and generalization degrade. The probe can't damage the features at all.

3. `opt = torch.optim.AdamW([{"params": model.backbone.parameters(), "lr": 1e-4}, {"params": model.head.parameters(), "lr": 1e-3}], weight_decay=0.01)`.

4. In training mode they're updated with each small batch's mean and variance: noisy estimates of a very different distribution, which replace the ImageNet statistics the later layers expect, degrading features and making evaluation
   inconsistent. Remedies: keep BatchNorm layers in eval mode (use the pretrained statistics, optionally still training $\gamma$ and $\beta$); or recompute the statistics once on a larger sample of target data; or use a backbone with GroupNorm or
   LayerNorm (26.4).

5. The preprocessing (thermal images are single-channel: how were they mapped to RGB? normalized with ImageNet constants?); the fine-tuning setup (learning rate, BatchNorm, LP-FT); the comparison (a linear probe and a from-scratch baseline,
   with the same augmentation and tuning effort); features from earlier layers (more general); and the amount of target data. It may be true (a very different domain), but "didn't help" is usually a setup problem first.
