# 27.2 CNN architectures: LeNet to ConvNeXt

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 27.1, 26.3, 26.4 (normalization, residuals). Lab: [`lab_27_2_architectures.py`](labs/lab_27_2_architectures.py).*

The history of CNN architectures is worth knowing for a practical reason: every design you'll pick off the shelf is a combination of a handful of
ideas introduced one at a time, each fixing a specific problem of its predecessor. Know the ideas and you can read any architecture diagram, choose a
backbone sensibly, and modify one without breaking it. This lesson walks the lineage from 1998 to 2022, and the lab builds a small version of each
design and trains them on the same budget.

## LeNet (1998): the template

LeCun's LeNet-5 read handwritten digits on bank cheques: convolution, subsampling, convolution, subsampling, then fully connected layers to the classes. Tiny (60,000 parameters), trained with backprop, deployed commercially. The
**template** it set still holds: **stacks of convolutions that shrink the spatial size and grow the number of channels**, followed by a classifier head. What's changed since is how the stacks are built.

## AlexNet (2012): the same idea, at scale

Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton won ImageNet 2012 by a margin that ended the debate about neural networks in vision. The architecture was a bigger LeNet; what made it work was the recipe around it: **ReLU** instead of saturating
activations (24.1), **dropout** in the large fully connected layers (26.3), heavy **data augmentation**, and training on **GPUs**. Most of the lessons of AlexNet are in Modules 25 and 26, not in its layer diagram.

## VGG (2014): depth from small filters

Simonyan and Zisserman showed that **depth** matters and that it's best obtained from stacks of **3×3** convolutions only (27.1: two 3×3 layers see 5×5 with fewer parameters and an extra nonlinearity). Uniform, easy to understand, and expensive: VGG-16 has
138 million parameters, most of them in the fully connected head, and deeper VGGs stopped improving (the degradation problem, 26.4).

## Inception / GoogLeNet (2014): width and 1×1 bottlenecks

Szegedy et al. used **parallel branches** (1×1, 3×3, 5×5 convolutions and pooling, concatenated), letting each block look at several scales, and **1×1 convolutions to reduce channels** before the expensive large filters. Much better
accuracy per FLOP than VGG, at the price of intricate hand design. The 1×1 **bottleneck** idea outlived the rest.

## ResNet (2015): residual connections

He et al. added **identity shortcuts** (26.4), which fixed the degradation problem and made 50, 101 and 152-layer networks trainable. The **bottleneck block** (1×1 to reduce channels, 3×3, 1×1 to expand, plus the shortcut) keeps the cost down in deep
versions. ResNet's structure is the canonical backbone layout: a **stem** (7×7 stride-2 convolution and max pooling: 224 to 56), **four stages** of blocks, each stage halving the resolution and doubling the channels, **global average pooling**, one
linear layer. ResNet-50 (25 million parameters, about 4 GFLOPs at 224×224) is still the reference baseline in countless papers and production systems. (DenseNet, 2017, concatenated every earlier layer's features instead of adding
them; parameter-efficient, memory-hungry, less used today.)

## MobileNet and EfficientNet (2017 to 2019): cost matters

For phones and embedded devices, FLOPs and memory dominate. **MobileNet** replaced standard convolutions with **depthwise separable** ones (27.1: about $k^2$ times cheaper). **MobileNetV2** introduced the **inverted residual**: expand channels with a 1×1, do a cheap
depthwise 3×3 in the wide space, project back down with a linear (no activation) 1×1, with the shortcut between the narrow ends. **EfficientNet** (Tan and Le, 2019) searched for a good small network and then **scaled depth, width and
resolution together** by fixed ratios ("compound scaling"), rather than just adding layers.

A warning the lab makes concrete: **FLOPs and parameters aren't latency.** Depthwise convolutions have few FLOPs but low arithmetic intensity (07.3): they're memory-bound, and on GPUs a "cheaper" MobileNet can run no faster than a
ResNet with several times the FLOPs. Always measure on the target hardware.

## ConvNeXt (2022): a ResNet modernized

When vision transformers (30.2) started beating CNNs, Liu et al. asked how much of the gap came from the transformer's *training recipe and design details* rather than attention itself. They modernized ResNet-50 step by step toward the
ViT design: the AdamW-and-heavy-augmentation training recipe, a **patchify stem** (4×4 stride-4 convolution), **large 7×7 depthwise** convolutions, an **inverted bottleneck** (MobileNetV2's), **LayerNorm** instead of BatchNorm, **GELU**, fewer
normalization and activation layers, and separate downsampling layers. The result matched Swin Transformers at similar cost. The lesson for engineers: much of what looks like an architecture breakthrough is training
recipe and accumulated details; compare architectures only with equally good recipes.

## Choosing a backbone in practice

- **Start pretrained** (27.3): you'll almost never train an ImageNet-scale backbone from scratch.
- **Pick by constraint**: accuracy target, latency on *your* hardware, memory, input resolution. ResNet-50 and ConvNeXt-T for general use; MobileNetV3 or EfficientNet-Lite on devices; ViTs (30.2) and their hybrids when data or pretraining is large.
  The `timm` library (PyTorch Image Models) has hundreds of pretrained backbones with benchmarks.
- **Resolution** is a knob as important as depth: small objects need pixels.
- **Modify carefully**: changing the stem or the number of classes is routine; changing normalization layers in a pretrained network breaks its learned statistics.

## Things that will bite you

- **Comparing architectures** trained with different recipes.
- **FLOPs as a proxy for speed** on the actual device.
- **BatchNorm backbones with tiny batches** (26.4); use GroupNorm or freeze the BatchNorm statistics.
- **Input resolution** different from pretraining without reason (positional assumptions, receptive fields).
- **A huge fully connected head** on top of the convolutions (VGG's mistake): global average pooling instead.

## Exercises

1. Count the parameters of a ResNet basic block (two 3×3 convolutions, 64 channels, with BatchNorm) and of a bottleneck block (1×1 256→64, 3×3 64→64, 1×1 64→256).
2. Why does an inverted residual block's projection layer have no activation?
3. VGG-16 has 138M parameters, of which about 124M are in its three fully connected layers. What replaced them in later architectures, and why doesn't that hurt accuracy?
4. A depthwise separable layer has one-eighth of the FLOPs of the standard convolution it replaces, but the model isn't faster on your GPU. Explain.
5. List the changes that turned a ResNet-50 into a ConvNeXt-T. Which of them are about the training recipe rather than the architecture?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Basic: two convolutions $64 \cdot 64 \cdot 9 = 36{,}864$ each (no bias, followed by BatchNorm), plus two BatchNorms with $2 \cdot 64$ each: $73{,}728 + 256 = 73{,}984$. Bottleneck: $256 \cdot 64 + 64 \cdot 64 \cdot 9 + 64 \cdot 256 = 16{,}384 + 36{,}864 + 16{,}384 = 69{,}632$, plus BatchNorms
   $2 \cdot (64 + 64 + 256) = 768$: 70,400, for a block operating on 256 channels. That's the point of the bottleneck: four times the width for similar cost.

2. The projection maps back into a narrow, low-dimensional space; a ReLU there would zero out part of an already small representation and destroy information (the MobileNetV2 authors show this). Nonlinearities are applied in the
   wide expanded space, where losing some dimensions is cheap.

3. Global average pooling over the last feature map followed by a single linear layer. The convolutional features already encode "what" is present; the fully connected layers mainly added parameters (and overfitting) by memorizing
   "where". Averaging keeps the evidence and discards the position, which classification doesn't need.

4. Depthwise convolutions do very little arithmetic per byte of memory read (each input channel is used by only one filter), so they're limited by memory bandwidth, not compute, and they map poorly onto the GPU's matrix units
   (07.3, 33.1). The FLOP count drops, the memory traffic doesn't drop as much, and the extra layers add kernel launch overhead. Measure latency, not FLOPs.

5. Training recipe: AdamW, longer training, heavy augmentation (Mixup, CutMix, RandAugment), stochastic depth, label smoothing, EMA of weights. Architecture: stage ratio changes, a 4×4 stride-4 patchify stem, depthwise convolutions with wider
   channels, an inverted bottleneck, 7×7 kernels (with the depthwise layer moved up), GELU instead of ReLU, fewer activations and normalizations, LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm, separate downsampling layers. The recipe alone gave a large
   share of the improvement.
