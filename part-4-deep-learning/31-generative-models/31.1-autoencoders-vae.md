## Autoencoders

An **autoencoder** is an encoder $z = f(x)$ to a smaller **latent code** and a decoder $\hat x = g(z)$ back, trained to reconstruct its input: minimize $\|x - g(f(x))\|^2$. The bottleneck forces it to keep what matters for reconstruction and discard the rest.

- A **linear** autoencoder with squared error learns the same **subspace** as PCA (Baldi and Hornik, 1989), though not the same axes: any rotation of the code works equally well. The lab checks both halves: the subspace angles between a trained linear autoencoder and PCA have cosines of 1.000, while individual decoder directions match individual principal components poorly (cosines 0.5 to 0.7). It also needed 600 epochs to get there; gradient descent finds the directions that matter least very slowly.
- **Nonlinear** autoencoders do better per latent dimension: on the lab's digits, with 2 latents, reconstruction error drops from 0.053 (PCA) to 0.034.
- Variants: **denoising** autoencoders reconstruct $x$ from a corrupted copy (a regularizer, and a direct ancestor of diffusion, 31.3); **sparse** autoencoders penalize activations instead of shrinking the code, and they're now the main tool for finding interpretable features inside LLMs.

Uses: compression, denoising, pretraining, **anomaly detection** by reconstruction error (23.3), and as the compressor in latent diffusion.

What an autoencoder can't do is **generate**. Its decoder only knows the codes it was trained on, scattered in some irregular region of latent space, and nothing tells you where that region is. Pick a random $z$ and decode it, and you're decoding a point the model never saw. The lab does the most you can: fit a Gaussian to the training codes and sample from it. On 8×8 digits with 8 latents that works tolerably (a digit classifier is 80% confident on average, against 96% on real digits), because the codes of a small, well-behaved dataset happen to be roughly Gaussian. It's luck, not design, and it fails on anything harder.

## The variational autoencoder

The VAE (Kingma and Welling, 2014; Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra, 2014) makes the latent space **samplable by construction**. It's a latent-variable model:

- **Prior** $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
- **Decoder** (likelihood) $p_\theta(x \mid z)$: a network outputting, for example, a Bernoulli probability per pixel, or a Gaussian mean.
- The model's distribution over data is $p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz$. Training should maximize $\log p_\theta(x)$, but the integral over all $z$ is intractable.

The fix is an **encoder** $q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2_\phi(x))$ that guesses which $z$ could have produced $x$, and a bound. For any $q$:

$$\log p_\theta(x) \;\ge\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\left[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\right]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{\operatorname{KL}\left(q_\phi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\right)}_{\text{stay close to the prior}} \;=\; \text{ELBO}.$$

(Derivation: $\log p(x) = \text{ELBO} + \operatorname{KL}(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z \mid x))$, and a KL is never negative, so the bound is tight when the encoder matches the true posterior.) Maximize the ELBO over both networks. The two terms say what the model is doing:

- **Reconstruction**: codes sampled from the encoder must decode back to $x$. For Bernoulli pixels it's binary cross-entropy; for a Gaussian with fixed variance, squared error up to a constant.
- **KL**: each input's code distribution must stay close to $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. For two Gaussians it's closed form, $\operatorname{KL} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_j \left(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2\right)$, which the lab checks against a Monte Carlo estimate.
  This term is what fills the latent space: codes can't hide in a corner, so decoding a sample from the prior lands somewhere the decoder has been trained.

The lab's VAE on digits gets a judge confidence of 0.82 on samples from the prior, a little better than the autoencoder-plus-fitted-Gaussian (0.80), with a guarantee instead of luck. The samples are recognizably digits and noticeably blurry, which is the VAE's signature (below).

## The reparameterization trick

The ELBO's first term is an expectation over $z \sim q_\phi(z \mid x)$, and we need its gradient with respect to $\phi$, the parameters of the distribution we're sampling from. You can't backpropagate through "draw a random number".

Write the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and independent noise: $z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Now the randomness is an input, $z$ is a differentiable function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$, and ordinary backprop works. That's the **reparameterization trick** (or pathwise gradient).

The alternative, the **score-function** estimator (REINFORCE, which reinforcement learning uses because it has no choice, 46.5), works for any distribution but has much higher variance. The lab estimates the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)}[z^2]$ with respect to $\mu$ both ways: both are unbiased, and the score-function estimator needs 13 times as many samples for the same precision on this one-dimensional toy. In high dimensions the gap is much worse.

## Blur, posterior collapse and beta

**Why VAE samples are blurry.** With a Gaussian or Bernoulli decoder, the likelihood is maximized by predicting the **average** of all images consistent with $z$. When the code doesn't pin down every detail (it never does), the average of the possibilities is a blur. That's the likelihood doing its job, and it's why GANs (31.2) and diffusion (31.3), which don't average, produce sharper images.

**$\beta$-VAE** (Higgins et al., 2017) weights the KL term by $\beta$. In information terms, the KL is the **rate** (how many nats the code carries about $x$) and the reconstruction term is the **distortion**. The lab's table:

| $\beta$ | reconstruction (nats) | KL (nats) | active latents | judge confidence on samples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 16.5 | 14.6 | 8 of 8 | 0.79 |
| 1 | 20.4 | 3.7 | 5 of 8 | 0.82 |
| 4 | 27.2 | 0.0 | 0 of 8 | 0.65 |

Small $\beta$ lets the code carry more information: better reconstructions, but a latent space whose occupied region no longer matches the prior, so prior samples fall into holes. And at $\beta = 4$ the model gives up on the latent code altogether: KL zero, no active latents, and the decoder draws the same average digit whatever $z$ is. That's **posterior collapse**: the KL term makes every bit of information in $z$ costly, and the decoder finds it cheaper to ignore $z$ and model the data's average.
It's also a real problem at $\beta = 1$ with powerful decoders (an autoregressive decoder, such as an LSTM or transformer that can model the data on its own, often ignores $z$ completely). Remedies: KL annealing (start with $\beta = 0$ and ramp up), "free bits" (don't penalize the first few nats of KL per dimension), weaker decoders.

Even at $\beta = 1$, only 5 of the 8 latents carry information (KL above 0.1 nats each); the rest are switched off, their posterior equal to the prior. VAEs pick their own effective dimensionality, which is one reason they're used for representation learning and disentanglement studies.

## Where VAEs are now

VAEs are rarely the final generator for images anymore: their samples are too blurry next to diffusion's. They are everywhere as **components**:

- The **autoencoder of latent diffusion** (Stable Diffusion's "VAE", 31.4) compresses a 512×512×3 image to a 64×64×4 latent, trained with a small KL weight plus perceptual and adversarial losses so its reconstructions are sharp.
- **VQ-VAE** (van den Oord et al., 2017) replaces the Gaussian code with a discrete codebook, turning images and audio into **tokens** that a transformer can model like text; that idea underlies many audio and multimodal generators.
- Scientific uses where a smooth latent space and a likelihood matter: molecule design, single-cell biology, anomaly scores.

## Things that will bite you

- **Treating an autoencoder as a generator.**
- **Posterior collapse**, and a KL term of zero reported as "converged".
- **The KL summed over dimensions but the reconstruction averaged over pixels** (or vice versa): an accidental $\beta$ of 64 or $1/64$. Use sums for both, per example, then average over the batch.
- **`logvar` instead of `var`** confusion: the encoder should output the log-variance for stability; exponentiate once, in one place.
- **Comparing ELBOs** across models with different decoder likelihoods (Bernoulli vs Gaussian): the numbers aren't on the same scale.
- **Blurry samples** "fixed" by lowering $\beta$, which breaks sampling instead.

1. $\operatorname{KL}(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z|x)) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z|x) - \log p(z|x)]$ and $p(z|x) = p(x|z)p(z)/p(x)$. Rearranging gives the identity; since the KL is at least 0, the ELBO is a lower bound, tight when $q$ equals the true posterior.

2. $\operatorname{KL} = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q - \log p]$ with $\log q(z) = -\frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ and $\log p(z) = -\frac12\log 2\pi - \frac{z^2}{2}$. Under $q$: $\mathbb{E}[(z-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb{E}[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$. So $\operatorname{KL} = -\frac12\log\sigma^2 - \frac12 + \frac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2) = \frac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)$.

3. Sampling is not a differentiable function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$ as written: the output is a random draw, and there's no path for gradients from $z$ back to the parameters. Writing $z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon$ independent of the parameters makes $z$ a differentiable function of them for each fixed $\varepsilon$, so the gradient of the expectation is the expectation of ordinary gradients. For discrete $z$ there's no such smooth function (a small change in the probabilities doesn't change the sample smoothly); options are the score-function estimator (high variance), continuous relaxations like Gumbel-softmax, or the straight-through estimator (VQ-VAE).

4. Posterior collapse: the autoregressive decoder can model the text well on its own, so the cheapest way to maximize the ELBO is to make $q(z|x)$ equal the prior (KL = 0) and ignore $z$. Try KL annealing (warm up $\beta$ from 0), free bits (no KL penalty below a threshold per dimension), word dropout in the decoder's inputs (so it must rely on $z$), or a weaker decoder.

5. At $\beta = 4$ the KL penalty is strong enough that encoding any information in $z$ costs more than it gains, so the model collapses: KL 0, no active latents, and the decoder outputs the same average digit for every $z$. Samples from the prior are then all the same blurry average, which the judge classifies with low confidence (0.65). "Closer to the prior" was meant to make the latent space fill in; pushed too far, it empties it.

6. The VAE maximizes a likelihood with a simple per-pixel decoder distribution. When $z$ doesn't determine every detail, the maximizing prediction is the average over the plausible images, which blurs edges and textures. A GAN's generator is trained to fool a discriminator, which easily detects blur, so it's pushed to commit to one sharp image instead of an average, at the cost of other failure modes (31.2).
