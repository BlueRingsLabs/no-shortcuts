# 31.2 GANs

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 31.1, 06.1 (KL and Jensen-Shannon divergences), 26.1 (Adam), 24.1 (saturation). Lab: [`lab_31_2_gans.py`](labs/lab_31_2_gans.py).*

A generative adversarial network is two networks in an arms race: a **generator** that turns random noise into samples, and a
**discriminator** that tries to tell those samples from real data. Each improves by exploiting the other's weaknesses. When it works, the
generator learns to produce samples the discriminator can't distinguish from the real thing, without ever writing down a likelihood. From
2014 to about 2021, GANs made the most realistic images anyone had seen (StyleGAN's faces are the ones you've been fooled by).

They also have a reputation, earned, for being the most temperamental models in deep learning. The lab reproduces that reputation precisely:
the same code on the same data with three random seeds gives one excellent model, one that gets there late, and one that never does. Diffusion
(31.3) took over image generation mostly because it doesn't do that. GANs are still worth understanding because the adversarial idea is
everywhere: in the losses of the autoencoders inside latent diffusion, in one-step generators distilled from diffusion models, in audio
vocoders, in super-resolution, and in every deepfake detector's future.

## The game

The generator $G$ maps noise $z \sim p(z)$ to samples; the discriminator $D(x) \in (0, 1)$ outputs the probability that $x$ is real. Goodfellow et al. (2014) set up a minimax game:

$$\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p(z)}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))].$$

The discriminator is a binary classifier with cross-entropy loss (real = 1, fake = 0). The generator tries to make that classifier wrong. Training alternates a step on $D$ and a step on $G$.

**The optimal discriminator.** For a fixed generator with sample density $p_g$, the $D$ that maximizes the objective is

$$D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)},$$

and substituting it back gives $2\,\operatorname{JSD}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g) - \log 4$: the generator minimizes the **Jensen-Shannon divergence** between the data and its samples, which is zero only when they're equal. The lab trains a discriminator between two fixed 1D Gaussians and it matches $D^*$ to within 0.01 wherever there's data. Where there isn't (four standard deviations out), its output is whatever its extrapolation happens to be, 0.78 against a true 0.24. A discriminator only knows the regions it has seen, which is part of why a generator can wander off into nonsense and get rewarded for it.

**The non-saturating loss.** Early in training, fakes are easy to spot: $D(G(z)) \approx 0$. There, $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$ is flat: its gradient with respect to the discriminator's logit is $-D(G(z)) \approx 0$ (the lab computes 0.0003 at $D = 0.0003$). The generator gets no signal at exactly the moment it needs one. The fix, in the original paper: have the generator **maximize $\log D(G(z))$** instead, the same fixed point with strong gradients when the generator is losing (gradient near $-1$). Everyone uses this version; it's 24.1's saturation problem again, fixed by choosing which side of the log to stand on.

## How it fails

- **Non-convergence**: a two-player game optimized by alternating gradient steps doesn't have to converge; it can orbit. Losses that oscillate forever are normal, and the losses tell you almost nothing about sample quality.
- **Mode collapse**: the generator produces only a few kinds of output. It found something the current discriminator accepts, the discriminator adapts, and the generator jumps to another mode instead of spreading out (**mode hopping**). Full collapse is obvious; **partial** collapse is worse, because each sample looks fine. The lab's digit GAN makes plausible digits (a classifier is 85% confident, against 97% on real ones) but almost never makes 0s, 2s and 7s (21 to 57 of 2,000 samples, against 200 expected). You'd never notice by looking at samples one at a time.
- **Discriminator too good**: with disjoint supports (common, since real images lie near a low-dimensional manifold), a perfect discriminator exists, the JSD is constant at $\log 2$ and gives no useful gradient (Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017).
- **Sensitivity to everything**: seeds, learning rates, architectures, batch size. The lab's ring of 8 Gaussians, same code, three seeds, vanilla GAN, final (modes covered, fraction of samples near a mode): (8, 0.68), (8, 0.89), (1, 0.38). One run found all modes by the second checkpoint; one needed 2,500 steps of flailing; one never got there.

## The fixes

**Wasserstein GANs** (Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017) replace the JSD with the **earth mover's (Wasserstein-1) distance**: the minimum cost of moving the generated mass onto the real distribution. It stays informative even when the distributions don't overlap: moving mass a little closer lowers it. By Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality it equals $\max_{\|f\|_L \le 1} \mathbb{E}_{\text{data}}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{g}[f(x)]$, where the maximum is over **1-Lipschitz** functions. So the discriminator becomes a **critic** $f$ with an unbounded real output, trained to maximize that difference, and it must be kept Lipschitz. Weight clipping did it badly; **WGAN-GP** (Gulrajani et al., 2017) adds a **gradient penalty**, $\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[(\|\nabla f(\hat x)\| - 1)^2]$ at random points between real and fake samples.
The lab's WGAN-GP covers all 8 modes on both seeds within 1,000 of its 1,500 steps, but with only 58% to 59% of samples near a mode against the best vanilla run's 89%: it spreads mass between the modes, the typical tradeoff of reliability against sharpness at a small budget.

One hyperparameter matters more than it looks: the penalty weight $\lambda$. The paper's $\lambda = 10$ is its setting for images. On this small 2D problem it over-constrains the critic, and my first version of the lab, which used it, stalled for 1,000 to 2,000 steps or never covered every mode, depending on the seed and even on the CPU: the same code passed on one machine and failed on another, because rounding differences decided which runs got unstuck in time. The paper's own code for its toy datasets uses $\lambda = 0.1$ (and Adam with $\beta_1 = 0.5$), and with those every run I tried, on every CPU code path, covered all 8 modes by step 750. "Reliable" is a property of a method *and* its settings, checked across seeds and machines.

Other tools that became standard:

- **Spectral normalization** (Miyato et al., 2018): divide each weight matrix of the discriminator by its largest singular value, a cheap Lipschitz constraint that works with the ordinary GAN loss.
- **R1 penalty** (Mescheder, Geiger and Nowozin, 2018): penalize the discriminator's gradient norm on real data. It's what StyleGAN uses, with a proof of local convergence behind it.
- **Two time-scale updates** (TTUR, Heusel et al., 2017): different learning rates for $D$ and $G$; **Adam with $\beta_1 = 0.5$** or 0 (the lab uses both), because momentum destabilizes a game.
- **One-sided label smoothing** (real labels 0.9, not 1; the lab's digit GAN) keeps the discriminator from becoming overconfident.
- **Architecture**: DCGAN's guidelines (Radford, Metz and Chintala, 2016: strided convolutions, BatchNorm, no pooling), progressive growing (Karras et al., 2018), and **StyleGAN** (Karras et al., 2019, 2020): a mapping network turns $z$ into a "style" vector that modulates every layer of the generator, giving control over coarse and fine features and the best face images before diffusion. **BigGAN** (Brock et al., 2019) scaled class-conditional GANs and introduced the **truncation trick** (sample $z$ from a truncated normal): trade diversity for quality with one knob.

## Conditional GANs and image translation

Feed a label (or an image) to both networks and the generator learns $p(x \mid y)$. **pix2pix** (Isola et al., 2017) maps paired images (sketches to photos, maps to satellite views) with an adversarial loss plus an L1 loss; **CycleGAN** (Zhu et al., 2017) does it without pairs, enforcing that translating A to B and back gives A. Super-resolution models (SRGAN, ESRGAN) use an adversarial term to produce sharp textures where a pixel loss would produce blur: 31.1's averaging argument, fixed by a critic that rejects averages.

## Evaluating generative models

The hardest part, and the part most often done badly.

- **Inception Score** (Salimans et al., 2016): confident class predictions per sample and diverse classes overall, according to an ImageNet classifier. Blind to whether samples resemble *your* data.
- **FID** (Heusel et al., 2017): fit a Gaussian to Inception features of real images and one to generated images, and compute the **Fréchet distance** between them. Lower is better; it catches both poor quality and missing diversity. It's the standard, and it's biased by sample size (always compare at the same $N$, typically 50,000), sensitive to image resizing and compression details (use a fixed implementation such as clean-fid), and it measures similarity in the feature space of a 2015 ImageNet classifier, which may not care about what you care about.
- **Precision and recall for generative models** (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2019): precision = fraction of generated samples that fall in the real data's manifold (quality); recall = fraction of real data covered by the generated manifold (diversity). The lab's two numbers on the ring are exactly these: the fraction of samples near a mode is precision, and modes covered is recall. One number can't tell a model that makes perfect copies of three modes from one that makes blurry versions of all eight.
- **Look at samples**, and look at the **distribution of samples**: class histograms by a classifier (the lab's digit counts), nearest training neighbors (to catch memorization), and failure cases, not just the curated grid.

## Deepfakes and detection

GAN-generated faces and, now more often, diffusion-generated and face-swapped video are used for fraud, harassment and disinformation. For anyone who has worked in security, the structure of the detection problem should look familiar: a deepfake detector *is* a discriminator. Publish a good one and you've published a training signal for the next generator. Detection works for a while against known generators and degrades against new ones.
The more durable defenses are about **provenance** rather than detection: cryptographically signed capture and edit history (C2PA content credentials), watermarking at generation time, and processes that don't treat a video call or a voice as proof of identity (call back on a known number; that one's older than neural networks).

> **Scar tissue.** A research group at my university wanted synthetic patient records so students could practice on something realistic,
> and I volunteered a GAN. The first samples looked great. A closer look showed that the generator had found about a dozen very convincing
> patients and produced them over and over, with a little noise: classic mode collapse, invisible in per-column histograms and obvious in
> a nearest-neighbor check against itself. Worse, a few of those dozen were very close to real individuals in the source data. Synthetic
> data needs its own privacy audit (54.2).

## Things that will bite you

- **The saturating generator loss.**
- **Reading the loss curves** as a measure of quality.
- **Judging from a few curated samples**: partial mode collapse is invisible that way.
- **One seed**: GAN results vary wildly between runs. Report several.
- **FID at different sample sizes or with different implementations**, compared as if equal.
- **BatchNorm in the discriminator with WGAN-GP** (the penalty is per sample; BatchNorm mixes samples; use LayerNorm or none).
- **Copying $\lambda = 10$ to every problem**: it's tuned for images; small, low-dimensional problems need a much smaller penalty.
- **Memorization**: a generator that reproduces training images looks great on every metric that doesn't check for it.

1. For fixed $G$, the objective is $\int \left[p_{\text{data}}(x)\log D(x) + p_g(x)\log(1 - D(x))\right] dx$. Maximize the integrand pointwise: for $a\log y + b\log(1 - y)$, the derivative $a/y - b/(1-y) = 0$ gives $y = a/(a + b)$. So $D^*(x) = p_{\text{data}}(x)/(p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x))$.

2. With $D = \sigma(l)$: $\frac{d}{dl}\log(1 - \sigma(l)) = -\sigma(l) = -0.01$, and $\frac{d}{dl}(-\log\sigma(l)) = -(1 - \sigma(l)) = -0.99$. The saturating loss gives the generator a gradient 99 times weaker exactly when its samples are bad, so it learns slowly or not at all early in training; the non-saturating loss gives a strong signal then.

3. At each step the generator moves its samples toward wherever the current discriminator says "real" most confidently. Nothing in that step rewards covering all of the data; concentrating on the single most convincing region is often the fastest way to fool the current discriminator. The discriminator then learns to reject that region, and the generator moves its concentrated mass elsewhere (mode hopping), rather than spreading out. The game has a fixed point with full coverage, but alternating gradient steps don't have to reach it.

4. When the supports are disjoint, the JSD is constant ($\log 2$) whatever the distance between them, so its gradient with respect to the generator's parameters is zero (or a perfect discriminator saturates); moving the samples closer doesn't change the loss. The Wasserstein distance measures how far mass has to move, so it decreases continuously as the generated distribution approaches the real one, even without overlap, giving a useful gradient everywhere.

5. A: high precision (every sample is on the data manifold), low recall (6 of 10 classes missing). B: lower precision (blur takes samples slightly off the manifold), high recall. FID usually prefers B, since missing 60% of the classes shifts the feature mean and covariance a lot, but the ranking depends on how blurry B is; FID folds both failures into one number. Precision and recall show the tradeoff explicitly.

6. Weaknesses: it's trained on known generation methods and degrades on new ones; attackers can test against it and adapt (and if it's exposed as an API, use it as a training signal); false positives block legitimate customers (compression, low light, unusual faces), with fairness implications; adversarial perturbations can evade it. Recommend it as one signal among several: liveness checks with unpredictable challenges, device and session risk signals, verification through an independent channel for high-value actions, provenance data (C2PA) where available, and a process that assumes some fakes will get through (limits, monitoring, reversal). Measure its performance on current, in-house red-team fakes (44.2), not only on public benchmarks.
