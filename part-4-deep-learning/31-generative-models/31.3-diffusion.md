# 31.3 Diffusion models

*About 12 hours. Prerequisites: 31.1 (ELBO, reparameterization), 31.2 (why GANs were replaced), 04.1 (Gaussians), 27.5 (U-Net). Lab: [`lab_31_3_diffusion.py`](labs/lab_31_3_diffusion.py).*

Here's a way to generate images that sounds like it shouldn't work. Take a training image and add a little Gaussian noise. Add a little more.
Keep going for a thousand steps until it's pure static. Now train a network to undo **one** step: given a noisy image and how noisy it is,
predict the noise that was added. To generate, start from pure static and apply the network a thousand times.

It works absurdly well. Diffusion models displaced GANs as the state of the art for images around 2021 and now generate images, video, audio,
3D shapes, molecules and robot actions. The reasons are mostly boring, which is a compliment: the training loss is a plain regression, it
doesn't need a second network to fight, and it doesn't drop modes. The cost is slow sampling, and much of the research since has been about
paying less of it.

## The forward process

Fix a **noise schedule** $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$ (small numbers, $T$ around 1,000) and define the noising steps

$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\; \beta_t I\right).$$

The $\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}$ shrinks the signal so the variance stays bounded. Because Gaussians compose, you don't have to iterate: with $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \le t}\alpha_s$,

$$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\right), \qquad x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon.$$

Any noise level can be sampled in one shot, which is what makes training cheap. The lab iterates 300 steps on 100,000 points and matches the closed form's mean and variance to three decimals. As $t \to T$, $\bar\alpha_t \to 0$ and $x_T$ is (nearly) standard Gaussian noise, whatever $x_0$ was.

**Schedules.** The quantity that matters is the **signal-to-noise ratio** $\bar\alpha_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)$. Ho et al.'s linear schedule ($\beta$ from $10^{-4}$ to 0.02) keeps 8% of the signal variance at $t = 500$ and 0.3% at $t = 750$: its last quarter of steps is spent on nearly pure noise. Nichol and Dhariwal's **cosine** schedule spends the steps more evenly. The lab trains the same denoiser with the same budget on 31.2's ring of Gaussians under both: 71% of samples land near a mode with the linear schedule, 98% with the cosine one. For higher-resolution images the ideal schedule shifts again (more noise is needed to destroy the structure of a large image), which is why schedules are still tuned per model.

## The reverse process and the loss

Generation runs the chain backward: start from $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and repeatedly sample $x_{t-1}$ from a learned $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$. For small $\beta_t$, the true reverse step is close to Gaussian, so the model is a Gaussian whose mean a network predicts.

DDPM (Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020) parameterizes that mean through a **noise predictor** $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$: given the noisy input and the step, guess the $\varepsilon$ that was mixed in. The training loss:

$$L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\, t,\, \varepsilon}\left\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\; t\right)\right\|^2,$$

with $t$ uniform. That's the whole training loop: pick an image, a time and some noise; noise the image; regress the noise. It is a reweighted version of the ELBO from 31.1 (a diffusion model is a VAE with a fixed encoder and a thousand latent layers), and the reweighting (dropping the ELBO's weights) is what makes samples look good.

Sampling (ancestral, DDPM): $x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, z$, with fresh noise $z$ at each step except the last. A thousand network evaluations per sample.

Other parameterizations of the same thing: predict $x_0$ directly, or predict $v = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,x_0$ ("v-prediction", better behaved at the extremes of the schedule). They're interconvertible given $x_t$; the choice changes the effective loss weighting.

## The score view

Why does predicting noise produce samples? Because the best possible noise predictor is the **score** of the noised data distribution, scaled:

$$\varepsilon^*(x_t, t) = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\;\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t),$$

where $p_t$ is the data distribution after noising to level $t$ (this is Tweedie's formula). The score points toward higher density: "which way is more data-like from here". Sampling follows it downhill in noise, uphill in probability, with some noise injected to keep samples diverse.

The lab checks this on a 1D mixture of two Gaussians, where $p_t$ and its score are known exactly: the trained network's output matches $\varepsilon^*$ with a mean absolute error of 0.055. The largest error (0.31) is at $x = 0$ at a low noise level, in the gap between the two modes, where lightly noised training samples almost never land: the network learned the score where it saw data.

This is the bridge to **score-based generative models** (Song and Ermon, 2019), developed in parallel, and to Song et al. (2021), who showed that both are discretizations of a **stochastic differential equation** that turns data into noise, with a reverse-time SDE that turns noise into data given the score, and an equivalent **probability flow ODE**: a deterministic path with the same marginals. That ODE is the key to fast sampling and to flow matching (31.4).

## Faster sampling

- **DDIM** (Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021) derives a non-Markovian sampler for the *same* trained model: predict $\hat x_0$ from the current $x_t$ and $\varepsilon_\theta$, then jump directly to the next chosen noise level: $x_{t'} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t'}}\,\hat x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t'}}\,\varepsilon_\theta$. With no added noise it's deterministic (a discretization of the probability flow ODE), and it tolerates large jumps. The lab's ring, same network: 5 DDIM steps put 79% of samples near a mode, 10 steps 91%, 25 steps 96%, against 98% for 1,000 DDPM steps. A 40-fold speedup for two points.
  Determinism also gives each sample a fixed latent $x_T$, so you can interpolate between noise vectors or invert a real image into the latent space.
- **Better ODE solvers** (DPM-Solver, Heun as in Karras et al.'s EDM) get good samples in 10 to 25 steps.
- **Distillation** trains a student to take bigger steps: progressive distillation halves the step count repeatedly; **consistency models** learn to map any point on the ODE path straight to its endpoint; adversarial distillation adds a GAN loss (31.2's idea, back in service) for 1 to 4 step generation.

## Architecture

The denoiser takes $(x_t, t)$ and outputs something the shape of $x_t$, so for images it's a **U-Net** (27.5) with the time step injected into every block (a sinusoidal embedding, 29.3, passed through an MLP and added or used to scale the features) and self-attention at the lower resolutions. Large recent models replace the U-Net with a transformer over patches, the **DiT** (Peebles and Xie, 2023), which scales more predictably. The lab uses an MLP, which is enough for 2D points and 8×8 digits.

## Why it replaced GANs

The lab reruns 31.2's comparisons:

- **Ring of 8 Gaussians**: all 8 modes, 98% of samples near a mode, on the first try, with no tuning of seeds. The GANs: (8, 0.68), (8, 0.89), (1, 0.38) for vanilla across three seeds; (8, 0.48) and (8, 0.43) for WGAN-GP.
- **Digits**: a classifier is 92% confident on diffusion samples (85% for the GAN, 97% for real digits), and every digit appears between 122 and 323 times in 2,000 samples, where the GAN made 21 twos and 35 sevens.

Nothing in diffusion training rewards dropping a mode: every training example is noised and reconstructed at every noise level with a regression loss, so a model that ignored some region of the data would pay for it directly. Add stable optimization (no adversary, no equilibrium to find) and a likelihood bound for free, and the only real disadvantage is sampling cost, which the previous section is chipping away at.

## Memorization

Diffusion models can reproduce training images nearly exactly, especially images duplicated in the training set: Carlini et al. (2023) extracted over a hundred near-copies of training images from Stable Diffusion by generating from their captions. That matters for privacy (medical images, faces), copyright, and evaluation (a model that memorizes scores well on FID). Deduplicate training data, check samples against nearest training neighbors, and treat a generative model trained on sensitive data as potentially leaking it (53.1 on extraction attacks, 54.2 on privacy).

## Things that will bite you

- **The linear schedule at a low budget**, or a schedule tuned for a different resolution.
- **Mismatched parameterization** between training and sampling ($\varepsilon$ vs $x_0$ vs $v$).
- **Off-by-one in $t$** (0- vs 1-indexed schedules): produces slightly wrong noise levels and quietly worse samples.
- **Clipping $\hat x_0$** (or not) to the data range: helps pixel-space models, wrong for latents.
- **Judging by a few samples** at the wrong number of steps.
- **Memorized training data** passing for generation.

5. What makes DDIM deterministic, and what does determinism make possible?
6. Give two reasons diffusion training doesn't suffer from mode collapse the way GANs do.

1. $x_1 = \sqrt{\alpha_1}x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_1}\varepsilon_1$, $x_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_2}x_1 + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_2}\varepsilon_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_1\alpha_2}x_0 + \sqrt{\alpha_2(1 - \alpha_1)}\varepsilon_1 + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_2}\varepsilon_2$. The two noise terms are independent Gaussians; their sum has variance $\alpha_2(1 - \alpha_1) + 1 - \alpha_2 = 1 - \alpha_1\alpha_2$. So $q(x_2 \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_2}x_0, (1 - \bar\alpha_2)I)$.

2. Signal variance is $\bar\alpha\,\operatorname{Var}(x_0)$; for unit-variance data the signal fraction is $\bar\alpha = 7.8\%$, noise 92.2%. Log SNR $= \log(0.078/0.922) \approx -2.47$.

3. Because $q(x_t \mid x_0)$ is available in closed form: for each training example, draw $t$ and $\varepsilon$ and compute $x_t$ in one line. There's no need to simulate the chain, and each training step can use a different $t$ per example, so all noise levels are trained in parallel.

4. $x_t$ is Gaussian with mean $\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\mu$ and variance $v = \bar\alpha s^2 + 1 - \bar\alpha$, so $\frac{d}{dx_t}\log p_t = -(x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\mu)/v$. $(x_t, \varepsilon)$ are jointly Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon, x_t) = \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha}$, so $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t] = \frac{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha}}{v}(x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\mu) = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha}\,\frac{d}{dx_t}\log p_t(x_t)$. The regression target's conditional mean is the scaled score, which is what the network learns; the same holds for any data distribution (Tweedie's formula).

5. DDIM's update sets the injected noise to zero: each step computes $\hat x_0$ and moves deterministically to the next noise level along the probability flow ODE. So a starting noise $x_T$ maps to exactly one sample. That allows few-step sampling (large steps along a smooth path), interpolation between samples through their latents, inversion (running the ODE forward from a real image to find its latent, for editing), and reproducibility.

6. The loss is a regression on every training example at every noise level: every region of the data contributes directly to the loss, and a model that fails on some region is penalized for it; there's no adversary rewarding the generator for concentrating on what fools it. And training is a single stable optimization rather than a two-player game whose alternating updates can cycle between modes. (Also: the learned score is used from pure noise, which covers the whole space, so sampling starts from everywhere.)
