# 31.4 Flow matching, latent diffusion and guidance

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 31.1 (autoencoders), 31.3 (diffusion, the probability flow ODE), 30.2 (CLIP text encoders), 29.1 (cross-attention). Lab: [`lab_31_4_flow_matching.py`](labs/lab_31_4_flow_matching.py).*

31.3 ended with a stochastic process that turns data into noise, its reverse, and a deterministic ODE with the same marginals. This lesson
takes the three steps that turn that into the image generators you've used. **Flow matching** goes straight to the ODE: pick a simple path
from noise to data and regress its velocity, a cleaner and now more common way to train the same kind of model. **Guidance** makes a
conditional model follow its condition harder, at a price you've seen in every oversaturated AI image. And **latent diffusion** runs the whole
thing inside an autoencoder's compressed space, which is what made it affordable. Stable Diffusion, Flux, and most current video models are
these three ideas plus a text encoder and an enormous dataset.

## Flow matching

Define a path from a noise sample $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ to a data sample $x_1$. The simplest is a straight line:

$$x_t = (1 - t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1, \qquad t \in [0, 1], \qquad \frac{dx_t}{dt} = x_1 - x_0.$$

Train a network $v_\theta(x, t)$ to predict that velocity at points along random paths:

$$L = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, x_0,\, x_1}\left\|v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0)\right\|^2.$$

To generate, draw $x_0$ from the noise and integrate $dx/dt = v_\theta(x, t)$ from $t = 0$ to 1 with any ODE solver (the lab uses Euler steps). That's **conditional flow matching** (Lipman et al., 2023) with straight paths, also called **rectified flow** (Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023); Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden's **stochastic interpolants** are the general framework.

Why it works: each training target is the velocity of *one* path, but at a given $(x, t)$ many paths pass through, and the regression learns their **average**, the marginal velocity field. Integrating that field transports the noise distribution exactly onto the data distribution (the continuity equation guarantees the marginals match). Diffusion's probability flow ODE is one such field, for a particular curved family of Gaussian paths; flow matching lets you choose the path, and straight lines are the obvious choice. With Gaussian paths the two views are nearly the same algorithm (flow matching's velocity is a reparameterization of diffusion's v-prediction), which is why the literature treats them together.

The lab trains a flow model with the same network and budget as 31.3's denoisers on the same ring of Gaussians:

| Euler steps | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 | 25 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| fraction of samples near a mode | 0.00 | 0.07 | 0.79 | 0.90 | 0.94 | 0.96 |

About the same as 31.3's DDIM at the same step counts (0.79, 0.91, 0.96 at 5, 10 and 25 steps). The advantages are in the simplicity (no noise schedule, no $\bar\alpha$ bookkeeping, one line of loss) and in what comes next.

## Straightening the paths

If every path is straight, why does one Euler step fail completely? Because the **learned** paths aren't straight. Noise and data samples are paired at random during training, so the straight training lines cross each other, and at a crossing the network learns the average of two directions. The resulting flow curves. The lab measures straightness (distance traveled divided by path length, 1 for a line): 0.66.

**Reflow** (Liu et al., 2023) fixes it by retraining on pairs that don't cross: sample noise $z$, run the trained model to get its output $\hat x_1$, and train a new model on the pairs $(z, \hat x_1)$. Those pairs are connected by the first model's own flow, which never crosses itself, so the straight lines between them don't cross either. The lab's reflow model has straightness 0.999, and one Euler step now puts 81% of samples near a mode, from 0%.
The cost is a second training run and inheriting the first model's errors. Related approaches pair noise and data more cleverly from the start (minibatch optimal transport couplings); distillation methods (31.3's consistency models) pursue the same goal. Few-step generation is where much of the current engineering effort goes, because each step of a large image model costs real money.

Flow matching with rectified-flow paths is used by Stable Diffusion 3, Flux and many recent video and audio models.

## Conditioning

A generator for a specific *something* takes the condition as an extra input: $v_\theta(x, t, c)$ or $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)$.

- **Class labels**: an embedding added to the time embedding (the lab).
- **Text**: a frozen text encoder (CLIP's, 30.2, or a T5 or LLM encoder for better prompt understanding) produces a sequence of token embeddings; the denoiser attends to them through **cross-attention** layers (29.1) in every block. That's text-to-image.
- **Images and structure**: a depth map, a pose skeleton, an edge map, via an added branch trained on top of a frozen model (**ControlNet**, Zhang et al., 2023), or an image prompt encoded like text.

## Guidance

Conditional models tend to follow their condition only loosely. **Classifier guidance** (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021) added the gradient of a classifier's $\log p(y \mid x_t)$ to the score, pushing samples toward what a classifier recognizes as class $y$, but it needs a classifier trained on noisy images.

**Classifier-free guidance** (CFG; Ho and Salimans, 2022) removes the classifier. Train *one* model that is both conditional and unconditional by replacing the condition with a "null" token some of the time (10% to 20%). At sampling:

$$v = v_\theta(x, t, \varnothing) + w\left(v_\theta(x, t, c) - v_\theta(x, t, \varnothing)\right).$$

$w = 0$ is the unconditional model, $w = 1$ the plain conditional model, and $w > 1$ extrapolates past it in the direction that distinguishes "with the condition" from "without". In score terms it samples from roughly $p(x)\,p(c \mid x)^w$: the condition's likelihood is sharpened. Text-to-image models typically use $w$ between 3 and 8, and it matters more than almost any other setting. It costs two network evaluations per step (conditional and unconditional). The unconditional branch can be replaced by a **negative prompt**, so the sampler steers away from it.

The lab's conditional digit model, with the judge classifier from 31.2 and 31.3:

| $w$ | on-label (judge) | pixels past the data range | distance to the nearest real digit of that class |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.09 | 0.14 | 2.06 |
| 1 | 0.995 | 0.14 | 1.07 |
| 2 | 1.000 | 0.20 | 0.96 |
| 4 | 0.999 | 0.32 | 1.09 |
| 6 | 0.995 | 0.42 | 1.33 |

On this easy task the plain conditional model is already on-label; moderate guidance makes samples a little more typical; strong guidance mostly pushes pixel values past the range of the data, and samples drift away from real digits again. That's the digit-sized version of the **oversaturated, over-contrasted, "burnt"** look of high-guidance images, and why Imagen introduced **dynamic thresholding** and others "CFG rescale". The other documented cost at scale, **lost diversity** (Ho and Salimans show FID and diversity worsening as $w$ grows while sample fidelity improves), is real but too subtle for this toy to show cleanly, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Guidance is a fidelity-versus-everything-else knob; tune it per model and per use.

## Latent diffusion

Running diffusion on 512×512×3 pixels means a denoiser applied 20 to 50 times to 786,432 numbers per image, most of which describe imperceptible texture. **Latent diffusion** (Rombach et al., 2022) splits the job:

1. Train an **autoencoder** once (31.1): encoder downsamples by 8 per side to a 64×64×4 latent (48 times fewer numbers), decoder reconstructs. Trained with a reconstruction loss, a **perceptual** loss (distance in a pretrained network's feature space) and an **adversarial** loss (31.2), with only a small KL term, so reconstructions are sharp and the latent is well-behaved.
2. Train the diffusion or flow model **in latent space**. It only has to model the semantic structure; the decoder handles perceptual detail.
3. Generate: sample a latent, decode.

Practical details: the latents are rescaled to roughly unit variance before diffusion (Stable Diffusion's magic constant 0.18215 is exactly that), and the autoencoder's quality caps the whole system's quality (small text and faces suffer at 8× downsampling; newer autoencoders use 16 channels instead of 4).

The lab does it at toy scale: an autoencoder compresses 64-pixel digits to 8 numbers, and a conditional flow model with half the width is trained on the standardized codes. With guidance 2, it's on-label 100% of the time and its samples are closer to real digits than the pixel model's (0.84 against 0.96). Some of that gap is the decoder, which produces smooth, clean strokes by construction; that's the point of the design, not a flaw in the comparison.

## The text-to-image pipeline

Put it together: a text encoder turns the prompt into embeddings; a U-Net or DiT denoiser (31.3) conditioned by cross-attention runs 20 to 50 guided steps of a diffusion or flow sampler in the latent space of an autoencoder; the decoder produces the image. Everything else is variations:

- **Image-to-image and editing**: **SDEdit** noises a real image partway (say to $t = 0.6$) and denoises it with a new prompt, keeping the layout; **inpainting** regenerates a masked region; **DDIM inversion** (31.3) finds a latent that reproduces an image, for precise edits.
- **Personalization**: DreamBooth, textual inversion and LoRA (38.2) adapt a model to a subject or style from a few images.
- **Video**: latents gain a time axis; attention runs across frames.

## Evaluation

FID (31.2) for realism and diversity, computed against a reference set; **CLIP score** (30.2) for prompt adherence, which inherits CLIP's weaknesses (it can't count or bind attributes, so it rewards images that are merely about the right things); and **human preference** studies or learned preference models, which are expensive and themselves biased. Test the specific things your use case needs (text rendering, hands, counts, spatial relations, your product), with prompts you wrote.

## Responsible use

Generated images are used for fraud, harassment, non-consensual imagery and disinformation (31.2), trained on data whose creators often didn't consent, and can reproduce training images (31.3). Mitigations you'll be expected to implement: training-data filtering and deduplication, output safety classifiers, **provenance** (C2PA content credentials) and **watermarking** at generation time (e.g., SynthID), rate limits and abuse monitoring, and legal review of training data licenses (54.3).

## Things that will bite you

- **One-step sampling from a model trained with random couplings.**
- **Guidance too high**: oversaturated, artifact-laden images judged as "high quality" by a metric that likes contrast.
- **Forgetting to train the unconditional branch** (no condition dropout): CFG then extrapolates from garbage.
- **Unscaled latents**: diffusion assumes roughly unit variance; SD's latents need the 0.18215 factor.
- **Mixing autoencoders**: a denoiser trained on one autoencoder's latents is useless with another's.
- **CLIP score as proof of prompt adherence.**

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $dx_t/dt = x_1 - x_0$: a straight line at constant speed, so the velocity is the same at every $t$ along one path. (The *learned* field $v_\theta(x, t)$ still depends on $t$, because which paths pass through a given point changes with $t$.)

2. At a point $(x, t)$, many training paths pass through, with different directions, because noise and data were paired at random and the lines cross. The regression's optimum is the average of their velocities (the conditional expectation). Following that averaged field gives paths that bend as the mix of directions changes; they're the marginal flow, which transports the distributions correctly but isn't straight. Reflow or better couplings reduce the crossings.

3. $v = v_u + w(v_c - v_u)$. $w = 0$: $v_u$, the unconditional model (ignores the condition). $w = 1$: $v_c$, the plain conditional model. $w = 3$: $v_c + 2(v_c - v_u)$, extrapolating past the conditional prediction in the direction the condition adds: more strongly conditioned, less diverse, and prone to saturation.

4. CFG needs the model's unconditional prediction $v_\theta(x, t, \varnothing)$ as well as the conditional one. Dropping the condition during training (replacing it with a learned null token) teaches the same network to model the unconditional distribution. Without it, the "unconditional" output for the null token is untrained, and the guidance direction $v_c - v_u$ is meaningless.

5. $512 \times 512 \times 3 = 786{,}432$ values vs $64 \times 64 \times 4 = 16{,}384$: a factor of 48 (and attention cost falls further, since sequence length drops 64 times). The main risk is that the autoencoder's reconstruction caps quality: fine details that it can't reconstruct (small text, fine textures, small faces, hands at low resolution) can't be generated, however good the denoiser.

6. Look at many samples across varied prompts, not a curated few: check for oversaturation, burnt highlights, artifacts, repetitive compositions and reduced diversity (several samples per prompt), and failures on prompts outside marketing's test set. Measure FID or diversity against a reference set and prompt adherence with a human check. Compare a few values (5, 7.5, 10, 15) blind with people who weren't in the original room. A per-use setting (a "vivid" preset) is likely better than changing the default for everyone.
