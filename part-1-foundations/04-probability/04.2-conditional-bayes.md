# 04.2 Joint, conditional and Bayes

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 04.1. Lab: [`lab_04_2_bayes.py`](labs/lab_04_2_bayes.py).*

Here's a story I've seen play out, with minor variations, at more than one organization I was hired to test. Someone deploys an
intrusion detection model. It catches 99% of real attacks and only raises a false alarm on 1% of normal traffic.
Everybody's happy. A week later, the SOC analysts are drowning in thousands of alerts a day, almost all of them false,
they start ignoring the alerts, and a real attack walks through untouched. Nobody changed the model. Nobody lied about
its accuracy. Everybody just forgot to multiply by the base rate.

That's Bayes' theorem, and it's the most practically important formula in this entire module. This lesson is about
reasoning with several random variables at once: joints, marginals, conditionals, independence, and how to update
beliefs with evidence without fooling yourself.

## Joint and marginal distributions

For two random variables $X$ and $Y$, the **joint distribution** $p(x, y)$ gives the probability (or density) of each
combination. For discrete variables, it's a table.

Example: 1,000 website sessions, by device and whether they converted.

| | converted | not converted | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| mobile | 30 | 570 | 600 |
| desktop | 40 | 360 | 400 |
| total | 70 | 930 | 1000 |

The joint probabilities are the cells divided by 1000: $p(\text{mobile}, \text{converted}) = 0.03$.

The **marginal** of one variable sums (or integrates) out the other:

$$p(x) = \sum_y p(x, y) \qquad \text{or} \qquad p(x) = \int p(x, y)\,dy$$

The margins of the table: $p(\text{converted}) = 0.07$, $p(\text{mobile}) = 0.6$. The name literally comes from writing the
sums in the margins.

## Conditional probability

The **conditional** distribution restricts attention to the cases where something is known:

$$p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)}$$

"Of the mobile sessions, what fraction converted?" $= 0.03 / 0.6 = 0.05$. For desktop: $0.04/0.4 = 0.10$. Conditioning is
zooming into a row of the table and renormalizing it so it sums to 1.

Rearranged, it's the **product rule** (or chain rule of probability):

$$p(x, y) = p(y \mid x)\,p(x) = p(x \mid y)\,p(y)$$

and for many variables:

$$p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = p(x_1)\,p(x_2 \mid x_1)\,p(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2)\cdots p(x_n \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1})$$

Look at that last formula carefully, because it's the mathematical definition of an autoregressive language model (35.2):
the probability of a sentence is the product of the probability of each token given all the previous ones. A GPT is a
neural network that outputs $p(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$. Nothing more, nothing less.

The **law of total probability** combines the product rule and marginalization:

$$p(y) = \sum_x p(y \mid x)\,p(x)$$

"The overall conversion rate is the average of the per-device rates, weighted by how common each device is":
$0.05 \cdot 0.6 + 0.10 \cdot 0.4 = 0.07$.

## Bayes' theorem

Since $p(x, y)$ can be written both ways, you can flip a conditional:

$$p(x \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid x)\,p(x)}{p(y)} = \frac{p(y \mid x)\,p(x)}{\sum_{x'} p(y \mid x')\,p(x')}$$

In the language of inference, with $H$ a hypothesis and $E$ some evidence:

$$\underbrace{p(H \mid E)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{p(E \mid H)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{p(H)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(E)}_{\text{evidence}}}$$

### The base rate fallacy, with numbers

The intrusion detector: true positive rate $p(\text{alert} \mid \text{attack}) = 0.99$, false positive rate
$p(\text{alert} \mid \text{normal}) = 0.01$. Attacks are rare: say 1 in 10,000 connections, $p(\text{attack}) = 10^{-4}$.

$$p(\text{attack} \mid \text{alert}) = \frac{0.99 \times 10^{-4}}{0.99 \times 10^{-4} + 0.01 \times 0.9999} \approx \frac{0.000099}{0.010098} \approx 0.0098$$

Less than 1%. **Ninety-nine alerts out of a hundred are false**, with a detector that everybody would call "99% accurate". At a
million connections per day, that's about 10,000 false alarms per day to find about 99 real attacks. The analysts will stop
reading alerts in a week, and they'll be making a rational decision.

The same math applies to the medical test in the module's self-check (1 in 10,000 prevalence, 99% accurate test: a positive
means about a 1% chance of disease), to fraud detection, to content moderation, to any rare-event detection. It's why 18.2 will
teach you precision and recall instead of accuracy, and why a detector's false positive rate must be judged against the base rate.
Stefan Axelsson wrote a classic paper about exactly this for intrusion detection back in 2000 ("The Base-Rate Fallacy and the
Difficulty of Intrusion Detection"). The field has been relearning it every few years since.

A clean way to do these computations in your head is **odds form**:

$$\underbrace{\frac{p(H \mid E)}{p(\neg H \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{p(E \mid H)}{p(E \mid \neg H)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{p(H)}{p(\neg H)}}_{\text{prior odds}}$$

Prior odds $1 : 9999$. Likelihood ratio $0.99 / 0.01 = 99$. Posterior odds $99 : 9999 \approx 1 : 101$. Done. Evidence multiplies odds by
its likelihood ratio, and independent pieces of evidence multiply their ratios. That's exactly how Naive Bayes classifiers
work (19.2), and it's why you add log-likelihood ratios when combining evidence.

## Independence

$X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if knowing one tells you nothing about the other:

$$p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad p(y \mid x) = p(y)$$

In the sessions table, device and conversion are *not* independent: 5% vs 10%.

Independence is a strong assumption, and it's assumed everywhere: "the training examples are i.i.d." (independent and
identically distributed) underlies almost every guarantee in ML. It fails whenever you have repeated measurements of the
same user, time series, or data collected in batches. When it fails, your cross-validation lies (18.1). Remember that.

**Conditional independence**: $X \perp Y \mid Z$ if $p(x, y \mid z) = p(x \mid z)\,p(y \mid z)$. Two things can be dependent but become
independent once you know a third. Shoe size and reading ability in children are correlated; given age, they're not. Age
explains both. Naive Bayes (19.2) assumes all features are conditionally independent given the class, which is almost never
true and works surprisingly often anyway.

## Simpson's paradox

Here's the one that makes executives angry. Two treatments for kidney stones, from a real 1986 study (Charig et al.,
analyzed in countless statistics courses since):

| | Treatment A | Treatment B |
|---|---|---|
| small stones | 81/87 = **93%** | 234/270 = 87% |
| large stones | 192/263 = **73%** | 55/80 = 69% |
| **all** | 273/350 = 78% | 289/350 = **83%** |

A is better for small stones *and* better for large stones, but B is better overall. No arithmetic trick: doctors gave A (the more
invasive treatment) mostly to the hard, large-stone cases, and large stones have lower success rates no matter what. Stone size
**confounds** the comparison.

In data science terms: an aggregate trend can reverse inside every subgroup. Which answer is right depends on the causal
structure, not on the numbers. Here, stone size affects both the choice of treatment and the outcome, so you should compare
within strata, and A is better. We'll formalize when to condition and when not to in 05.4. For now: before you report "B beats A"
from observational data, ask what drove the assignment.

## A first look at Bayesian updating

Bayes' rule also works on continuous unknowns. Say you want to know a conversion rate $\theta$. Start with a prior belief, a
Beta($\alpha, \beta$) distribution (04.1). Observe $k$ conversions in $n$ trials; the likelihood is Binomial, $\propto \theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}$.
Multiply prior and likelihood:

$$p(\theta \mid k) \propto \theta^{\alpha - 1}(1-\theta)^{\beta - 1} \cdot \theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k} = \theta^{\alpha + k - 1}(1-\theta)^{\beta + n - k - 1}$$

That's a Beta($\alpha + k$, $\beta + n - k$). The posterior is in the same family as the prior (the Beta is **conjugate** to the
Binomial), and the update is just "add successes to $\alpha$, failures to $\beta$". The prior acts like $\alpha$ pseudo-successes and $\beta$
pseudo-failures you'd seen before the data. With little data, the prior matters; with lots of data, the likelihood takes over.

This is the entire engine behind Bayesian A/B testing and behind Thompson sampling for bandits (46.1), which is how a lot of
real systems decide which variant of a page, an ad or a recommendation to show next.

## Graphical models, in one paragraph

When you have many variables, the product rule plus conditional independence lets you factor a huge joint into small pieces.
Draw a **directed acyclic graph** where each variable points from its "causes"; then
$p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid \text{parents}(x_i))$. Naive Bayes is a graph with the class pointing at every feature. Hidden
Markov models (34.2) are a chain. Causal DAGs (05.4) are the same drawings with a causal interpretation. You won't need the full
theory of graphical models in this course, but you'll see these drawings often, and now you can read them.

> **Scar tissue.** In a data mining course, my group presented a fraud classifier trained on a public dataset of card transactions: 99.8%
> accuracy, and a slide with the number in a very large font. The professor, a man who could derive anything on a blackboard and, as far
> as any of us could tell, had never compiled a line of code, asked one question: what's the fraud rate? About 0.17%. A model that
> answered "not fraud" to everything scored 99.83%, slightly better than ours. He didn't need a computer to take us apart. He needed
> Bayes' theorem and a base rate, and I've carried both into every evaluation since.

## Things that will bite you

- **Base rates.** Always ask: how common is the thing I'm detecting? Then compute precision, not just recall and FPR.
- **Confusing $p(A \mid B)$ with $p(B \mid A)$** (the "prosecutor's fallacy"). "The probability of this evidence if innocent is tiny"
  is not "the probability of innocence given the evidence is tiny".
- **Aggregated comparisons from observational data.** Simpson's paradox is not rare; it appears whenever group sizes and a
  confounder are correlated, which in business data is most of the time.
- **Assuming i.i.d.** when the data has users, sessions, time or batches. Your uncertainty estimates will be too small.
