# 04.1 Random variables and distributions

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: Module 02, 03.1. Lab: [`lab_04_1_distributions.py`](labs/lab_04_1_distributions.py).*

Every model in this course is a probabilistic statement, even the ones that pretend not to be. Linear regression
secretly assumes Gaussian noise. A classifier's "confidence" is a probability, whether it's calibrated or not (18.3). A
language model is literally a probability distribution over the next token, and sampling from it is how it writes. If
you can't speak probability fluently, you'll use these models the way a tourist uses a phrasebook: enough to order
food, not enough to notice you're being ripped off.

This lesson is the vocabulary: random variables, the distributions that keep showing up, and how to sample from them.
The next three lessons are the grammar.

## Probability spaces, briefly

A probability model has three ingredients:

- A **sample space** $\Omega$: all possible outcomes. Rolling a die: $\{1, \ldots, 6\}$. Measuring a temperature: $\mathbb{R}$.
- **Events**: subsets of $\Omega$ ("the roll is even" $= \{2, 4, 6\}$).
- A **probability measure** $P$ that assigns each event a number, satisfying Kolmogorov's axioms: $P(A) \ge 0$,
  $P(\Omega) = 1$, and for disjoint events, $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$ (and the same for countably many).

Everything else is derived from those three axioms. For example $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$, and $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$
(inclusion-exclusion: don't count the overlap twice).

I'm deliberately not going into measure theory. You need it to prove things about continuous spaces rigorously; you don't
need it to use probability correctly in engineering. If you ever want to go there, the further reading has you covered.

## Random variables

A **random variable** is a function that assigns a number to each outcome: $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$. The number of heads in
10 flips. A customer's spend next month. Whether an email is spam (0 or 1). We stop caring about $\Omega$ and work directly
with the **distribution** of $X$: which values it takes and how likely they are.

**Discrete** random variables take countably many values and have a **probability mass function** (PMF):
$p(x) = P(X = x)$, with $\sum_x p(x) = 1$.

**Continuous** random variables have a **probability density function** (PDF) $p(x)$ with

$$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\,dx = 1$$

A density is **not** a probability. $P(X = x) = 0$ for any single value of a continuous variable, and $p(x)$ can be larger
than 1 (a uniform distribution on $[0, 0.1]$ has density 10). Densities only become probabilities when you integrate them
over an interval. People mix this up all the time, including in production code that thresholds "likelihoods" as if they
were probabilities.

Both kinds have a **cumulative distribution function** (CDF), $F(x) = P(X \le x)$, which is non-decreasing from 0 to 1. For
continuous variables $p(x) = F'(x)$. The inverse of the CDF, the **quantile function** $F^{-1}(q)$, gives the value below which a
fraction $q$ of the mass lies: the median is $F^{-1}(0.5)$, and the p99 latency of your model server (49.1) is $F^{-1}(0.99)$
of the latency distribution.

## The distributions you'll actually meet

You don't need to memorize a hundred distributions. You need to know about a dozen really well, including *where they show
up* and *what assumption they encode*.

### Discrete

| Distribution | PMF | Mean, variance | What it models | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli($p$) | $p^x(1-p)^{1-x}$, $x \in \{0,1\}$ | $p$, $p(1-p)$ | one yes/no event | binary labels, click/no click, logistic regression (17.3) |
| Binomial($n, p$) | $\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}$ | $np$, $np(1-p)$ | number of successes in $n$ independent trials | conversions in an A/B test (05.3) |
| Categorical($\pi$) | $\pi_k$ for outcome $k$ | | one of $K$ outcomes | multiclass labels, the next token of an LLM |
| Multinomial | | | counts of $K$ outcomes in $n$ trials | bag-of-words (34.2) |
| Geometric($p$) | $(1-p)^{x-1}p$ | $1/p$, $(1-p)/p^2$ | trials until the first success | retries until a request succeeds |
| Poisson($\lambda$) | $\frac{\lambda^x e^{-\lambda}}{x!}$ | $\lambda$, $\lambda$ | number of rare events in a fixed window | requests per second, errors per day, defects (count GLMs, 17.3) |

The Poisson has mean equal to variance. Real count data usually has *more* variance than that ("overdispersion"): the
number of purchases per customer is much more variable than Poisson predicts, because customers differ. When you fit a
Poisson model and the residuals are too spread out, that's what's going on, and the negative binomial is the usual fix.

### Continuous

| Distribution | PDF | Mean, variance | What it models | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uniform($a, b$) | $\frac{1}{b-a}$ on $[a,b]$ | $\frac{a+b}{2}$, $\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}$ | "no preference" in a range | random search (22.2), initialization |
| Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$ | $\mu$, $\sigma^2$ | sums of many small effects (CLT, 04.3) | noise models, initialization, VAEs, diffusion |
| Exponential($\lambda$) | $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, $x \ge 0$ | $1/\lambda$, $1/\lambda^2$ | waiting time between Poisson events | time between requests, survival |
| Beta($\alpha, \beta$) | $\propto x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}$ on $[0,1]$ | $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$ | uncertainty about a probability | Bayesian A/B tests, Thompson sampling (46.1) |
| Gamma($k, \theta$) | $\propto x^{k-1}e^{-x/\theta}$ | $k\theta$, $k\theta^2$ | positive skewed quantities | priors on rates and precisions |
| Laplace($\mu, b$) | $\frac{1}{2b}e^{-\lvert x-\mu\rvert/b}$ | $\mu$, $2b^2$ | like Gaussian, heavier tails | $L_1$ loss and lasso (17.2), differential privacy (54.2) |
| Student-t($\nu$) | heavier tails than Gaussian | | Gaussian with uncertain variance | t-tests (05.2), robust regression |
| Log-normal | $\log X$ is Gaussian | | products of many positive effects | incomes, prices, latencies, file sizes |

Two remarks that will save you pain:

- **Heavy tails are the norm in real data**, not the exception. Latencies, incomes, transaction amounts, word frequencies
  (Zipf's law), the number of followers: almost anything produced by a "rich get richer" or multiplicative process is
  log-normal or power-law distributed. The Gaussian's tails fall off so fast that a 6-sigma event "should" happen once in
  about half a billion samples. In real log data, it happens before lunch. When someone assumes Gaussian noise on latency
  data, their p99 estimates are fiction.
- **The exponential is memoryless**: $P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)$. If time-to-failure were exponential, a machine that has
  run for 10 years would be as good as new. Real hardware isn't like that (it wears out), which is why reliability engineers use
  the Weibull instead. Know what assumption you're buying.

## The multivariate Gaussian

The most important distribution in ML, by far. For $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$ (symmetric positive
definite):

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\det(\Sigma)^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)\right)$$

Read it with the geometry from 02.4. The exponent is a quadratic form, $(x - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)$, which is the squared
**Mahalanobis distance** from $x$ to $\mu$. Its level sets (constant density) are ellipsoids centered at $\mu$, with axes along the
eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ and semi-axis lengths proportional to $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. A Gaussian is an ellipsoid-shaped cloud; the covariance
matrix *is* the ellipsoid. The determinant in the normalization is the volume factor from 02.2.

Properties that make it everyone's favorite:

- **Linear maps keep it Gaussian**: if $x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$ then $Ax + b \sim \mathcal{N}(A\mu + b, A\Sigma A^\top)$.
- **Marginals and conditionals are Gaussian** (and have closed forms). This is what makes Gaussian processes (23.4) and
  Kalman filters work.
- **Uncorrelated implies independent** for jointly Gaussian variables. For other distributions, it doesn't (04.3).

**Sampling** uses the first property. Factor $\Sigma = LL^\top$ with the Cholesky decomposition, draw standard normals
$z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, and return $\mu + Lz$. Its covariance is $L I L^\top = \Sigma$. That's how every library does it. The
diffusion models of 31.3 and the VAE reparameterization trick (31.1) are this exact formula, with $\mu$ and $L$ produced by a
neural network.

## Sampling from anything: the inverse CDF trick

If $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ and $F$ is a continuous CDF, then $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$. Proof: $P(X \le x) = P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) =
P(U \le F(x)) = F(x)$.

So to sample an exponential, compute $-\ln(1 - U)/\lambda$. For discrete distributions, the same idea: compute the cumulative
sums of the PMF and find where $U$ falls (`np.searchsorted`). That's how `np.random.choice` with probabilities works, and it's
how an LLM picks the next token after computing its softmax (37.1).

When $F^{-1}$ has no closed form, there are other methods (rejection sampling, MCMC). You'll meet MCMC briefly in 23.4.

## Change of variables

If $Y = g(X)$ with $g$ invertible and differentiable, densities don't just get relabeled, they get stretched:

$$p_Y(y) = p_X(g^{-1}(y))\,\left|\frac{d\,g^{-1}(y)}{dy}\right|$$

In $d$ dimensions, the factor becomes the absolute determinant of the Jacobian (03.2), which is the local volume stretch
(02.2). This formula is the entire foundation of **normalizing flows** (31.4), which build complex densities by pushing a
Gaussian through a sequence of invertible neural networks while keeping track of the Jacobian determinants.

## Things that will bite you

- **Treating densities as probabilities.** A likelihood of 3.7 is perfectly valid. A probability of 3.7 is a bug.
- **Assuming Gaussian everything.** Plot the histogram (on a log scale if it's positive and skewed) before you choose a model.
- **Log-normal data summarized by the mean.** The mean of a heavy-tailed distribution is dominated by a few huge values.
  Report medians and quantiles for latencies, incomes and prices.
- **Covariance matrices that aren't positive definite** (from rounding, missing data, or pairwise estimates). Cholesky fails.
  Add a small jitter to the diagonal or fix the estimation.

## Exercises

1. A uniform distribution on $[0, 0.25]$: what's its density? What's $P(X \le 0.1)$? Is anything wrong with a density of 4?
2. Requests arrive at a Poisson rate of 3 per second. What's the probability of seeing zero requests in a given second? At
   least 5?
3. Show that if $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ then $-\ln(U)/\lambda$ is exponential with rate $\lambda$.
4. For $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, describe the contours of the density. Which point is
   "farther" from the mean in Mahalanobis distance, $(2, 0)$ or $(0, 2)$?
5. If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, what is the distribution of $Y = e^X$? Derive its density with the change of variables formula.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Density $1/0.25 = 4$ on the interval. $P(X \le 0.1) = 0.1 \times 4 = 0.4$. Nothing wrong: densities can exceed 1; only their
   integral must be 1.

2. $P(0) = e^{-3} \approx 0.050$. $P(\ge 5) = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{4} \frac{3^k e^{-3}}{k!} = 1 - e^{-3}(1 + 3 + 4.5 + 4.5 + 3.375) \approx 1 - 0.815 = 0.185$.

3. $P(-\ln U/\lambda \le x) = P(\ln U \ge -\lambda x) = P(U \ge e^{-\lambda x}) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, which is the exponential CDF. (Using $U$
   instead of $1 - U$ is fine because both are uniform.)

4. Ellipses elongated along the x-axis, with semi-axes in ratio 2:1 (standard deviations 2 and 1). Mahalanobis distance:
   $(2,0)$ gives $\sqrt{4/4} = 1$, $(0, 2)$ gives $\sqrt{4/1} = 2$. So $(0, 2)$ is farther, even though both are at Euclidean distance 2:
   the distribution is much more spread out horizontally, so moving 2 units horizontally is less "surprising".

5. $g^{-1}(y) = \ln y$, derivative $1/y$. So $p_Y(y) = \frac{1}{y\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\ln y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ for $y > 0$. That's
   the log-normal density. Its median is $e^\mu$ and its mean is $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$, larger than the median: the heavy right tail
   pulls the mean up.
