# 04.4 Maximum likelihood and MAP

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 04.1 to 04.3, 03.1. Lab: [`lab_04_4_mle_map.py`](labs/lab_04_4_mle_map.py).*

Here's something that took me embarrassingly long to realize: almost every loss function you'll ever use was not
invented, it was *derived*. Mean squared error, cross-entropy, the loss of a Poisson regression, the loss of a language
model, even the $L_2$ penalty you add "to prevent overfitting", all fall out of one principle, applied to different
assumptions about the data. When somebody picks a loss because "that's what the tutorial used", they've made a
statistical assumption without knowing it. This lesson makes the assumption visible.

The principle is maximum likelihood. Its Bayesian cousin, MAP, explains regularization. Together they are the bridge
between probability and machine learning.

## The likelihood

You have data $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, assumed i.i.d. from a distribution $p(x \mid \theta)$ with unknown parameters $\theta$. The
**likelihood** is the probability (or density) of the observed data, viewed as a function of $\theta$:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta)$$

It's the same formula as the joint probability, read differently: data fixed, parameters varying. It is **not** a probability
distribution over $\theta$ (it doesn't integrate to 1 over $\theta$). It measures how well each candidate $\theta$ explains what you saw.

The **maximum likelihood estimate** (MLE) picks the $\theta$ that makes the data most probable:

$$\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \prod_i p(x_i \mid \theta) = \arg\max_\theta \sum_i \log p(x_i \mid \theta)$$

We always work with the **log-likelihood**: it turns products into sums (easier derivatives, and averages that the LLN applies to),
it's numerically sane (a product of a million probabilities underflows to 0.0 in float64; a sum of logs doesn't), and since log is
increasing, the maximizer is the same. In ML we minimize instead of maximize, so we use the **negative log-likelihood** (NLL):

$$\hat\theta = \arg\min_\theta\; -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x_i \mid \theta)$$

That's a loss function. Averaged over the data, minimized by gradient descent. Every model you'll train in Parts III and IV fits
this template.

## Worked MLEs

**Bernoulli** (coin flips, clicks): $k$ successes in $n$ trials. $\log\mathcal{L}(p) = k\log p + (n-k)\log(1-p)$. Derivative:
$k/p - (n-k)/(1-p) = 0 \Rightarrow \hat{p} = k/n$. The sample proportion. Reassuring.

**Gaussian**: $\log\mathcal{L}(\mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2$. Setting derivatives to zero:

$$\hat\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2$$

Notice the $1/n$, not $1/(n-1)$. The MLE of the variance is **biased**: $\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$. It underestimates, because
$\hat\mu$ was fitted to the same data and sits closer to the points than the true mean. (Bessel's correction from 01.3, now explained.)
MLEs are not always unbiased. They're usually *consistent* (right in the limit), which is a different property.

**Poisson** (counts): $\hat\lambda = \bar{x}$, the sample mean. Exercise 1.

## Your loss functions are likelihoods

Now the important part. Take a regression model $f_\theta(x)$ and assume the target is the prediction plus noise, $y = f_\theta(x) + \epsilon$.

**Gaussian noise**, $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$:

$$-\log p(y \mid x, \theta) = \frac{(y - f_\theta(x))^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const}$$

Minimizing the NLL is minimizing the **mean squared error**. Least squares (02.3) is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise. That's
the third derivation of the normal equations, promised in 02.3.

**Laplace noise**, $\epsilon \sim \text{Laplace}(0, b)$: the NLL is $|y - f_\theta(x)|/b + \text{const}$, the **mean absolute error**. Laplace has
heavier tails than the Gaussian, so it "expects" outliers and doesn't panic about them, which is why MAE is more robust. And the
minimizer of MAE for a constant prediction is the *median*, while for MSE it's the *mean*.

**Bernoulli labels** with $p = \sigma(f_\theta(x))$ (a classifier outputting a probability):

$$-\log p(y \mid x, \theta) = -[y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)]$$

That's **binary cross-entropy**, a.k.a. log loss. Logistic regression (17.3) is MLE for a Bernoulli model with a linear logit.

**Categorical labels** with $p = \operatorname{softmax}(f_\theta(x))$: the NLL is $-\log p_y$, the **cross-entropy** loss. Every neural classifier
and every language model (predicting a categorical distribution over the vocabulary) is trained with this.

**Poisson counts** with rate $\lambda = e^{f_\theta(x)}$: the NLL is $\lambda - y\log\lambda$, the **Poisson deviance**. Use it for counts
(orders per day, clicks per hour) instead of MSE.

So choosing a loss *is* choosing a noise model. Predicting counts with MSE says "I believe the count is Gaussian around my
prediction, with the same variance whether I predict 2 or 2,000". That's usually wrong, and the fit will be dominated by the large
counts. When your loss and your data disagree, the model doesn't complain. It just gives you worse answers.

## Properties of the MLE

Under regularity conditions (smooth model, true parameter in the interior, identifiable model), as $n \to \infty$:

- **Consistent**: $\hat\theta \to \theta_{\text{true}}$.
- **Asymptotically normal**: $\hat\theta \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\theta_{\text{true}}, \frac{1}{n}I(\theta)^{-1}\right)$, where $I(\theta)$ is the **Fisher
  information**, the expected curvature (Hessian, 03.2) of the NLL per observation. Sharp peak in the likelihood (high curvature)
  means precise estimates; flat likelihood means uncertain estimates. This gives you standard errors for free: invert the Hessian of the
  NLL at the optimum. That's how statsmodels reports standard errors for regression coefficients (05.4, 17.3).
- **Efficient**: asymptotically, no unbiased estimator has lower variance (the Cramér-Rao bound).
- **Invariant**: the MLE of $g(\theta)$ is $g(\hat\theta)$. The MLE of the standard deviation is the square root of the MLE of the variance.

These are asymptotic results. With small data, they can be quite wrong, and that's where MLE's main weakness appears.

## Where MLE fails: overconfidence with little data

Flip a coin 3 times, get 3 heads. The MLE says $\hat{p} = 1$: tails is *impossible*. A language model trained by MLE on a corpus where
a word never followed another assigns that bigram probability zero, and any sentence containing it gets probability zero (and
infinite perplexity, 34.1). A spam classifier that never saw the word "crypto" in a ham email concludes that "crypto" means spam
with certainty, and one such word overrides everything else.

MLE trusts the data completely, including its accidents. With lots of data per parameter, that's fine. With few (small datasets,
rare events, or models with huge numbers of parameters), it overfits.

## MAP: adding a prior

The Bayesian fix (04.2): put a **prior** $p(\theta)$ on the parameters and use Bayes' rule:

$$p(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$$

The **maximum a posteriori** (MAP) estimate is the mode of the posterior:

$$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_\theta \left[-\sum_i\log p(x_i \mid \theta) - \log p(\theta)\right]$$

Same as MLE, plus a term that depends only on $\theta$. That term is a **regularizer**. Look at what common priors give you:

- **Gaussian prior** $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2 I)$: $-\log p(\theta) = \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\theta\|_2^2 + \text{const}$. That's **$L_2$ regularization** (ridge,
  weight decay), with strength $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$ for a Gaussian likelihood. A narrow prior (small $\tau$) means strong regularization.
- **Laplace prior**: $-\log p(\theta) \propto \|\theta\|_1$. That's **$L_1$ regularization** (lasso), and the Laplace's sharp peak at zero is why
  it produces exact zeros (17.2).
- **Beta prior** on a Bernoulli probability: MAP with Beta($\alpha, \beta$) gives $\hat{p} = \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{n + \alpha + \beta - 2}$. With $\alpha = \beta = 2$:
  $\hat{p} = \frac{k+1}{n+2}$. Three heads in three flips now gives $4/5$, not 1. That's **Laplace smoothing** ("add-one smoothing"), used in
  Naive Bayes and n-gram models (19.2, 34.1).

So regularization isn't a hack to "prevent overfitting". It's a prior belief, written as a penalty: "I think the weights are
probably small", "I think most features are irrelevant", "I think the probability isn't exactly 0 or 1". Making that explicit helps
you choose it sensibly.

## MLE, MAP and full Bayes

Three levels of commitment:

1. **MLE**: one point estimate, no prior. Simple, overconfident with little data.
2. **MAP**: one point estimate, with a prior. Regularized. Still no uncertainty. (And not invariant to reparameterization, a subtle
   issue I'll leave to the further reading.)
3. **Full Bayesian inference**: keep the whole posterior $p(\theta \mid \text{data})$ and average predictions over it. Honest uncertainty, but
   usually expensive (integrals over millions of parameters). You'll do it exactly for conjugate models and Gaussian processes (23.4),
   and approximately elsewhere.

In practice, deep learning mostly does MAP (loss + weight decay), and gets uncertainty, when it needs it, from ensembles or
approximate methods. Classical statistics and small-data problems (A/B tests, medicine, insurance) lean more Bayesian.

## Fitting MLE numerically

Most models don't have closed-form MLEs. Write the NLL, compute its gradient (or let autodiff do it), and minimize with gradient
descent or L-BFGS. That's literally what `LogisticRegression.fit` does. Two practical tips:

- **Parameterize constraints away** (03.4): optimize $\log\sigma$ instead of $\sigma$, logits instead of probabilities.
- **Use numerically stable NLL formulas**: `log(sigmoid(z))` computed naively fails for large negative $z$; use `logaddexp` or the
  framework's fused `binary_cross_entropy_with_logits`. Same for softmax + cross-entropy (07.2).

## Things that will bite you

- **A loss that doesn't match the data's noise**: MSE on counts, MSE on heavy-tailed targets, MSE on probabilities.
- **MLE on tiny data**: zero probabilities, extreme estimates. Smooth or regularize.
- **Reading the likelihood as a probability of the parameters.** $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = 0.8$ doesn't mean "80% chance $\theta$ is right".
- **Biased variance estimates** in small samples, and forgetting that the MLE standard errors are asymptotic.

   median.
5. You observe 0 failures in 50 runs of a system. What's the MLE of the failure probability? What does a Beta(1, 1) prior give (posterior
   mean)? What's the "rule of three" upper bound on the failure rate with 95% confidence, and where does it come from?
