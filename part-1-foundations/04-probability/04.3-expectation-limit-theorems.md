# 04.3 Expectation, variance and the limit theorems

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 04.1, 04.2. Lab: [`lab_04_3_limit_theorems.py`](labs/lab_04_3_limit_theorems.py).*

Why does averaging work? Why does a model evaluated on 10,000 test examples give a number you can (sort of) trust,
while one evaluated on 50 doesn't? Why does a mini-batch gradient point in roughly the right direction? Why does every
statistical test in the next module end up looking at a Gaussian? The answers are two theorems from the 18th and
19th centuries, the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem. They are the reason data science works at all.
And, as usual, the interesting part is when they *don't* apply.

## Expectation

The **expected value** is the probability-weighted average:

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x) \qquad \text{or} \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\,p(x)\,dx$$

and for a function of $X$ (the "law of the unconscious statistician", a name I didn't invent):

$$\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\,p(x)$$

The most useful property in all of probability is **linearity**:

$$\mathbb{E}[aX + bY + c] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] + c$$

It holds **always**, whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent or not. This is what makes so many derivations short. The expected
number of fixed points in a random permutation of $n$ items? Define $X_i = 1$ if item $i$ stays in place; $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = 1/n$; by
linearity the expected total is $n \cdot 1/n = 1$, for any $n$, without ever thinking about the horrible dependence between the
$X_i$'s. The unbiasedness of the mini-batch gradient in 03.3 was the same argument.

What is **not** linear: $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] \ne g(\mathbb{E}[X])$ in general, and $\mathbb{E}[XY] \ne \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ unless $X$ and $Y$ are
uncorrelated.

### Jensen's inequality

For a **convex** function $g$ (03.3):

$$g(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[g(X)]$$

(reversed for concave functions like $\log$). Geometrically: the chord of a convex function lies above it. Consequences you'll use:

- $\mathbb{E}[X^2] \ge \mathbb{E}[X]^2$, so variance is non-negative.
- $\log\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log X]$. This inequality is how the **ELBO** is derived in variational inference and VAEs (31.1),
  and how you prove that KL divergence is non-negative (06.1). Two of the most important derivations in generative modeling are
  one application of Jensen each.
- Averaging predictions then applying a nonlinearity is not the same as applying it then averaging. When you average
  probabilities from an ensemble vs averaging logits, you get different answers, and Jensen tells you in which direction.

## Variance, covariance, correlation

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2$$

The standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$ has the same units as $X$. Scaling: $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}X)(Y - \mathbb{E}Y)] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}X\,\mathbb{E}Y$$

$$\rho(X, Y) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y} \in [-1, 1]$$

Correlation is a normalized covariance (it's a cosine similarity between centered variables, 02.1, which is why Cauchy-Schwarz
bounds it by 1).

The variance of a sum:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$$

If they're independent (or just uncorrelated), the covariance term vanishes and **variances add**. Standard deviations don't.
In vector form, for a random vector $x$ with covariance matrix $\Sigma$, $\operatorname{Var}(a^\top x) = a^\top\Sigma a$, and more generally
$\operatorname{Cov}(Ax) = A\Sigma A^\top$ (you used this in 04.1). A portfolio's risk, the variance of a model ensemble, and the uncertainty of a
linear model's prediction are all this formula.

### Correlation is not dependence

Two warnings:

1. **Zero correlation does not mean independent.** Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$. $Y$ is completely determined by $X$, and
   $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0$. Correlation only measures *linear* association. For nonlinear dependence, use mutual
   information (06.2) or just plot the data.
2. **Correlation doesn't mean causation.** You know this. Everybody knows this. Then they ship a feature importance plot to
   management and forget it. Module 05.4.

## Inequalities that bound the tails

Sometimes you know only the mean or the variance and still want to say something about extreme values:

- **Markov**: for $X \ge 0$, $P(X \ge a) \le \mathbb{E}[X] / a$.
- **Chebyshev**: $P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2$. At least 75% of any distribution with finite variance is within 2 standard
  deviations of the mean, 89% within 3. Compare with the Gaussian's 95% and 99.7%: Chebyshev is the worst case over all
  distributions, and it's loose, but it never lies.
- **Hoeffding** (for bounded i.i.d. variables in $[a, b]$): the sample mean deviates from the true mean by more than $t$ with
  probability at most $2\exp(-2nt^2/(b-a)^2)$. Exponentially small in $n$. This is the inequality behind most learning theory
  bounds (16.3), and a very practical tool: it tells you how many test examples you need to estimate an accuracy to within $\pm t$
  with high confidence, without assuming anything about the distribution.

## The law of large numbers

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mu$. The **sample mean** $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i$ converges to $\mu$ as $n \to \infty$.

Why, in two lines: $\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_n] = \mu$ (linearity) and, if the variance $\sigma^2$ is finite,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

(variances of independent variables add). The spread of the average shrinks like $1/n$, so by Chebyshev it concentrates on $\mu$.

The **standard error** of the mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Memorize the square root, because it has brutal practical consequences: **to
halve your uncertainty you need four times the data**. To get one more decimal digit of precision, a hundred times the data. That's
why A/B tests on small effects need enormous samples (05.3), why Monte Carlo methods converge slowly, and why doubling your test
set from 5,000 to 10,000 examples only shrinks the error bars on your accuracy by about 30%.

**Monte Carlo estimation** is the LLN used as an algorithm: to compute $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$, sample $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ and average $g(X_i)$. It
works in any dimension, with error $\propto 1/\sqrt{n}$ regardless of dimension, which is why it's the method of choice for
high-dimensional integrals: the ELBO in VAEs, policy gradients in RL (46.5), expected losses in general. The price is noise, and a
lot of research is about reducing its variance (control variates, baselines in REINFORCE, importance sampling).

## The central limit theorem

The LLN says the sample mean converges to $\mu$. The **CLT** says what the *fluctuations* look like on the way: for i.i.d. variables
with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$,

$$\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Whatever the original distribution (skewed, discrete, bimodal, ugly), the average of enough independent copies is approximately
Gaussian. That's why the Gaussian is everywhere: many real-world quantities are sums of many small independent effects. And it's why
confidence intervals and tests for means and proportions (05.1, 05.2) can be based on the normal distribution even when the data
isn't normal.

How big is "enough"? For roughly symmetric distributions, a few dozen. For heavily skewed ones (exponential, log-normal, revenue per
user), hundreds or thousands. Always check by simulation or bootstrap (05.1) when in doubt.

### When the limit theorems fail

Both theorems need assumptions, and real data violates them regularly:

1. **Infinite variance.** The **Cauchy distribution** (a ratio of two standard normals, or a Student-t with 1 degree of freedom) has no
   mean and no variance. The average of a million Cauchy samples is *exactly as spread out* as a single one. No convergence, ever. Less
   extreme power laws (Pareto with tail index between 1 and 2) have a mean but infinite variance: the LLN holds, but painfully slowly,
   and the CLT doesn't. Some real quantities (file sizes, city populations, losses in some financial and insurance data) are close to this
   regime. The lab makes you watch it.
2. **Dependence.** If samples are correlated (time series, multiple rows per user, clustered data), the effective sample size is smaller
   than $n$, and $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ underestimates your uncertainty, sometimes by a lot. If 100 test examples come from 5 users, you don't have
   100 independent data points. You have something closer to 5.
3. **Non-identical distributions** (drift): the average of a moving target converges to nothing in particular (51.1).

## Things that will bite you

- **$1/\sqrt{n}$**: plan sample sizes with it. A "significant" 0.2% improvement measured on 1,000 examples is noise.
- **Heavy tails**: the mean revenue per user in an A/B test can be moved by a single whale. Use robust statistics, trimming or
  transformations, and check the tails before trusting a CLT-based test.
- **Correlated samples**: compute uncertainty at the level of the independent unit (user, session, day), not the row.
- **Jensen in disguise**: $\mathbb{E}[\text{metric}(\text{model})]$ over seeds vs $\text{metric}(\mathbb{E}[\text{model}])$; average of ratios vs ratio of averages.
  They're different numbers.

## Exercises

1. You roll two dice. Use linearity to compute the expected sum, and the variance formula to compute its variance.
2. Show that $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2$ from the definition.
3. A classifier has true accuracy 90%. How many i.i.d. test examples do you need so that the standard error of the measured accuracy is
   below 0.5 percentage points? Use Hoeffding to find $n$ such that the measured accuracy is within 1 point of the truth with
   probability at least 99%.
4. Let $X \sim \text{Uniform}(-1, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$. Compute $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$. Are they independent?
5. You average the predicted probabilities of 5 models vs you average their logits and then apply the sigmoid. For a positive example
   where all models are fairly confident, which average is larger? (Use Jensen; the sigmoid is concave for positive inputs.)
