# 05.4 Causal inference for engineers

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 05.1 to 05.3, 04.2. Lab: [`lab_05_4_causal.py`](labs/lab_05_4_causal.py).*

Sooner or later someone will show you a model with a feature importance chart and say "look, customers who use the mobile
app churn 40% less, so let's push everyone to the app". And you'll have to explain, politely, that the app doesn't
necessarily cause anything: maybe the loyal customers are the ones who bothered to install it. Pushing disengaged
customers to install an app they don't want might do nothing, or make things worse.

Machine learning is excellent at prediction: given what I see, what will happen? Business decisions usually need a different
answer: if I *do* something, what will happen? That second question is causal, and a predictive model, however accurate,
doesn't answer it on its own. A/B tests (05.3) answer it by construction. This lesson is about what you can and can't
conclude when you can't randomize, which is most of the time.

## Potential outcomes

The cleanest way to think about causality: every unit $i$ (a customer, a patient) has two **potential outcomes**:
$Y_i(1)$ if treated and $Y_i(0)$ if not. The **individual treatment effect** is $Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$.

The fundamental problem of causal inference: you only ever observe one of the two. The customer either got the discount or didn't. The
other outcome is a **counterfactual**. So individual effects are unobservable, and we settle for averages: the **average treatment effect**

$$\text{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]$$

The naive estimate compares treated and untreated groups: $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = 0]$. Add and subtract $\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T = 1]$ and it
splits into

$$\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T = 1]}_{\text{effect on the treated}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T = 0]}_{\text{selection bias}}$$

The selection bias term asks: would the treated group have had different outcomes *even without the treatment*? For app users, yes:
loyal customers would churn less anyway. Randomization kills this term, because a coin flip makes the groups identical in expectation in
everything, including their $Y(0)$. Without randomization, you need assumptions to kill it. The rest of this lesson is about which
assumptions and how.

## Causal graphs

Draw variables as nodes and causal influences as arrows (a DAG, 04.2). Three elementary structures explain almost every confusion in
observational data:

**Fork (confounder)**: $T \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y$. $Z$ causes both treatment and outcome. Customer loyalty $\rightarrow$ app installation, loyalty $\rightarrow$
lower churn. $T$ and $Y$ are correlated with no causal link between them. **Condition on $Z$** (compare within levels of loyalty) to remove the
spurious association. The kidney stones of 04.2 were a fork: stone size affected both treatment choice and success.

**Chain (mediator)**: $T \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$. A discount $\rightarrow$ more items in cart $\rightarrow$ more revenue. $M$ is *how* the effect works. **Don't condition on $M$** if you
want the total effect: comparing customers with the same cart size removes the very mechanism you're measuring.

**Collider**: $T \rightarrow C \leftarrow Y$. Two things both cause $C$. $T$ and $Y$ are independent, but **conditioning on $C$ creates a spurious association**. The
classic: among hospitalized patients (hospitalization caused by disease A *or* disease B), A and B look negatively correlated, because if you're
in the hospital and don't have A, you probably have B (Berkson's paradox). Tech version: among startups that got funding (because of a great
product *or* a great network), product quality and network look negatively correlated. Or: you analyze only users who filed a support ticket,
and discover that "users with more features enabled are angrier". Selecting your dataset on an outcome is conditioning on a collider, and it
manufactures correlations. This one bites data scientists constantly, because "just filter to the relevant users" feels so innocent.

The practical rule that summarizes this, the **backdoor criterion** (Pearl): to estimate the effect of $T$ on $Y$, adjust for a set of variables
that blocks every non-causal path from $T$ to $Y$ (paths with an arrow *into* $T$), without including mediators or colliders (or descendants of
$T$). In words: control for common causes, don't control for consequences.

That means "throw every available variable into the regression as a control" is **wrong**. More controls isn't safer. It's a good way to
condition on a mediator or a collider and get a precisely estimated wrong answer.

## Estimating effects when you've measured the confounders

The key assumption is **no unmeasured confounding** (a.k.a. ignorability, or "selection on observables"): given the covariates $X$, treatment
assignment is as good as random. It's untestable from the data. Everything below is only as good as that assumption, which is why domain
knowledge matters more than method choice.

You also need **overlap** (positivity): for every covariate profile, some units were treated and some weren't. If all high-loyalty customers
installed the app, you have no data about high-loyalty customers without the app, and no method can conjure it.

### Regression adjustment

Regress $Y$ on $T$ and $X$: $Y = \alpha + \tau T + \beta^\top X + \epsilon$. Under a correct linear model and no unmeasured confounding, $\hat\tau$ estimates the
effect. CUPED (05.3) was this with a pre-period covariate. Flexible versions replace the linear model with ML models (S-learners, T-learners).

### Propensity scores and inverse probability weighting

The **propensity score** $e(x) = P(T = 1 \mid X = x)$ is the probability of treatment given covariates. Rosenbaum and Rubin showed (1983) that if
adjusting for $X$ is enough, adjusting for the one-dimensional $e(X)$ is also enough. Estimate it with logistic regression or any classifier,
then weight each unit by the inverse of the probability of the treatment it actually got:

$$\widehat{\text{ATE}}_{\text{IPW}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left(\frac{T_i Y_i}{\hat{e}(X_i)} - \frac{(1 - T_i) Y_i}{1 - \hat{e}(X_i)}\right)$$

Intuition: a treated customer who was unlikely to be treated (low $e$) is rare and informative, so they count more; they stand in for all the
similar customers who weren't treated. It's the same trick as correcting a biased survey by reweighting respondents. Watch out for extreme
weights when $\hat{e}$ is near 0 or 1 (poor overlap): they make the estimate explode in variance. Clip or trim, and look at the propensity
distribution before trusting anything.

**Doubly robust** estimators (AIPW) combine a regression model and a propensity model, and are consistent if *either* is correct. Modern
"double machine learning" (Chernozhukov et al.) uses ML models for both with cross-fitting. If you do causal inference seriously, that's where
you end up. Libraries: `DoWhy`, `EconML`, `causalml`.

### Matching

Pair each treated unit with the most similar untreated unit(s), by covariates or propensity score, and compare outcomes within pairs.
Intuitive and easy to explain to stakeholders, which counts for a lot.

## Designs that don't need all confounders measured

Sometimes the world gives you something close to an experiment. These **quasi-experimental** designs are what economists live on:

**Difference-in-differences (DiD)**: a policy changes in one region and not another. Compare the change over time in the treated region with the
change over time in the control region:

$$\hat\tau = (\bar{Y}_{\text{treated, after}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{treated, before}}) - (\bar{Y}_{\text{control, after}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{control, before}})$$

The first difference removes fixed differences between regions; the second removes common time trends. Key assumption: **parallel trends**,
i.e. without the policy, both would have evolved the same way. Plot the pre-period trends to check that it's plausible.

**Instrumental variables (IV)**: find a variable $Z$ that affects the treatment, doesn't affect the outcome except through the treatment, and
isn't confounded with it. Random encouragement is the typical engineering example: you randomly send some users a push notification promoting
a feature ($Z$); usage of the feature ($T$) is still self-selected, but the notification shifts it. The effect of $T$ on $Y$ is then estimated by
the ratio (the Wald estimator) $\frac{\text{effect of } Z \text{ on } Y}{\text{effect of } Z \text{ on } T}$, or by two-stage least squares. Great instruments are rare;
weak or invalid instruments give garbage.

**Regression discontinuity**: treatment is assigned by a threshold on a score (free shipping above 50 dollars, a credit decision above a score
cutoff). Units just above and just below are nearly identical, so comparing them near the threshold estimates the local effect.

## What this means for ML engineers

- **Feature importance is not a causal effect.** SHAP values (22.3) explain what the *model* uses to predict, not what would happen if you changed
  the feature in the world. Don't let a feature importance chart become a business strategy without an experiment.
- **Your training data is observational, and your model's actions change it.** A recommender trained on clicks learns from items it chose to
  show. A fraud model's labels only exist for transactions it didn't block. These feedback loops are causal problems (51.1, 47.2).
- **Uplift modeling**: for targeting (who should get the discount?), predict the *treatment effect* per customer, not the outcome. Customers who
  would buy anyway don't need a coupon. You need experimental data (or careful causal adjustment) to train these models.
- **When in doubt, run the experiment.** Everything in this lesson is what you do when you can't. When you can, randomize.

## Exercises

1. For each, name the structure (fork, chain, collider) and whether you should adjust for the middle variable when estimating the effect of the
   first variable on the last: (a) exercise $\rightarrow$ weight loss $\rightarrow$ blood pressure; (b) age $\rightarrow$ both "uses premium plan" and "spends more";
   (c) "has a bug in their integration" $\rightarrow$ "filed a ticket" $\leftarrow$ "is on the enterprise plan".
2. In a dataset of users who filed support tickets, enterprise users seem to have fewer integration bugs. Explain why this might be an artifact.
3. Why does the IPW estimator get unstable when some propensity scores are close to 0 or 1? What does that tell you about the data?
4. A city introduces a bike lane on one street. Traffic accidents on that street go from 40 to 30 per year; on a comparable street without changes,
   from 50 to 45. What's the DiD estimate? What assumption does it rely on?
5. Your team finds that users who enabled two-factor authentication have 60% fewer account takeovers. Should you conclude that 2FA reduces takeovers
   by 60%? What's likely going on, and how would you measure the real effect?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. (a) Chain: weight loss is a mediator. For the total effect of exercise, don't adjust for it (adjust only if you specifically want the "direct"
   effect not going through weight). (b) Fork: age is a confounder of plan and spend. Adjust for it. (c) Collider: "filed a ticket" is caused by both.
   Don't condition on it (and don't select your dataset on it).

2. Filing a ticket is a collider: caused by bugs and, independently, by being enterprise (enterprise users have a support contract and file
   tickets for everything). Among ticket filers, an enterprise user needs no bug to be there, while a non-enterprise user probably has one. So
   within the filtered data, enterprise and bugs look negatively associated, even if they're independent in the full user base.

3. Weights are $1/\hat{e}$ and $1/(1-\hat{e})$: a treated unit with $\hat{e} = 0.01$ gets weight 100 and can dominate the estimate. It means overlap is poor:
   for that covariate profile almost nobody is treated (or untreated), so the data has almost no information about the counterfactual there. The
   honest fix is often to restrict the question to the population with overlap, and say so.

4. $(30 - 40) - (45 - 50) = -10 - (-5) = -5$ accidents per year. It assumes parallel trends: without the bike lane, the treated street would have seen
   the same change as the comparison street (-5). If both were already on different trajectories, the estimate is biased.

5. No. Users who enable 2FA are probably more security-conscious in general (better passwords, less phishing clicks): a confounder. Also, attackers
   may target accounts without 2FA precisely because it's easier (the effect on a user is real, but the total takeover reduction if everyone had 2FA
   could be smaller as attackers adapt). To measure it: a randomized encouragement design (randomly prompt some users to enable 2FA and use the prompt
   as an instrument), or careful adjustment for security behavior, or a staged mandatory rollout by region compared with DiD. And frankly, 2FA is
   good practice regardless of the exact number; not every decision needs an effect size.
