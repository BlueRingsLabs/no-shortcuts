"The new model has 87.3% accuracy." Okay. Plus or minus what? If the answer is "plus or minus 0.1%", that's a result.
If it's "plus or minus 4%", you've just reported a coin flip with three significant digits. The single most common
failure I see in data science reports and ML papers is a number without an uncertainty: a point estimate presented as
if it were a fact.

This module is statistics for people who build things. This first lesson is about quantifying uncertainty: estimators,
their bias and variance, confidence intervals and what they actually mean, and the bootstrap, which is the most useful
statistical tool you'll ever learn because it works when the formulas don't.

## Estimators and their errors

An **estimator** is a function of the data that tries to approximate an unknown quantity $\theta$: the sample mean for the
population mean, the test-set accuracy for the "true" accuracy of a model, the MLE (04.4) for a model parameter. Because the
data is random, the estimator $\hat\theta$ is a random variable, with its own distribution: the **sampling distribution**.

Two properties of that distribution matter:

- **Bias**: $\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta] - \theta$. Does it aim at the right place on average?
- **Variance**: $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)$. How much does it jump around between datasets? Its square root is the **standard error** (SE).

They combine into the mean squared error of the estimator:

$$\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2] = \operatorname{Bias}^2 + \operatorname{Var}$$

(Proof: add and subtract $\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]$ inside the square; the cross term has expectation zero.) This is the first appearance of the
**bias-variance trade-off**, and it's the same decomposition that governs model generalization in 16.2. A slightly biased estimator
with much lower variance often has lower MSE than an unbiased one. Ridge regression (17.2), shrinkage estimators, and regularization in
general exploit this. Unbiasedness is overrated.

## Confidence intervals

A **95% confidence interval** is a procedure that, applied to repeated samples, produces intervals that contain the true $\theta$ 95% of the
time.

Read that again, because the common interpretation is wrong. A 95% CI does **not** mean "there's a 95% probability that $\theta$ is in *this*
interval". In frequentist statistics, $\theta$ is fixed and the interval is random; once you've computed it, it either contains $\theta$ or it
doesn't. The 95% is a property of the *method*, a long-run coverage guarantee. (The Bayesian **credible interval** from 04.2 does mean
"95% posterior probability", given the prior. In practice, with reasonable priors and decent amounts of data, they're numerically close,
and the distinction matters more to philosophers than to engineers. But know it, because someone will ask you in an interview.)

### The normal-approximation interval

By the CLT (04.3), many estimators are approximately Gaussian around $\theta$ with standard deviation SE. Then

$$\hat\theta \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\widehat{\text{SE}}$$

with $z_{0.975} \approx 1.96$ for 95%. For a mean, $\widehat{\text{SE}} = s/\sqrt{n}$ with $s$ the sample standard deviation. For a proportion (an
accuracy, a conversion rate), $\widehat{\text{SE}} = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$.

Example: 870 correct predictions out of 1,000. $\hat{p} = 0.87$, SE $= \sqrt{0.87 \times 0.13/1000} \approx 0.0106$. The 95% CI is
$0.87 \pm 0.021$, roughly $[0.849, 0.891]$. So "87.3% vs 86.1%" on a test set of 1,000 examples is not a difference you can claim.

For small samples from a Gaussian, replace $z$ with the Student-t quantile $t_{n-1}$, which is wider, to account for the uncertainty in $s$.
For proportions near 0 or 1 or with small $n$, the simple (Wald) interval behaves badly; use the **Wilson** interval instead
(`statsmodels.stats.proportion.proportion_confint(method="wilson")`). The lab shows the Wald interval failing for rare events, which is
exactly the regime of fraud rates, error rates and conversion rates.

## The bootstrap

Here's the problem with formulas: they exist for means and proportions and a few other things. What's the standard error of a median?
Of an F1 score? Of the difference in AUC between two models evaluated on the same test set? Of the 99th percentile latency? Of a ratio
of two sums (revenue per session)? Formulas either don't exist, are horrible, or rely on assumptions you can't check.

In 1979, Bradley Efron proposed an idea so simple that people initially thought it had to be wrong: **use the sample as a stand-in for the
population, and resample from it.**

1. From your $n$ observations, draw $n$ observations **with replacement**. That's a bootstrap sample. Some points appear several times,
   some not at all (on average about 63.2% of the distinct points appear, since $1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \to 1 - 1/e$).
2. Compute your statistic on it.
3. Repeat $B$ times (1,000 to 10,000).
4. The distribution of those $B$ values approximates the sampling distribution of your statistic.

The standard deviation of the bootstrap values estimates the SE. For a 95% interval, the simplest method takes the 2.5th and 97.5th
percentiles of the bootstrap distribution (the **percentile interval**). Better variants exist (the **BCa** interval corrects for bias and
skewness, and `scipy.stats.bootstrap` implements it). All of this in about ten lines of NumPy:

```python
def bootstrap_ci(data, stat, B=5000, alpha=0.05, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    n = len(data)
    idx = rng.integers(0, n, size=(B, n))          # B resamples at once
    boots = np.array([stat(data[i]) for i in idx])
    return np.quantile(boots, [alpha / 2, 1 - alpha / 2])
```

### Bootstrapping ML metrics

For model evaluation, resample **test examples** (rows), recompute the metric each time:

```python
def metric_ci(y_true, y_score, metric, B=2000, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    n = len(y_true)
    vals = []
    for _ in range(B):
        i = rng.integers(0, n, n)
        if len(np.unique(y_true[i])) < 2:      # AUC is undefined if a resample has one class
            continue
        vals.append(metric(y_true[i], y_score[i]))
    return np.quantile(vals, [0.025, 0.975])
```

To compare two models on the same test set, **bootstrap the difference**, using the *same* resampled indices for both models in each
iteration. That preserves the pairing: both models are scored on the same examples, so shared difficulty cancels out, and the interval on
the difference is much narrower than you'd get by comparing two separate intervals. "Their intervals overlap, so the difference isn't
significant" is a common and wrong conclusion; paired differences can be clearly significant even when the individual intervals overlap.

### When the bootstrap fails

It's not magic. It needs:

- **Independent units.** If your rows aren't independent (several rows per user, time series), resample the independent units: whole
  users (the **cluster bootstrap**) or blocks of consecutive time points (the **block bootstrap**). Resampling rows of correlated data
  gives intervals that are far too narrow, the same trap as 04.3.
- **A representative sample.** The bootstrap can't create information that's not in the data. If the sample has no extreme values, the
  bootstrap won't invent them. Extremes (max, min, very high quantiles of heavy-tailed data) are badly estimated by the bootstrap.
- **Enough data.** With $n = 10$, the "population" you resample from is tiny. The intervals will be too narrow.
- **Smooth statistics.** The median of small samples and other statistics that jump discretely can misbehave.

## Things that will bite you

- **Point estimates without intervals.** Put error bars on everything you report. Your future self and your colleagues will thank you,
  and your claims will survive contact with a skeptical reviewer.
- **Wrong unit of resampling.** Rows vs users vs days. Resample what is independent.
- **Comparing overlapping intervals** instead of computing the interval of the difference.
- **Test sets too small to detect the differences you care about.** Compute the SE before you run the experiment: if a 0.5-point
  improvement matters and your SE is 1 point, no evaluation on that test set will settle it.
- **Misreading CIs** as posterior probabilities in presentations. Say "we're 95% confident" if you must; know that it's shorthand.
