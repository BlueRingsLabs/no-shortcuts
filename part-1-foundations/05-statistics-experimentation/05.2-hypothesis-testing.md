# 05.2 Hypothesis testing and how people abuse it

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 05.1. Lab: [`lab_05_2_testing.py`](labs/lab_05_2_testing.py).*

The p-value is probably the most misunderstood number in science. It has been misread so badly, for so long, that the
American Statistical Association issued a formal statement in 2016 to explain what it isn't, which is the statistical
equivalent of a product recall. A good chunk of published research in some fields doesn't replicate, and misuse of
hypothesis tests is one of the main reasons.

You still need tests. They're how you decide whether a new model, feature or page is actually better or just lucky. So
this lesson teaches you how they work, and then, because I spend a good part of my professional life thinking about how
systems get abused, it teaches you exactly how people break them, so you can spot it in others' work and, more
importantly, in your own.

## The logic of a test

A hypothesis test is a proof by contradiction, with probabilities:

1. State a **null hypothesis** $H_0$, usually "no effect": the new model is no better, the two groups have the same mean, the coin
   is fair.
2. Choose a **test statistic** $T$ that would be large if $H_0$ were false: a difference in means scaled by its standard error, say.
3. Figure out the distribution of $T$ **assuming $H_0$ is true** (the null distribution), either from theory (CLT, t, chi-square) or
   by simulation (permutations).
4. Compute the **p-value**: the probability, under $H_0$, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one you observed.
5. If the p-value is below a threshold $\alpha$ (conventionally 0.05), **reject** $H_0$.

### What a p-value is, and isn't

The p-value is $P(\text{data this extreme or more} \mid H_0)$. That's it.

It is **not**:

- the probability that $H_0$ is true (that would be $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$; confusing them is the conditional-flip fallacy from 04.2),
- the probability that your result is a fluke,
- a measure of how big or important the effect is,
- a measure of the probability that the result will replicate.

A tiny p-value with a huge sample can come from an effect so small nobody cares (a 0.01% improvement in conversion, measured on 50
million users). A large p-value can come from an important effect measured on too few examples. **Always report the effect size with a
confidence interval.** The p-value alone answers the least interesting question.

One property worth knowing: **under $H_0$, the p-value is uniformly distributed** on $[0, 1]$ (for continuous test statistics). So if
you run 100 tests where nothing is going on, about 5 will come out "significant" at 0.05. That's not a bug of the test; that's the
definition of $\alpha$. It's also the root of most abuse, as we'll see.

## Errors and power

| | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
|---|---|---|
| reject $H_0$ | **Type I error** (false positive), probability $\alpha$ | correct, probability = **power** $= 1 - \beta$ |
| don't reject | correct | **Type II error** (false negative), probability $\beta$ |

$\alpha$ is chosen by you. Power depends on the effect size, the noise, and the sample size, and it's the thing people forget to compute.
An underpowered experiment (power 20%, say) mostly fails to detect real effects, and, nastier, when it *does* reach significance, it
overestimates the effect, because only the lucky noisy draws cross the threshold (the "winner's curse"). You'll size experiments
properly in 05.3.

"Not significant" does not mean "no effect". It means "we couldn't tell". Absence of evidence, and so on.

## The tests you'll actually use

**Comparing two means** (average order value in groups A and B, latency before and after a change): **Welch's t-test**.

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B}{\sqrt{s_A^2/n_A + s_B^2/n_B}}$$

Use Welch's version (`scipy.stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)`), which doesn't assume equal variances. The classic Student's t-test
does, and there's no good reason to make that assumption. For large samples, the CLT makes it valid even for non-normal data; for
heavily skewed data (revenue!) and moderate samples, be careful (see permutation tests and 05.3).

**Paired data** (same users before/after, same test examples scored by two models): the **paired t-test** on the differences. Pairing
removes the between-unit variability and is dramatically more powerful, the same idea as the paired bootstrap in 05.1.

**Comparing proportions** (conversion rates, error rates): a **two-proportion z-test**, or a **chi-square test** on the 2x2 table,
which is equivalent. For small counts, **Fisher's exact test**.

**Comparing two classifiers on the same test set**: **McNemar's test**. It looks only at the examples where the models *disagree*: $b$ =
A right and B wrong, $c$ = A wrong and B right. Under $H_0$ (equal accuracy), disagreements are equally likely to go either way, so
$b \sim \text{Binomial}(b + c, 0.5)$. It's the right test for "is model B's accuracy really higher on this test set?".

**Non-parametric**: the **Mann-Whitney U** (a.k.a. Wilcoxon rank-sum) test compares distributions using ranks, robust to outliers. It
tests whether one group tends to have larger values, which is not exactly "different medians" (a common misstatement). Fun fact: the U
statistic divided by $n_A n_B$ is exactly the ROC AUC (18.2).

### The permutation test: the one I trust most

If $H_0$ says "the group labels don't matter", then shuffling the labels shouldn't change the statistic's distribution. So:

1. Compute the observed statistic (e.g. difference in means).
2. Shuffle the group labels, recompute the statistic. Repeat 10,000 times.
3. The p-value is the fraction of shuffled statistics at least as extreme as the observed one.

```python
def permutation_test(a, b, stat=lambda x, y: x.mean() - y.mean(), n_perm=10_000, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    observed = stat(a, b)
    pooled = np.concatenate([a, b])
    count = 0
    for _ in range(n_perm):
        rng.shuffle(pooled)
        count += abs(stat(pooled[:len(a)], pooled[len(a):])) >= abs(observed)
    return (count + 1) / (n_perm + 1)          # +1: the observed labeling is one of the permutations
```

No distributional assumptions, works for any statistic (medians, AUC differences, ratios), and it's exact in the sense that it tests
precisely the hypothesis "labels are exchangeable". It needs the same thing the bootstrap needs: exchangeable units. Permute users, not
rows, if rows are clustered.

## How people break hypothesis tests

Now the fun part. Each of these inflates the false positive rate far above the nominal $\alpha$, while every individual test still looks
"correct".

### 1. Multiple comparisons

Test 20 metrics, or 20 user segments, or 20 model variants, at $\alpha = 0.05$ each. If nothing is real, the probability that at least one
comes out significant is $1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 64\%$. Then the report says "variant 14 significantly improved retention among Android users in
Brazil". Maybe. Or maybe you rolled 20 dice and are reporting the six.

Corrections:

- **Bonferroni**: use $\alpha / m$ for each of $m$ tests. Controls the probability of *any* false positive (the family-wise error rate).
  Simple, conservative.
- **Holm**: a step-down version of Bonferroni, uniformly more powerful, same guarantee. There's no reason to use plain Bonferroni over
  Holm.
- **Benjamini-Hochberg**: controls the **false discovery rate** (the expected fraction of false positives among your "discoveries").
  Sort p-values $p_{(1)} \le \cdots \le p_{(m)}$, find the largest $k$ with $p_{(k)} \le \frac{k}{m}\alpha$, reject the first $k$. Much more powerful
  when you test many things and can tolerate a few false discoveries (feature screening, genomics, monitoring hundreds of metrics).

`statsmodels.stats.multitest.multipletests` implements all of them.

### 2. Peeking and optional stopping

Run an A/B test, check the p-value every day, stop as soon as it's below 0.05. This feels innocent. It's devastating: with daily peeking
over a month, the false positive rate can reach 20-30% or more. The p-value wanders randomly under $H_0$ and, given enough looks, will
dip below any threshold eventually. 05.3 covers how to peek legitimately (sequential tests).

### 3. The garden of forking paths (p-hacking)

Try a few outlier filters. Try log-transforming. Try excluding the first week "because of a bug". Try a different metric definition. Try
controlling for a covariate. Stop when something is significant. No single step is fraudulent, and each decision can be justified after
the fact, but together they're a multiple comparisons problem where the number of comparisons is hidden, even from yourself. Gelman
and Loken called this the "garden of forking paths", and it doesn't require bad intent, just flexibility and hope.

The defense is to **decide the analysis before seeing the results**: pre-register the primary metric, the test, the sample size, the
exclusion rules. In a company, write them in the experiment doc before launch. It's the same principle as keeping a test set locked away
until the end in ML (18.1): as soon as you make decisions based on the test data, it stops being a test.

### 4. HARKing

"Hypothesizing After the Results are Known": finding a pattern in the data, then writing it up as if it had been the hypothesis all along.
Exploratory analysis is great; just label it as exploratory and confirm it on new data.

### 5. Ignoring the base rate of true hypotheses

If only 10% of the ideas you test are real improvements, and your tests have 50% power at $\alpha = 0.05$, then among significant results:
true positives $0.1 \times 0.5 = 0.05$, false positives $0.9 \times 0.05 = 0.045$. Nearly half of your "wins" are false. That's Bayes' rule again (04.2),
and it's the core argument of Ioannidis's famous "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False". It applies to your team's
experimentation program exactly as it applies to science.

## Things that will bite you

- **p-values without effect sizes and intervals.**
- **Many tests, no correction.** Count your tests honestly, including the ones you ran and didn't report.
- **Peeking.** Fix the sample size in advance, or use a sequential method.
- **Unpaired tests on paired data**, throwing away most of your power.
- **Row-level tests on user-level randomization**: the same clustered-data problem as before, now producing false positives.

## Exercises

1. Explain in two sentences, to a product manager, what "p = 0.03" means for their A/B test, without saying anything false.
2. Two models are evaluated on 1,000 test examples. They agree on 940. Of the 60 disagreements, B is right on 38 and A on 22. Run McNemar's
   test (by hand, with the normal approximation). Is B better?
3. You test 50 features for association with churn. The smallest p-values are 0.0004, 0.002, 0.009, 0.012, 0.03, ... Which ones survive
   Holm at $\alpha = 0.05$? Which survive Benjamini-Hochberg at FDR 0.05?
4. Why is the p-value uniform under $H_0$? (Hint: for a continuous statistic with null CDF $F$, the p-value is $1 - F(T)$. What's the
   distribution of $F(T)$?)
5. Your team runs 40 experiments a year. About 20% of the ideas are real improvements, and tests have 80% power at $\alpha = 0.05$. Of the
   experiments that come out significant, what fraction are real?

3. Holm compares the $k$-th smallest to $\alpha/(m - k + 1)$: $0.0004 \le 0.05/50 = 0.001$ yes; $0.002 \le 0.05/49 \approx 0.00102$ no, stop. Only

   design.

4. If $T$ has continuous CDF $F$ under $H_0$, then $F(T)$ is uniform (the inverse CDF trick from 04.1, run backwards: $P(F(T) \le u) = P(T \le F^{-1}(u)) = u$).
   So $1 - F(T)$ is uniform too.

5. Real and significant: $0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.16$. False and significant: $0.8 \times 0.05 = 0.04$. Fraction real: $0.16/0.20 = 80\%$. Not bad. With 30%
