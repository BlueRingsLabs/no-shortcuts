# 05.3 A/B testing: power, peeking and other crimes

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 05.1, 05.2. Lab: [`lab_05_3_ab_testing.py`](labs/lab_05_3_ab_testing.py).*

An A/B test is the closest thing a company has to a scientific experiment, and it's the only reliable way to know
whether a change *caused* an improvement. Offline metrics lie (47.1 will explain why), dashboards lie, and before/after
comparisons lie because the world changes while you're comparing. Randomization is the one trick that cuts through all of
that: if users are assigned to A or B by a coin flip, the only systematic difference between the groups is the change you
made.

And yet. I've seen more broken A/B tests than working ones: in friends' startups, in blog posts announcing a winner, in a thesis or two. Tests too small to detect anything, tests stopped the moment
they looked good, tests where the randomization was broken and nobody checked, tests where the "winning" metric was
chosen after the fact. The statistics are the easy part. This lesson is about designing tests that can actually answer the
question, and about the specific ways they go wrong in real systems.

## Anatomy of an experiment

Before launching, write down (in a document, dated, before seeing any data):

1. **Hypothesis**: what change, and what do you expect it to do? "Showing delivery estimates on the product page increases
   checkout conversion."
2. **Randomization unit**: users, sessions, devices, pages, stores, days? Usually users (with a stable ID), so a person sees
   one consistent experience. The unit of analysis must match the unit of randomization (05.1, clustered data).
3. **Primary metric**: one, decided in advance. Conversion rate per user, revenue per user, retention at day 7.
4. **Guardrail metrics**: things that must not get worse: latency, error rate, unsubscribe rate, revenue if the primary is
   engagement. A change that increases clicks by making the page slower is not a win.
5. **Minimum detectable effect (MDE)**: the smallest effect that would matter to the business. Not the effect you hope for.
6. **Sample size and duration**, from a power analysis (next section). Run for whole weeks: behavior differs between Monday and
   Saturday.
7. **Decision rule**: what you'll do for each outcome.

That document is your pre-registration (05.2). It protects you from the garden of forking paths, and from your boss.

## Power and sample size

For comparing two conversion rates with baseline $p$ and an absolute difference $\delta$ you want to detect, with significance level $\alpha$
(two-sided) and power $1 - \beta$, the required sample size **per group** is approximately

$$n \approx \frac{2\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2\,p(1-p)}{\delta^2}$$

With $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80% power, $(1.96 + 0.84)^2 \approx 7.85$, so $n \approx 16\,p(1-p)/\delta^2$ ("Lehr's rule of 16", which for continuous metrics reads
$n \approx 16\sigma^2/\delta^2$).

Let's make it concrete. Baseline conversion 5%, and you want to detect a 5% **relative** lift (5.00% to 5.25%, $\delta = 0.0025$):

$$n \approx \frac{16 \times 0.05 \times 0.95}{0.0025^2} \approx 121{,}600 \text{ users per group}$$

A quarter of a million users for one test. If your site has 10,000 users a week, that's six months. Forget it, or test bigger changes, or
use a more sensitive metric, or reduce variance (CUPED, below). What you must *not* do is run it for two weeks, see "no significant
difference", and conclude the feature doesn't work. You just ran a test with about 10% power. It couldn't have told you anything.

Notice the $\delta^2$ in the denominator: halving the effect you want to detect **quadruples** the sample size. Small effects are expensive.
Mature products mostly have small effects left to find, which is why big companies run tests on millions of users and small ones should
test bold changes.

## Checks before you look at results

### Sample ratio mismatch (SRM)

You configured a 50/50 split. You got 50,812 users in A and 49,188 in B. Is that fine? Run a chi-square goodness-of-fit test against the
expected 50/50:

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(50812 - 50000)^2}{50000} + \frac{(49188 - 50000)^2}{50000} \approx 26.4, \quad p \approx 3 \times 10^{-7}$$

That's not chance. Something in the pipeline is dropping or misassigning users in one arm: a redirect that fails on some browsers, a bot
filter that interacts with the variant, a crash in the new code path that prevents logging, a caching layer. **If you have an SRM, the
results are invalid**, whatever they say, because the groups are no longer comparable. Microsoft's experimentation team reported that a
noticeable fraction of their experiments had SRMs, and it's the first thing any good experimentation platform checks. Check it every time,
before looking at the metric.

### A/A tests

Run the system with two identical arms. You should see "significant" differences about 5% of the time, not more, and no SRM. If your A/A
tests fail more often, your randomization, logging or variance estimates are broken, and every A/B test on that platform is suspect.

## Variance reduction: CUPED

The noise in a metric like revenue per user comes mostly from the fact that users are very different from each other, not from your change.
If you have a pre-experiment measurement of the same metric (revenue in the 30 days *before* the test), you can remove the predictable part
of the variance. The CUPED method (Deng et al., Microsoft, 2013):

$$Y_{\text{adj}} = Y - \theta\,(X - \bar{X}), \qquad \theta = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$$

where $X$ is the pre-experiment covariate. Because $X$ was measured before randomization, it's independent of the treatment, so the adjustment
doesn't bias the estimated difference; it only removes noise. The variance is reduced by a factor $1 - \rho^2$, with $\rho$ the correlation
between $X$ and $Y$. With $\rho = 0.7$ (common for revenue or engagement), variance drops by about half, which is the same as doubling your
sample size for free. It's just regression adjustment (17.1) in disguise, and it's one of the highest-value, lowest-effort tricks in the
whole field.

## Ratio metrics and the delta method

Many metrics are ratios where the numerator and denominator are both random: click-through rate = clicks / impressions, revenue per session
when randomization is by user. Each user contributes several sessions, so sessions aren't independent, and the naive standard error (treating
each session as an observation) is too small.

Two correct options: bootstrap at the user level (05.1), or use the **delta method**, which linearizes the ratio. For $R = \bar{Y}/\bar{X}$ with
per-user totals $Y_i, X_i$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(R) \approx \frac{1}{n\,\bar{X}^2}\left(\sigma_Y^2 - 2R\,\sigma_{XY} + R^2\sigma_X^2\right)$$

It's derived from a first-order Taylor expansion (03.2) of $f(\bar{X}, \bar{Y}) = \bar{Y}/\bar{X}$. The lab checks it against the user-level bootstrap.

## Peeking, done legitimately

From 05.2: checking a fixed-sample test repeatedly inflates false positives. But in real companies, people *will* look at running tests,
and sometimes you genuinely need to stop early (the change is breaking something). The solutions:

- **Group sequential designs**: decide in advance to look at, say, 5 interim points, and use stricter thresholds at each look so that the
  overall $\alpha$ stays at 5%. The **O'Brien-Fleming** boundaries are very strict early (you only stop for enormous effects) and close to
  the usual threshold at the end, so you lose very little power. Pocock boundaries use the same threshold at every look.
- **Always-valid inference** (mixture sequential probability ratio tests, confidence sequences): p-values and intervals that stay valid
  no matter how often or when you look. Several commercial experimentation platforms use variants of this. You pay with somewhat wider
  intervals.
- **Bayesian monitoring**: posterior probability that B > A, which many teams find more intuitive. Be aware that with optional stopping, a
  Bayesian decision rule ("stop when P(B > A) > 95%") also has frequentist error rates higher than you might think. There's no free lunch;
  there's only choosing which guarantee you want.

## Things that go wrong in real systems

- **Novelty and primacy effects**: users click on the new thing because it's new, and the effect fades after two weeks. Or they initially
  resist a change and then adapt. Look at the effect over time within the test.
- **Interference (network effects)**: in a marketplace, a social network or a ride-sharing app, treated and control users affect each other
  (a pricing change in B moves supply away from A). Randomizing users violates the assumption that one unit's treatment doesn't affect
  another's outcome. Solutions: cluster randomization (by city, by region), switchback experiments (alternate treatment over time windows).
- **Heterogeneous effects**: the average effect is zero because it helps new users and hurts old ones. Look at pre-registered segments,
  with the multiple comparisons caveat.
- **Long-term effects**: a change that increases short-term revenue by showing more ads can reduce long-term retention. Holdout groups that
  don't get the change for months help measure that.
- **Bots and outliers**: a single scraper or a single enterprise customer can dominate revenue metrics. Cap or winsorize heavy-tailed metrics
  (pre-registered!), and filter bots before the analysis, identically in both arms.
- **Bandits vs A/B tests**: if you care about maximizing reward *during* the test more than learning the exact effect, multi-armed bandits
  (46.1) shift traffic toward the winner as evidence accumulates. Great for headlines and ad creatives; worse for learning precise effect
  sizes.

> **Scar tissue.** The first A/B test I ever ran was for a friend's small online shop, as a freelance job, and it "won" on day three: p =
> 0.03 on a new checkout button. I stopped it and shipped it, proud of my statistics. Over the next month conversion drifted back to
> exactly where it had been. I'd checked the p-value every morning and stopped the first time it dipped under 0.05, which with daily peeks
> is a coin flip with extra steps. The button is probably still there. It does nothing. It's my monument.

## Exercises

1. Baseline conversion 12%. How many users per group do you need to detect an absolute lift of 1 percentage point with 80% power at
   $\alpha = 0.05$? And for 0.5 points?
2. Your 50/50 test has 10,120 users in A and 9,880 in B. SRM or not? What about 101,200 vs 98,800?
3. The correlation between pre-period and in-period revenue per user is 0.6. By what factor does CUPED reduce the required sample size?
4. A test shows +3% revenue per user with p = 0.04, but the chart of the daily effect starts at +8% and falls to 0% by day 14. What do you tell
   the product manager?
5. You run a promotion test on a ride-sharing app by randomizing riders: treated riders get a discount. Treated riders take more rides; control
   riders take fewer rides than before the test started. Explain what happened and how you'd redesign the test.
