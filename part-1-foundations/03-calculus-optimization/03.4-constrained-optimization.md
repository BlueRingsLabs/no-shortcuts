# 03.4 Constrained optimization: Lagrange multipliers and KKT

*About 8 hours. Prerequisites: 03.1 to 03.3. Lab: [`lab_03_4_constrained.py`](labs/lab_03_4_constrained.py).*

Real problems come with rules. The weights must sum to one (a portfolio, a probability distribution). The direction
must have unit length (PCA). The margin must be at least one for every training point (SVMs). The model's predictions
must satisfy a fairness constraint. The cloud bill can't exceed what's in your bank account, a constraint I've
violated more often than any mathematical one.

This lesson gives you the tool to handle those rules: Lagrange multipliers, their generalization to inequalities (the
KKT conditions), and a glimpse of duality. You'll use it immediately to derive three things that otherwise look like
magic: why PCA is an eigenvector problem, why softmax is the "right" way to turn scores into probabilities, and (in
19.3) why only a few training points matter in an SVM.

## Equality constraints: the geometry

Minimize $f(x)$ subject to $g(x) = 0$. Picture the contour lines of $f$ and the curve $g(x) = 0$. Walk along the curve.
As long as the curve *crosses* a contour line, you can keep walking in the direction that lowers $f$. You're stuck (at a
constrained optimum) exactly when the curve is *tangent* to a contour line of $f$. Tangent means their normals are
parallel, and the normals are the gradients (03.1). So at the optimum:

$$\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda\,\nabla g(x^*) \quad \text{for some scalar } \lambda$$

That's the Lagrange condition. $\lambda$ is the **Lagrange multiplier**. Package it as the **Lagrangian**

$$\mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda\,g(x)$$

and the conditions become "all partial derivatives of $\mathcal{L}$ are zero": $\nabla_x\mathcal{L} = 0$ gives the parallel gradients,
and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\lambda = 0$ gives back the constraint. (Sign conventions vary between books: $f + \lambda g$ or
$f - \lambda g$. It only flips the sign of $\lambda$.)

With several constraints $g_1(x) = 0, \ldots, g_m(x) = 0$, you get one multiplier each: $\nabla f = \sum_i \lambda_i\nabla g_i$.

### What the multiplier means

$\lambda$ isn't just a bookkeeping trick. It's the **sensitivity** of the optimal value to the constraint: if you relax
the constraint to $g(x) = \epsilon$, the optimal value changes by about $\lambda\epsilon$. Economists call it a *shadow price*:
how much would you pay to loosen this rule a little? That interpretation turns out to be useful in ML too; in
constrained training (fairness constraints, for example), the multiplier tells you how much accuracy each unit of
constraint is costing you.

## Worked example 1: PCA is an eigenvector problem

Find the unit direction $w$ along which centered data has maximum variance. The variance of the projections $Xw$ is
$w^\top C w$ with $C$ the covariance matrix. Without a constraint, you'd make $w$ huge, so:

$$\max_w\; w^\top C w \quad \text{subject to} \quad w^\top w = 1$$

Lagrangian: $\mathcal{L} = w^\top C w - \lambda(w^\top w - 1)$. Gradient with respect to $w$ (03.1 table): $2Cw - 2\lambda w = 0$, so

$$Cw = \lambda w$$

The optimal direction is an **eigenvector** of the covariance matrix. Which one? The variance at an eigenvector is
$w^\top C w = \lambda w^\top w = \lambda$, so to maximize it, pick the eigenvector with the **largest eigenvalue**. And the
multiplier *is* the variance. The whole of PCA (21.3) is this three-line derivation, repeated with orthogonality
constraints for the next components. When somebody tells you PCA "finds the directions of maximum variance" and "is
an eigendecomposition", this is why those are the same statement.

## Worked example 2: maximum entropy gives softmax

You want a probability distribution $p$ over $k$ outcomes with scores $s_1, \ldots, s_k$. You want the expected score to be
some value $\mu$, but otherwise you want to assume as little as possible, which means maximum entropy (06.1):

$$\max_p\; -\sum_i p_i\log p_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_i p_i = 1,\; \sum_i p_i s_i = \mu$$

(We'll ignore $p_i \ge 0$; it turns out to hold automatically.) Lagrangian with two multipliers:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i - \alpha\left(\sum_i p_i - 1\right) + \beta\left(\sum_i p_i s_i - \mu\right)$$

$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial p_i = -\log p_i - 1 - \alpha + \beta s_i = 0$ gives $p_i = e^{\beta s_i}/e^{1+\alpha}$. The normalization constraint fixes
the denominator:

$$p_i = \frac{e^{\beta s_i}}{\sum_j e^{\beta s_j}} = \operatorname{softmax}(\beta s)_i$$

The softmax is the maximum-entropy distribution with a given expected score. The multiplier $\beta$ is an **inverse
temperature**: large $\beta$ concentrates the mass on the top score, $\beta \to 0$ gives the uniform distribution. When you
turn the temperature knob while sampling from an LLM (37.1), you're changing this $\beta$. Physicists call this the
Boltzmann distribution, and they got there first by about a century.

## Inequality constraints: KKT

Now minimize $f(x)$ subject to $h_j(x) \le 0$ for $j = 1, \ldots, m$ (plus possibly equalities). At the optimum, each
inequality is either:

- **Inactive** ($h_j(x^*) < 0$): the constraint isn't binding, you're in the interior as far as it's concerned, and it
  doesn't affect the solution. Its multiplier is zero.
- **Active** ($h_j(x^*) = 0$): you're pressed against the wall, and the wall pushes back. Its multiplier is positive.

The **Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions** formalize this. With the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = f + \sum_j \mu_j h_j$:

1. **Stationarity**: $\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_j \mu_j\nabla h_j(x^*) = 0$
2. **Primal feasibility**: $h_j(x^*) \le 0$
3. **Dual feasibility**: $\mu_j \ge 0$
4. **Complementary slackness**: $\mu_j\,h_j(x^*) = 0$ for every $j$

Condition 4 is the one with the insight: for each constraint, either the multiplier is zero or the constraint is tight.
Never both nonzero.

For convex problems (convex $f$ and $h_j$, affine equalities, plus a mild regularity condition like Slater's: some strictly
feasible point exists), the KKT conditions are **necessary and sufficient** for global optimality. For non-convex ones,
they're only necessary (under regularity conditions).

### Why this matters for SVMs (preview of 19.3)

The SVM has one constraint per training point: "this point must be on the correct side of the margin". Complementary
slackness says the multiplier of a point is nonzero only if its constraint is active, meaning the point sits exactly on
the margin (or violates it, in the soft version). All other points have zero multipliers and don't affect the solution
at all. The points with nonzero multipliers are the **support vectors**, and the name "support vector machine" comes
directly from this KKT condition. Move any other point (without crossing the margin) and the classifier doesn't change.

## Duality, in one page

For every constrained problem (the **primal**), there's a **dual** problem built from the Lagrangian:

$$g(\mu) = \min_x \mathcal{L}(x, \mu), \qquad \text{dual: } \max_{\mu \ge 0} g(\mu)$$

Two facts:

- **Weak duality** (always true): the dual's optimal value is a lower bound on the primal's. $g(\mu) \le f(x)$ for any
  feasible $x$ and $\mu \ge 0$. The gap between them tells you how far from optimal you might be, which makes it a great
  stopping criterion for solvers.
- **Strong duality** (true for convex problems satisfying Slater's condition): the two optimal values are equal, and you
  can solve whichever is easier.

The dual is sometimes dramatically easier or more revealing. For SVMs, the dual depends on the data only through dot
products $x_i^\top x_j$, which is what makes the kernel trick possible (19.3). That's not a coincidence of notation; it's the
reason kernels exist.

## Solving constrained problems in practice

You'll rarely solve KKT systems by hand beyond small derivations. The practical toolbox:

- **Projected gradient descent**: take a normal gradient step, then project back onto the feasible set. Simple and
  effective when the projection is cheap: onto a box (clip), a ball (rescale), the probability simplex (a sort-based
  algorithm), non-negative orthant (ReLU the weights). Weight clipping in the original Wasserstein GAN (31.2) and
  norm constraints on embeddings are this.
- **Penalty methods**: replace the constraint with a penalty term, $f(x) + \rho\cdot\text{violation}(x)$. Easy, approximate.
  Soft-margin SVMs and many "regularized" objectives are this in disguise. Regularization and constraints are two views
  of the same thing: $\min f(w)$ s.t. $\|w\|^2 \le t$ and $\min f(w) + \lambda\|w\|^2$ have the same solutions for matched $t$
  and $\lambda$ (by the KKT conditions!). That's why you'll see lasso drawn both as a penalty and as a diamond-shaped
  constraint (17.2).
- **Reparameterization**: make the constraint impossible to violate. Want positive numbers? Optimize $\log\sigma$ and use
  $\sigma = e^{\log\sigma}$. Want probabilities? Optimize logits and apply softmax. Want a unit vector? Optimize any vector and
  normalize. This is by far the most common approach in deep learning, because it turns the problem back into an
  unconstrained one that SGD can handle.
- **Dedicated solvers**: `scipy.optimize.minimize` (SLSQP, trust-constr), `cvxpy` for convex problems (you describe the
  problem, it picks a solver), and specialized solvers for LPs and QPs.

## Things that will bite you

- **Forgetting that Lagrange conditions are necessary, not sufficient**: they find all candidates (minima, maxima,
  saddles on the constraint). Check which is which.
- **Constraints that SGD silently violates**: if you "constrain" a parameter by hoping the loss keeps it in range, it won't.
  Reparameterize or project.
- **Sign conventions** for multipliers differ between textbooks and software. When in doubt, re-derive with your own
  convention.
- **Non-convex constraints** (like $\|w\| = 1$, a sphere, which is not a convex set) make the problem non-convex even if $f$
  is convex. PCA gets away with it because of its special eigenvector structure; most problems don't.

## Exercises

1. Minimize $x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + 2y = 5$. Solve with a Lagrange multiplier and interpret the solution geometrically.
2. Maximize $xy$ subject to $x + y = 10$. What's the multiplier, and what does it tell you about relaxing the constraint to
   $x + y = 10.1$?
3. Find the distribution $p$ over $\{1, 2, \ldots, 6\}$ (a die) with maximum entropy and expected value 4.5. Use the
   softmax form from the lesson and find $\beta$ numerically.
4. Minimize $(x - 2)^2$ subject to $x \le 1$. Write the KKT conditions and identify which constraint is active. Then do the
   same with $x \le 3$.
5. Write the projection of a vector $v$ onto the $L_2$ ball of radius $r$, and onto the box $[0, 1]^d$.
