# 03.3 Gradient descent and convexity

*About 10 hours. Prerequisites: 03.1, 03.2. Lab: [`lab_03_3_gradient_descent.py`](labs/lab_03_3_gradient_descent.py).*

Gradient descent is the dumbest possible optimization algorithm: look at the slope, take a small step downhill,
repeat. It's also the algorithm that trains every large neural network on the planet. There's a lesson in that about
engineering, I think: simple things that scale beat clever things that don't.

But "take a small step" hides everything interesting. How small? Why does it zig-zag? Why does it converge quickly on
some problems and crawl on others? When is the answer it finds the *best* answer? This lesson answers those questions
for the cases where we have real theory (convex problems), and builds the intuition you'll carry into the messy
non-convex world of deep learning.

## The algorithm

To minimize $f(w)$, start somewhere and iterate:

$$w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta\,\nabla f(w_t)$$

$\eta > 0$ is the **step size** or **learning rate**. From the first-order Taylor approximation (03.1),

$$f(w_{t+1}) \approx f(w_t) - \eta\,\|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2$$

so for small enough $\eta$, each step decreases $f$. The whole theory of gradient descent is about making "small
enough" precise and figuring out how fast it goes.

## Convexity

A function is **convex** if the line segment between any two points on its graph lies above the graph:

$$f(\theta a + (1 - \theta)b) \le \theta f(a) + (1 - \theta) f(b) \quad \text{for all } a, b \text{ and } \theta \in [0, 1]$$

Equivalent characterizations, which are the ones you actually use:

- **First order:** the function lies above all its tangent planes: $f(b) \ge f(a) + \nabla f(a)^\top(b - a)$.
- **Second order:** the Hessian is PSD everywhere: $H(w) \succeq 0$ (all eigenvalues $\ge 0$, 03.2).

Why convexity is the best thing that can happen to you: **every local minimum of a convex function is a global
minimum.** The first-order condition says it directly: if $\nabla f(a) = 0$, then $f(b) \ge f(a)$ for every $b$. So for a
convex problem, "gradient is zero" means "done, and optimal". No saddles, no bad local minima, no dependence on
initialization (for the loss value, at least).

Convex problems you'll meet: least squares (Hessian $2X^\top X \succeq 0$), ridge and lasso (17.2), logistic regression
(17.3), SVMs (19.3), softmax regression (17.4). Useful facts for recognizing convexity: sums of convex functions are
convex, a convex function of an affine function ($f(Aw + b)$) is convex, norms are convex, and the maximum of convex
functions is convex.

Non-convex problems you'll meet: basically all neural networks, k-means (21.1), matrix factorization (23.2), mixture
models (21.2). There, gradient descent finds *a* stationary point, and which one depends on where you start. We'll
live with it.

## How big should the step be? Smoothness

A function is **$L$-smooth** if its gradient doesn't change too fast: $\|\nabla f(a) - \nabla f(b)\| \le L\|a - b\|$. For twice
differentiable functions, that means the Hessian's eigenvalues are at most $L$. $L$ is the maximum curvature.

Smoothness gives a quadratic *upper bound*:

$$f(b) \le f(a) + \nabla f(a)^\top(b - a) + \frac{L}{2}\|b - a\|^2$$

Plug in the gradient step $b = a - \eta\nabla f(a)$:

$$f(w_{t+1}) \le f(w_t) - \eta\left(1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\right)\|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2$$

So any $\eta < 2/L$ guarantees a decrease, and $\eta = 1/L$ gives the biggest guaranteed decrease,
$\frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f\|^2$. You found the same threshold $2/\lambda_{\max}$ in exercise 5 of 03.2: along the steepest direction of
the quadratic model, a step above $2/L$ overshoots so badly that it lands higher on the other side, and the next step
overshoots more. Divergence. That's what you see when your training loss explodes to `NaN` in the first few hundred
steps: the learning rate is above the stability threshold for the current curvature.

## How fast? Strong convexity and the condition number

A function is **$\mu$-strongly convex** if it curves up at least as much as a quadratic with curvature $\mu > 0$ (Hessian
eigenvalues at least $\mu$). For a function that's both $L$-smooth and $\mu$-strongly convex, gradient descent with $\eta = 1/L$
satisfies

$$f(w_t) - f^* \le \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^t (f(w_0) - f^*)$$

This is **linear convergence** (the error shrinks by a constant factor per step, so it looks like a straight line on a
log plot). The factor depends on the **condition number** $\kappa = L / \mu$, which is the ratio of the largest to the smallest
curvature. To reduce the error by a factor $\epsilon$ you need about

$$t \approx \kappa \log(1/\epsilon)$$

steps. Condition number 10: fast. Condition number $10^4$: ten thousand times slower. The same $\kappa$ from 02.2, now
measuring optimization difficulty instead of numerical accuracy. It's everywhere.

For convex but not strongly convex functions, the rate drops to $O(1/t)$, which is much slower (sublinear).

### The zig-zag, explained

Picture an elongated bowl, like $f(w) = \frac{1}{2}(100w_1^2 + w_2^2)$. The step size must be below $2/100$ to stay stable in the
steep $w_1$ direction. In the flat $w_2$ direction, that step is tiny: progress is $1/100$ of the distance per step. And
in $w_1$, a step near the limit overshoots back and forth across the valley. Result: bouncing between the walls while
slowly creeping along the floor. You watched it take 463 steps in lab 03.2, where Newton took one.

Three ways out, and you'll use all of them:

1. **Fix the problem's conditioning.** Standardize features (22.1). For linear models this directly shrinks $\kappa$ of
   $X^\top X$. For neural networks, normalization layers (26.4) do something similar inside the network.
2. **Momentum.** Accumulate a velocity so that oscillations cancel and consistent directions accelerate. Heavy-ball
   momentum and Nesterov's accelerated gradient improve the dependence from $\kappa$ to $\sqrt{\kappa}$, provably optimal for
   first-order methods on this class. Details in 26.1.
3. **Per-coordinate step sizes** (Adagrad, RMSProp, Adam): rescale each coordinate by an estimate of its gradient
   scale, a cheap diagonal version of what Newton does with the full Hessian (26.1).

## Choosing the step size in practice

- **Fixed learning rate**, tuned by trying a few values on a log scale (1e-4, 3e-4, 1e-3, 3e-3...). The deep learning
  default, together with schedules (26.2).
- **Backtracking line search** (for classical optimization): start with a big $\eta$, and halve it until the step achieves
  a sufficient decrease, the **Armijo condition** $f(w - \eta g) \le f(w) - c\,\eta\|g\|^2$ with $c$ around $10^{-4}$. Cheap and
  robust when function evaluations are exact. Useless with noisy mini-batch losses, which is why deep learning doesn't
  use it.

## Stochastic gradient descent

In ML the loss is an average over the training data:

$$f(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(w; x_i, y_i)$$

Computing the full gradient costs a pass over all $n$ examples. With $n$ in the millions or billions, that's one step per
hour. **Stochastic gradient descent** replaces it with the gradient on a random **mini-batch** $B$ of size $b$:

$$g_t = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i \in B}\nabla\ell(w_t; x_i, y_i), \qquad w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta_t\,g_t$$

The key property: $g_t$ is an **unbiased** estimate of the true gradient ($\mathbb{E}[g_t] = \nabla f(w_t)$), with variance that
shrinks like $1/b$. So each step is noisy but right on average, and it costs $b$ examples instead of $n$.

Consequences worth understanding:

- **SGD can't converge exactly with a fixed step size**: near the minimum, the true gradient is ~0 but the noise isn't,
  so the iterates bounce around in a region whose size is proportional to $\eta$. To converge you need a decaying step size
  (theory says $\sum_t \eta_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \eta_t^2 < \infty$, e.g. $\eta_t \propto 1/t$). In practice: warmup, then
  cosine or step decay (26.2).
- **Many cheap noisy steps beat few expensive exact ones.** For large datasets, SGD reaches a good-enough solution
  after seeing each example a few times, while full-batch GD would still be computing its tenth gradient.
- **The noise is not only a nuisance.** In non-convex landscapes, it helps escape saddle points and sharp regions, and
  there's evidence that it biases solutions toward flatter minima that generalize better. Larger batches reduce the noise
  and sometimes generalize slightly worse at the same number of epochs, unless you adjust the learning rate (the
  "linear scaling rule": scale $\eta$ with $b$, with warmup).
- **Shuffle your data.** Mini-batches must be (approximately) random samples. If data is sorted by class or by date and
  you don't shuffle, consecutive batches are biased and training is garbage. I've seen a model "learn" the day of the
  week because the data loader read files in date order.

## Non-convex optimization, briefly

For non-convex smooth functions, the guarantee weakens to: gradient descent with $\eta \le 1/L$ finds a point with small
gradient, $\min_t \|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2 \le 2L(f(w_0) - f^*)/T$. A stationary point, not necessarily a minimum. With a bit of noise
(SGD) or random perturbations, it escapes strict saddle points and ends up near local minima. Why those local minima are
usually good enough for neural networks is still an active research question; we'll revisit it in 26.2 and 16.3.

## Things that will bite you

- **Learning rate too high**: loss explodes or oscillates. Too low: it decreases so slowly you think the model is broken.
  Always plot the loss curve. Always try a range of learning rates.
- **Unscaled features**: huge condition number, painfully slow convergence, and a learning rate that works for one feature
  and explodes another.
- **Evaluating convergence on noisy mini-batch losses**: smooth them (moving average) before deciding anything.
- **Not shuffling**, or shuffling only once and reusing the same order every epoch in a way that correlates with the labels.

4. Show that the mini-batch gradient, with the batch drawn uniformly at random without replacement, is an unbiased
   estimator of the full gradient.
5. Why does doubling the batch size not halve the number of steps SGD needs to reach a given loss, once the batch is
   large?

4. Each example $i$ is in the batch with probability $b/n$, so
   $\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i \in B}\nabla\ell_i] = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{b}{n}\nabla\ell_i = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\nabla\ell_i = \nabla f$.

   beyond the stability limit. Past that "critical batch size", extra examples per step are mostly wasted compute.
