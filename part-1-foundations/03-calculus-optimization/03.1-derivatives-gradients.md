# 03.1 Derivatives, gradients and the chain rule

*About 12 hours. Prerequisites: high school derivatives (even rusty), 02.1. Lab:
[`lab_03_1_gradients.py`](labs/lab_03_1_gradients.py).*

Training a neural network with a hundred billion parameters comes down to one question asked a hundred billion times
per step: *if I nudge this number a tiny bit, does the loss go up or down, and by how much?* The answer is a
derivative. The algorithm that computes all hundred billion of them efficiently is the chain rule, applied in a
clever order. That's backpropagation. There's nothing else in it.

People get scared of backprop because it's usually presented with a wall of indices. We'll avoid that by building
the pieces in order: derivatives as local linear approximations, gradients as the multivariable version, the chain
rule on graphs, and a numerical check so you never have to trust a derivation blindly. Including mine.

## The derivative is a local linear approximation

Forget "slope of the tangent line" for a moment. The useful definition is: $f'(x)$ is the number that makes

$$f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h \quad \text{for small } h$$

as accurate as possible. The derivative tells you how the output responds to a small change in the input, *to first
order*. Formally,

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

The rules you need (and should be able to use without thinking):

| Function | Derivative |
|---|---|
| $x^n$ | $n x^{n-1}$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ |
| $\ln x$ | $1/x$ |
| $\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$ | $\sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$ |
| $\tanh x$ | $1 - \tanh^2 x$ |
| $\operatorname{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$ | $1$ if $x > 0$, $0$ if $x < 0$ (undefined at 0; frameworks pick 0) |
| $f(x)g(x)$ | $f'g + fg'$ |
| $f(g(x))$ | $f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$ |

The sigmoid derivative is worth deriving once yourself (exercise 1), because you'll see it everywhere, and it
explains a lot: its maximum is $0.25$ (at $x = 0$) and it goes to zero for large $|x|$. Stack many sigmoids and
multiply their derivatives (chain rule!) and you get something tiny. That's the vanishing gradient problem, and it's
why ReLU replaced sigmoid in hidden layers (24.1).

## Partial derivatives and the gradient

For a function of many variables $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ (a loss as a function of all the weights), the
**partial derivative** $\partial f / \partial x_i$ is the derivative with respect to $x_i$ with all the other variables
held fixed. The **gradient** collects them into a vector:

$$\nabla f(x) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}\right)$$

The local linear approximation becomes

$$f(x + h) \approx f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top h$$

and that dot product (02.1!) tells you everything. The change in $f$ for a small step $h$ is the projection of $h$ onto
the gradient. Therefore:

- The **directional derivative** in direction $u$ (unit vector) is $\nabla f^\top u$.
- It's maximized when $u$ points along $\nabla f$: **the gradient is the direction of steepest ascent**, and $\|\nabla f\|$
  is the rate of increase in that direction.
- $-\nabla f$ is the direction of steepest *descent*. Gradient descent (03.3) just walks that way.
- Directions perpendicular to the gradient don't change $f$ to first order: the gradient is perpendicular to the
  **level sets** (contour lines) of $f$. Draw contour lines of a bowl; the gradient arrows cross them at right angles.

## Gradients you'll need constantly

Let me give you the matrix-calculus identities that cover about 90% of what shows up in ML derivations. Convention:
$\nabla_w f$ has the same shape as $w$.

| $f(w)$ | $\nabla_w f$ | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| $a^\top w$ | $a$ | linear |
| $w^\top A w$ | $(A + A^\top)w$, which is $2Aw$ if $A$ is symmetric | quadratic form |
| $\lVert w \rVert_2^2$ | $2w$ | ridge penalty |
| $\lVert Xw - y \rVert_2^2$ | $2X^\top(Xw - y)$ | least squares (02.3) |
| $\log \sum_j e^{w_j}$ | $\operatorname{softmax}(w)$ | log-sum-exp |
| $-\sum_j t_j \log \operatorname{softmax}(z)_j$ (with $\sum_j t_j = 1$) | $\operatorname{softmax}(z) - t$ | cross-entropy with softmax |

Let's derive the least squares one to see how the pattern works. Write $r = Xw - y$, so $f = r^\top r = \sum_i r_i^2$. Then
$\partial f / \partial r_i = 2r_i$, and $r_i = \sum_j X_{ij} w_j - y_i$ gives $\partial r_i / \partial w_j = X_{ij}$. By the chain
rule, $\partial f / \partial w_j = \sum_i 2 r_i X_{ij} = 2(X^\top r)_j$. So $\nabla_w f = 2X^\top(Xw - y)$. Setting it to zero gives
the normal equations from 02.3. Everything connects.

The last row of the table deserves a moment. The gradient of cross-entropy loss with respect to the logits
(the inputs to the softmax) is simply **prediction minus target**. That absurdly clean result is why softmax and
cross-entropy are always used together, and why the gradient for logistic regression looks exactly like the gradient
for linear regression (17.3). You'll derive it in exercise 4. Please do.

## The chain rule, properly

For compositions $y = f(g(x))$ with scalars: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dg}\frac{dg}{dx}$. For vectors, the same idea holds with
Jacobian matrices (03.2), but here's the version that matters for backprop, which is about *graphs*:

**If $x$ influences $L$ through several paths, add up the contributions of each path.**

Consider $L = f(a, b)$ where $a = g(x)$ and $b = h(x)$. Then

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\frac{\partial a}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\frac{\partial b}{\partial x}$$

That's the multivariable chain rule. Every computation you'll ever write (a loss function, a neural network, a
physics simulation) can be drawn as a **computational graph**: nodes are simple operations, edges carry values.
The chain rule tells you how to get the derivative of the output with respect to any node: multiply local
derivatives along each path, then sum over paths.

### A worked example

Let's take a single neuron with a squared loss:

$$z = wx + b, \qquad a = \sigma(z), \qquad L = (a - y)^2$$

with $x = 2$, $w = 0.5$, $b = -1$, $y = 1$.

**Forward pass** (compute values left to right):

- $z = 0.5 \cdot 2 - 1 = 0$
- $a = \sigma(0) = 0.5$
- $L = (0.5 - 1)^2 = 0.25$

**Backward pass** (compute derivatives right to left, reusing what you already have):

- $\partial L / \partial a = 2(a - y) = -1$
- $\partial L / \partial z = \partial L / \partial a \cdot \sigma'(z) = -1 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = -0.25$
- $\partial L / \partial w = \partial L / \partial z \cdot x = -0.25 \cdot 2 = -0.5$
- $\partial L / \partial b = \partial L / \partial z \cdot 1 = -0.25$

Notice what happened: we computed $\partial L / \partial z$ once and reused it for both $w$ and $b$. In a network with
millions of weights, this reuse is everything. It's why computing *all* the gradients costs about the same as
2-3 forward passes, instead of a million forward passes.

The gradient says: increase $w$ (its partial is negative, so the loss goes *down* when $w$ goes up). Makes sense: we
predicted 0.5, the target is 1, and increasing $w$ increases $z$ and therefore $a$.

### Forward mode vs reverse mode

There are two orders in which you can apply the chain rule on a graph:

- **Forward mode**: pick one *input*, propagate derivatives forward alongside the values. One pass gives you the
  derivative of every output with respect to *that one input*.
- **Reverse mode**: pick one *output*, propagate derivatives backward from it. One pass gives you the derivative of
  *that one output* with respect to every input.

A loss is one output and the weights are millions of inputs. Reverse mode gets all the gradients in one backward pass;
forward mode would need millions of passes. **Backpropagation is reverse-mode automatic differentiation**, applied to
the loss. That's the entire trick, and it was discovered and rediscovered several times (control theory in the 60s,
Linnainmaa's 1970 thesis on reverse-mode automatic differentiation, Werbos in 1974, and the famous Rumelhart, Hinton and
Williams paper in 1986 that made it popular for neural networks). In module 24 you'll build a reverse-mode engine
yourself.

## Trust, but verify: numerical gradient checking

Derivations have bugs. Mine too. Transposes get forgotten, factors of 2 get lost, signs flip. The cure is cheap:
compare your analytic gradient with a numerical one computed from the definition.

Use the **central difference**, which is much more accurate than the one-sided one:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \approx \frac{f(x + h e_i) - f(x - h e_i)}{2h}$$

Its error is $O(h^2)$ instead of $O(h)$. Choose $h$ around $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ in float64: too large and the
approximation error dominates, too small and floating point cancellation dominates (07.2 explains the trade-off
precisely). Compare with a **relative** error:

$$\text{rel\_err} = \frac{\|g_{\text{analytic}} - g_{\text{numeric}}\|}{\max(\|g_{\text{analytic}}\|, \|g_{\text{numeric}}\|, \epsilon)}$$

In float64, a correct gradient typically gives relative errors around $10^{-8}$ or better. Around $10^{-4}$, something is
fishy (or you hit a kink, like ReLU at 0). Above $10^{-2}$, your gradient is wrong.

```python
def numerical_grad(f, x, h=1e-6):
    g = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i in range(x.size):
        e = np.zeros_like(x); e.flat[i] = h
        g.flat[i] = (f(x + e) - f(x - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g
```

It costs two function evaluations per parameter, so it's only for testing, on small inputs. But every time you
implement a gradient by hand, in this course and in your career, check it this way. It takes five minutes and it has
saved me days. PyTorch has a built-in version, `torch.autograd.gradcheck`, which we'll use in 25.1.

> **Scar tissue.** For my thesis I hand-derived the gradient of a custom ranking loss, implemented it, and trained with it for two weeks.
> The metrics improved a bit, which is the worst possible outcome, because nobody questions a bit of improvement, least of all a tired
> student. One term had its sign flipped: the model was minimizing half of the loss and maximizing the other half, and the half it
> minimized happened to correlate with what I measured. A finite-difference gradient check found it in ten minutes. I'd skipped it because
> "the derivation was simple".

## Things that will bite you

- **Kinks.** ReLU, absolute value, max: not differentiable at the kink. Numerical checks near a kink disagree with any
  analytic choice. Evaluate gradient checks at random points, away from kinks.
- **Shape confusion.** $\nabla_w f$ should have the same shape as $w$. If your gradient for a $(d,)$ weight vector comes out
  $(d, 1)$ or $(1, d)$, you're one broadcasting accident away from a disaster (01.3).
- **Checking in float32.** Numerical gradients in float32 are noisy (relative errors around $10^{-3}$ are normal). Do gradient
  checks in float64.
- **Saturation.** When the sigmoid's input is large, its derivative is almost exactly zero: gradients vanish and learning
  stops. If your gradients are all ~0 from the first step, check whether your pre-activations are huge (usually an
  initialization or input scaling problem, 26.2).
