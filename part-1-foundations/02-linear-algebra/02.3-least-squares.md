# 02.3 Least squares and projections

*About 12 hours. Prerequisites: 02.1, 02.2. Lab: [`lab_02_3_least_squares.py`](labs/lab_02_3_least_squares.py).*

Least squares is two hundred years old (Legendre published it in 1805, Gauss claimed he'd been using it since
1795, and they argued about it, because some things never change in academia). It's still the most used fitting
method in the world. It's linear regression. It's the inner loop of many optimizers. It's how GPS receivers find
where you are. And it has the most beautiful geometric interpretation in all of this module: **fitting a model is
dropping a perpendicular.**

We'll derive it three ways (geometry, calculus, and in 17.1 statistics), then learn how to compute it without
destroying your precision, which is the part most courses skip and most bugs come from.

## The problem

You have $n$ observations and $d$ features, stacked into a data matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ and a
target vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$. You want weights $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ such that $Xw \approx y$.

When $n > d$ (more equations than unknowns, the normal situation) there's usually no exact solution: the system
is **overdetermined**. So we ask for the $w$ that makes the error as small as possible in the $L_2$ sense:

$$\hat{w} = \arg\min_w \|Xw - y\|_2^2 = \arg\min_w \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^\top w - y_i)^2$$

Why squared $L_2$ and not, say, $L_1$? Three reasons, in increasing order of depth: it's differentiable, it has a
closed-form solution, and (17.1) it's what maximum likelihood gives you when the noise is Gaussian. It also has a
downside you should know from day one: squaring makes big errors dominate, so least squares is **sensitive to
outliers**. One wrong row with a huge error can drag the whole fit. More on robust alternatives (Huber, $L_1$) in 17.1.

## The geometric view: projection onto the column space

Here's the picture. $Xw$ is a linear combination of the columns of $X$ (column view, 02.2). So as $w$ ranges over
all of $\mathbb{R}^d$, $Xw$ ranges over the **column space** of $X$, a $d$-dimensional flat subspace sitting inside
$\mathbb{R}^n$. The target $y$ is a point in $\mathbb{R}^n$ that, in general, is *not* in that subspace.

The least squares question is: **which point of the subspace is closest to $y$?**

And the answer, as in 2D with a point and a line, is the orthogonal projection: the closest point $\hat{y} = X\hat{w}$
is the one where the residual $r = y - \hat{y}$ is **perpendicular** to the subspace, meaning perpendicular to every
column of $X$:

$$X^\top (y - X\hat{w}) = 0$$

Rearranging gives the **normal equations** (called "normal" because they say the residual is normal, i.e.
perpendicular, to the columns):

$$X^\top X\,\hat{w} = X^\top y$$

If $X$ has full column rank, $X^\top X$ is invertible (it's PSD, and full rank makes it positive definite), and

$$\hat{w} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$$

The fitted values are $\hat{y} = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y = Py$, where

$$P = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$$

is the **projection matrix** onto the column space (statisticians call it the *hat matrix*, because it puts the
hat on $y$). You can check that $P^2 = P$ (projecting twice changes nothing) and $P^\top = P$.

The residual is $r = (I - P)y$, the projection onto the orthogonal complement. So least squares splits $y$ into two
perpendicular pieces, the part your features can explain and the part they can't:

$$y = \underbrace{Py}_{\hat{y}} + \underbrace{(I - P)y}_{r}, \qquad \hat{y} \perp r$$

and by Pythagoras, $\|y\|^2 = \|\hat{y}\|^2 + \|r\|^2$. If you center everything first, this decomposition is exactly
"total variance = explained variance + residual variance", which is where $R^2$ comes from (18.2).

## The calculus view (same answer)

Expand the loss:

$$L(w) = \|Xw - y\|^2 = w^\top X^\top X w - 2 y^\top X w + y^\top y$$

Take the gradient with respect to $w$ (you'll learn to do this properly in 03.1; for now, trust the rules
$\nabla_w (b^\top w) = b$ and $\nabla_w (w^\top A w) = 2Aw$ for symmetric $A$):

$$\nabla L(w) = 2X^\top X w - 2X^\top y$$

Setting it to zero gives the normal equations again. And since $X^\top X$ is PSD, $L$ is convex (a bowl), so this
stationary point is the global minimum. Geometry and calculus agree, which is always reassuring.

## An intercept, and why centering helps

A model $y \approx w_0 + w_1 x_1 + \cdots$ has an intercept (bias). The standard trick: add a column of ones to $X$.
Then $w_0$ is just another weight.

One consequence of the normal equations with an intercept column: $\mathbf{1}^\top r = 0$, so **the residuals of a
least squares fit with an intercept always sum to zero.** Without an intercept, they generally don't, and your
model is systematically biased. Always include the intercept unless you have a physical reason to force the fit
through the origin.

## Computing it properly

Now the part that matters in practice. The formula $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ is correct and you should **almost
never compute it that way**.

### Problem 1: forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number

From 02.2: $\kappa(X^\top X) = \kappa(X)^2$. If $X$ has a condition number of $10^6$ (not unusual for polynomial
features or badly scaled data), $X^\top X$ has $10^{12}$, and you've thrown away 12 of your 16 digits before doing
anything. With $\kappa(X) = 10^8$, the normal equations give you garbage, while a stable method still gives you about
8 correct digits.

### Solution: QR decomposition

Any $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ with full column rank can be factored as

$$X = QR$$

where $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ has orthonormal columns ($Q^\top Q = I$) and $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is upper
triangular. It's Gram-Schmidt (02.1) done in a numerically stable way (Householder reflections). Substitute into
the normal equations:

$$R^\top Q^\top Q R\,w = R^\top Q^\top y \;\Rightarrow\; R^\top R\,w = R^\top Q^\top y \;\Rightarrow\; R\,w = Q^\top y$$

and solve the triangular system $Rw = Q^\top y$ by back substitution. No $X^\top X$ ever formed, so the condition
number stays $\kappa(X)$, not its square.

```python
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(X)                       # reduced QR: Q is (n, d), R is (d, d)
w = scipy.linalg.solve_triangular(R, Q.T @ y)
```

In practice, just call:

```python
w, residuals, rank, sv = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
```

which uses the SVD (02.4) and handles rank-deficient matrices gracefully. scikit-learn's `LinearRegression` uses a
similar approach (it calls `scipy.linalg.lstsq`).

### The rank-deficient case: the pseudo-inverse

If $X$ doesn't have full column rank (duplicated or collinear features, more features than samples), $X^\top X$ is
singular and infinitely many $w$ minimize the loss (they differ by null space directions, 02.2). The **Moore-Penrose
pseudo-inverse** $X^+$ picks the one with the smallest norm:

$$\hat{w} = X^+ y$$

`np.linalg.lstsq` and `np.linalg.pinv` both give you this minimum-norm solution. That choice is not just a
convention: it turns out that gradient descent started from zero converges to exactly this minimum-norm solution,
which is one of the ingredients in explaining why hugely overparameterized models can still generalize (the double
descent story in 16.3).

### Cost

QR on an $n \times d$ matrix costs about $2nd^2$ FLOPs. For a million rows and 100 features, that's $2 \times 10^{10}$:
a few seconds. For a million features, forget closed forms; you'll use iterative methods like gradient descent
(03.3) or conjugate gradients, which only need matrix-vector products.

## Beyond plain least squares

Variants you'll meet later, all built on the same projection idea:

- **Weighted least squares**: minimize $\sum_i s_i (x_i^\top w - y_i)^2$ when some observations are more reliable
  than others. Solution: $(X^\top S X)^{-1}X^\top S y$ with $S = \operatorname{diag}(s)$. It's also the inner step of
  the algorithm that fits logistic regression and GLMs (iteratively reweighted least squares, 17.3).
- **Ridge regression**: add $\lambda\|w\|^2$ to the loss, giving $(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$. Better
  conditioned, always solvable, and a prior in disguise (17.2).
- **Basis expansions**: least squares is linear *in the weights*, not in the inputs. Fitting a polynomial
  $w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + w_3 x^3$ is linear least squares with columns $[1, x, x^2, x^3]$. So is fitting any fixed set
  of nonlinear features. That's a big idea: nonlinear functions, linear algebra. Kernels (19.3) take it to infinity.

## Things that will bite you

- **Polynomial features on raw inputs.** Columns $x, x^2, \ldots, x^{10}$ with $x$ around 1000 have values from $10^3$ to
  $10^{30}$. The condition number is astronomical. Center and scale $x$ first (or use orthogonal polynomials). The lab
  shows you the carnage.
- **The normal equations in production code.** Search your codebase for `inv(X.T @ X)`. I'll wait. Replace them.
- **Outliers.** One corrupted row can move the fit a lot. Plot residuals. Always.
- **Forgetting the intercept**, and then wondering why residuals have a nonzero mean.
- **More features than samples** ($d > n$): the fit is perfect on training data and meaningless outside it.
  Regularization (17.2) or fewer features.

   killed ($\lambda = 0$). There are $d$ ones and $n - d$ zeros, so $\operatorname{trace}(P) = d$: the "number of
   parameters", a fact used to define effective degrees of freedom for ridge regression and smoothers.

3. The first normal equation (the one for the intercept column) is $\mathbf{1}^\top(y - Xw) = 0$, i.e.

4. Adding a column enlarges the column space (or leaves it the same). The projection onto a bigger subspace is at

   reason we'll never judge a model by training error (16.2, 18.1).

5. (a) The normal equations work with $\kappa^2 = 10^{18}$, more than float64's $\approx 10^{16}$ range, so potentially
   no correct digits. (b) QR works with $\kappa = 10^9$, so about 16 - 9 = 7 correct digits. The lab lets you check
