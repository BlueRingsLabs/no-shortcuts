# 02.4 Eigendecomposition and the SVD

If you only remember one decomposition from this whole module, make it the SVD. It's the Swiss army knife of
applied math: it gives you PCA, low-rank compression, the pseudo-inverse, the condition number, the best rank-$k$
approximation of any matrix, latent semantic analysis, the first recommender systems that actually worked, and the
intuition behind LoRA fine-tuning. It also has a simple geometric story: **every matrix is a rotation, then a
stretch along the axes, then another rotation.** That's it. Everything else is consequences.

We'll get there through eigenvectors, because they come first historically and because the symmetric case is
where your intuition gets built.

## Eigenvectors: the directions a matrix doesn't turn

For a square matrix $A$, a nonzero vector $v$ is an **eigenvector** with **eigenvalue** $\lambda$ if

$$Av = \lambda v$$

$A$ sends $v$ to a multiple of itself: same line, stretched by $\lambda$ (flipped if $\lambda < 0$). Most vectors get
rotated off their line by $A$; eigenvectors are the special directions that don't.

If $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors, stack them as columns of $V$ and the eigenvalues in a
diagonal $\Lambda$, and you get

$$A = V \Lambda V^{-1}$$

In the eigenvector basis, $A$ is just a diagonal scaling. That's the whole point: find the coordinate system where
the problem becomes trivial. Powers become easy too: $A^k = V\Lambda^k V^{-1}$. After many applications, the direction
with the largest $|\lambda|$ dominates everything else, which is why:

- **Power iteration** (repeatedly multiply a vector by $A$ and normalize) converges to the top eigenvector. PageRank
  is power iteration on the web's link matrix.
- **Repeated application of the same matrix explodes or vanishes** depending on whether its largest eigenvalue is
  above or below 1 in magnitude. That's the exploding/vanishing gradient problem in recurrent networks (28.1), in
  one sentence.

Caveats for general matrices: eigenvalues can be complex (a 2D rotation has no real eigenvectors, obviously), and
some matrices don't have enough eigenvectors to diagonalize. You'll rarely care, because the matrices ML cares about
are mostly symmetric.

## The symmetric case: the spectral theorem

If $A$ is **symmetric** ($A = A^\top$), everything is as nice as it gets:

1. All eigenvalues are real.
2. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and you can always choose an **orthonormal** set of $n$
   eigenvectors.
3. So $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with $Q$ orthogonal.

That's the **spectral theorem**. Geometrically: a symmetric matrix stretches space along $n$ perpendicular axes (its
eigenvectors) by factors given by its eigenvalues. No rotation, no shearing. A covariance matrix is symmetric, so
its eigenvectors are the perpendicular axes of your data cloud's ellipsoid, and its eigenvalues are the variances
along those axes. That sentence *is* PCA (21.3).

You can also write the decomposition as a sum of rank-1 pieces:

$$A = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\, q_i q_i^\top$$

**Positive semidefinite** matrices (02.2) are exactly the symmetric matrices with all $\lambda_i \ge 0$. This gives you a
practical test (compute eigenvalues with `np.linalg.eigvalsh`) and a practical fix when rounding produces tiny negative
eigenvalues in a covariance matrix (clip them to zero, or add a small multiple of the identity, the famous
"jitter" in Gaussian processes, 23.4).

For quadratic forms, the eigenvalues tell you the shape of the bowl: $f(x) = x^\top A x$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge
\cdots \ge \lambda_n > 0$ is an elongated bowl whose steepest direction has curvature $\lambda_1$ and flattest has $\lambda_n$.
Their ratio is the condition number, and it's precisely what makes gradient descent slow in narrow valleys (03.3). The
Hessian of a loss is symmetric, so this is how you'll analyze optimization.

In code, for symmetric matrices, always use `eigh` (or `eigvalsh`), never the general `eig`: it's faster, exploits the
symmetry, returns real sorted eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors.

```python
lam, Q = np.linalg.eigh(C)          # ascending eigenvalues
lam, Q = lam[::-1], Q[:, ::-1]      # I usually flip to descending
```

## The SVD: every matrix, not just square symmetric ones

Eigendecompositions need square matrices, and nice ones need symmetric. Data matrices are rectangular. The
**singular value decomposition** works for *any* $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$:

$$A = U \Sigma V^\top$$

- $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ orthogonal: the **right singular vectors**, an orthonormal basis of the input space.
- $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ diagonal with $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0$: the **singular values**.
- $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ orthogonal: the **left singular vectors**, an orthonormal basis of the output space.

Read it right to left, as a composition of functions (02.2): $V^\top$ rotates the input so that the special directions
$v_i$ line up with the axes, $\Sigma$ stretches axis $i$ by $\sigma_i$ (and drops or pads dimensions), and $U$ rotates the
result into place. **Rotate, stretch, rotate.** Every linear map, no matter how ugly, is that. The unit circle always
maps to an ellipse whose semi-axes are $\sigma_i u_i$.

Equivalently, $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$: each input direction $v_i$ is sent to the output direction $u_i$, scaled by $\sigma_i$.

As a sum of rank-1 matrices:

$$A = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^\top, \qquad r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$$

The number of nonzero singular values is the rank. The singular vectors for $\sigma_i > 0$ span the column space
($u$'s) and the row space ($v$'s); the ones for $\sigma_i = 0$ span the null spaces. The SVD hands you all four
fundamental subspaces of a matrix at once.

### Connection to eigenvectors

$$A^\top A = V \Sigma^\top \Sigma V^\top, \qquad A A^\top = U \Sigma \Sigma^\top U^\top$$

So the right singular vectors are the eigenvectors of $A^\top A$, the left ones are eigenvectors of $AA^\top$, and
$\sigma_i^2$ are their eigenvalues. For a centered data matrix $X$, $X^\top X / (n-1)$ is the covariance matrix, so
**PCA can be computed by the SVD of the data matrix directly**, without ever forming the covariance. And by now you
know why that's better: forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number (02.3). Every serious PCA implementation uses
the SVD.

## What the SVD gives you

**The condition number and the norm.** The spectral norm (largest stretch) is $\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1$, and the condition
number is $\kappa(A) = \sigma_1 / \sigma_{\min}$. Singular values near zero are directions the matrix nearly destroys.

**The pseudo-inverse.** $A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^\top$, where $\Sigma^+$ inverts the nonzero singular values and leaves the zeros
alone. This is how `lstsq` and `pinv` handle rank-deficient least squares (02.3), and the `rcond` argument is the
threshold below which a singular value is treated as zero. Truncating tiny singular values is also a form of
regularization: it refuses to amplify noise along nearly destroyed directions.

**The best low-rank approximation (Eckart-Young theorem).** Keep only the top $k$ terms:

$$A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$$

Then $A_k$ is the *closest* rank-$k$ matrix to $A$, in both the spectral and Frobenius norm, and the error is exactly what
you threw away:

$$\|A - A_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{i > k} \sigma_i^2, \qquad \|A - A_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}$$

No other rank-$k$ matrix does better. This is a remarkable result, and it's the mathematical foundation of:

- **Compression**: an image as a matrix, stored as $k$ singular triplets instead of all pixels. If the singular values
  decay fast, a small $k$ keeps most of the image.
- **Denoising**: signal tends to live in the top singular directions and noise spreads evenly across all of them.
  Truncation keeps the first and discards most of the second.
- **Latent semantic analysis**: SVD of a term-document matrix, the ancestor of word embeddings (34.3).
- **Matrix factorization for recommendations**: the user-item matrix is approximately low rank, because tastes are
  driven by a few latent factors (23.2). The famous Netflix Prize solutions were built on this idea.
- **LoRA**: fine-tuning updates to large weight matrices are empirically close to low rank, so learning a rank-$r$
  update $BA$ captures most of the benefit with a tiny fraction of the parameters (38.2).

**Explained variance.** For centered data, $\sigma_i^2 / \sum_j \sigma_j^2$ is the fraction of total variance captured by
direction $i$. Plotting that (the "scree plot") is how you decide how many principal components to keep (21.3).

### Computing it

```python
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)   # "thin" SVD: U is (m, r), s is (r,), Vt is (r, n)
A_k = U[:, :k] * s[:k] @ Vt[:k]                    # broadcasting s over columns, then matmul
```

A full SVD costs $O(mn\min(m,n))$, which is fine up to a few thousand in the smaller dimension. For huge matrices where
you only need the top $k$ components, use **randomized SVD** (`sklearn.utils.extmath.randomized_svd`, the default in
scikit-learn's PCA for large data): multiply by a small random matrix to capture the dominant subspace, then do an exact
SVD of that small thing. It's one of the most useful algorithms of the last twenty years, and it's surprisingly simple.

## Things that will bite you

- **Sign ambiguity.** If $(u_i, v_i)$ is a singular pair, so is $(-u_i, -v_i)$. Different libraries, or the same library
  on different machines, can return different signs. Never compare singular vectors or principal components without
  aligning signs first. (I once watched a classmate chase a "bug" for a day that was just a PCA component flipping sign
  between two runs.)
- **`eig` on symmetric matrices**: use `eigh`. `eig` can return tiny imaginary parts from rounding, and unsorted results.
- **Eigenvalues are sorted ascending in `eigh`, singular values descending in `svd`.** Yes, really.
- **Scale before decomposing data.** Singular values are dominated by the features with the largest variance. If one
  column is in dollars and another in millions of dollars, the "principal component" is just the second column.
