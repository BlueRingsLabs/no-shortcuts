## How floats work

A floating point number stores a sign, an exponent and a mantissa (the significant digits), like scientific notation in
base 2:

$$x = (-1)^s \times 1.m_1m_2\ldots m_p \times 2^{e}$$

The IEEE 754 formats you'll meet:

| Format | Bits (sign/exp/mantissa) | Machine epsilon | Largest | Smallest normal | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| float64 | 1 / 11 / 52 | $2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ | $1.8 \times 10^{308}$ | $2.2 \times 10^{-308}$ | NumPy default, statistics |
| float32 | 1 / 8 / 23 | $1.2 \times 10^{-7}$ | $3.4 \times 10^{38}$ | $1.2 \times 10^{-38}$ | deep learning default |
| float16 | 1 / 5 / 10 | $9.8 \times 10^{-4}$ | $65{,}504$ | $6.1 \times 10^{-5}$ | mixed precision (older GPUs) |
| bfloat16 | 1 / 8 / 7 | $7.8 \times 10^{-3}$ | $3.4 \times 10^{38}$ | $1.2 \times 10^{-38}$ | mixed precision (modern GPUs, TPUs) |
| fp8 (E4M3) | 1 / 4 / 3 | $0.125$ | $448$ | about $0.016$ | training/inference of large models on the newest hardware |

**Machine epsilon** $\epsilon$ is the gap between 1 and the next representable number. It's the relative precision: every operation's result is
rounded to the nearest representable value, with relative error at most $\epsilon/2$. So float64 has about 16 significant decimal digits, float32
about 7, float16 about 3, bfloat16 about 2.

Two consequences that surprise people:

1. **Most decimals aren't representable.** 0.1 in binary is a repeating fraction (like 1/3 in decimal), so it's stored rounded. Hence
   `0.1 + 0.2 = 0.30000000000000004`. Never compare floats with `==`; use `math.isclose` or `np.allclose` with a tolerance you chose
   deliberately.
2. **The gaps grow with magnitude.** Representable numbers are dense near 0 and sparse far from it. In float32, above $2^{24} \approx 16.7$ million,
   the gap between consecutive floats is 2: you can't represent 16,777,217. Adding 1 to 16,777,216 gives 16,777,216. A float32 counter stops
   counting. A float32 timestamp in seconds since 1970 has a resolution of about two minutes. I've seen both in production.

bfloat16 vs float16 is a trade-off worth understanding: bfloat16 keeps float32's exponent range (no overflow at 65,504, no underflow of small
gradients) but has only 8 bits of precision; float16 has more precision but a tiny range. For training, range matters more than precision,
which is why bfloat16 won (25.3). float16 training needs **loss scaling** to keep small gradients from underflowing to zero.

## Special values

- `inf` and `-inf`: from overflow (`np.exp(1000)`) or division by zero.
- `nan` (not a number): from `0/0`, `inf - inf`, `0 * inf`, `sqrt(-1)`. **NaN is contagious**: any arithmetic with NaN gives NaN, and
  `nan != nan` (the only value not equal to itself, which is how `np.isnan` could be implemented). One NaN in a batch poisons the loss, the
  gradients, and then every weight in the network after the optimizer step. That's why a single bad input can destroy a training run.
- **Subnormals**: tiny numbers below the smallest normal, with reduced precision. They can be very slow on some CPUs. Deep learning
  frameworks often flush them to zero.

## The four ways floating point bites

### 1. Catastrophic cancellation

Subtracting two nearly equal numbers destroys relative precision. If $a$ and $b$ each carry 16 correct digits and agree in the first 12, then
$a - b$ has about 4 correct digits: the leading digits cancel and the rounding errors don't.

You met this in 01.3 ($\|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2 - 2a^\top b$ for nearby points) and in 03.1 (numerical derivatives with tiny $h$). The classic statistical
victim is the **one-pass variance formula**:

$$\operatorname{Var}(x) = \mathbb{E}[x^2] - (\mathbb{E}x)^2$$

For data with a large mean and small spread (timestamps, prices around 1000.00 with cents of variation), both terms are huge and nearly
equal, and the subtraction produces garbage, sometimes negative. Use the two-pass formula (subtract the mean first) or **Welford's online
algorithm** for streams, which updates the mean and the sum of squared deviations incrementally and stays stable. The lab computes a negative
variance with the naive formula. Seeing it once is enough to never forget.

### 2. Overflow and underflow

$e^{x}$ overflows float32 for $x > 88.7$ and float64 for $x > 709.8$. $e^{x}$ underflows to 0 for $x < -104$ (float32). Softmax, sigmoid,
log-likelihoods and anything with exponentials are at risk.

The fixes are a small set of identities you should know by heart:

**Log-sum-exp**: for any constant $c$,
$$\log\sum_i e^{x_i} = c + \log\sum_i e^{x_i - c}$$
Choose $c = \max_i x_i$. The largest exponent becomes $e^0 = 1$, nothing overflows, and at least one term is 1 so the sum doesn't underflow to 0
either. `scipy.special.logsumexp`, `torch.logsumexp`.

**Softmax** by the same shift: $\operatorname{softmax}(x) = \operatorname{softmax}(x - \max x)$.

**Log-softmax**: $\log\operatorname{softmax}(x)_i = x_i - \operatorname{logsumexp}(x)$. Never compute `log(softmax(x))` in two steps: the softmax can
underflow to exactly 0 for very negative logits, and then the log is `-inf`. This is why PyTorch's `CrossEntropyLoss` takes **logits**, not
probabilities, and fuses log-softmax with the NLL internally.

**Sigmoid and softplus**: $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ overflows in the exponential for very negative $x$ (harmlessly, giving 0, but with warnings).
The stable log-sigmoid is $\log\sigma(x) = -\operatorname{softplus}(-x)$ with $\operatorname{softplus}(z) = \log(1 + e^z) = \max(z, 0) + \log(1 + e^{-|z|})$. In NumPy,
`np.logaddexp(0, z)`. This is how `binary_cross_entropy_with_logits` stays finite for any input, while `binary_cross_entropy` on a sigmoid output
dies when the sigmoid rounds to exactly 0 or 1.

**`log1p` and `expm1`**: for small $x$, `np.log(1 + x)` loses precision because `1 + x` rounds; `np.log1p(x)` computes it accurately. Same for
`np.expm1(x)` $= e^x - 1$.

The pattern behind all of them: **stay in log space as long as possible**, and use the fused, stable functions your library provides. Products of
many probabilities become sums of logs (04.4). The likelihood of a 1,000-token sequence is $\approx e^{-2000}$, far below float64's range, but its log
is a perfectly ordinary number.

### 3. Accumulation error

Adding many numbers accumulates rounding error. Adding a small number to a large running sum can lose the small number entirely (float32:
$10^8 + 1 = 10^8$). Summing a million float32 values naively can be off in the third or fourth significant digit, and the result depends on the
**order** of summation, because floating point addition is not associative: $(a + b) + c \ne a + (b + c)$ in general.

Remedies: sum in higher precision (accumulate float16/bfloat16 in float32, which is what GPUs do in matrix multiplications and why the tensor
core "accumulator" precision matters), **pairwise summation** (what NumPy's `sum` does, with error growing like $O(\log n)$ instead of $O(n)$), or
**Kahan summation** (track the lost low-order bits in a compensation variable). The lab compares them.

Non-associativity also explains **non-determinism on GPUs**: parallel reductions add numbers in an order that depends on thread scheduling, so
the same computation can give slightly different results from run to run. Usually harmless, occasionally maddening when you're trying to
reproduce a bug. PyTorch has `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` for when you really need bit-exact results, at some speed cost (25.3).

### 4. Ill-conditioning

A well-written algorithm can still give inaccurate answers if the *problem* is ill-conditioned (02.2): the condition number bounds how much input
errors (including rounding) get amplified. Stable algorithms (QR instead of normal equations, 02.3) avoid adding extra error on top. The rule of
thumb stays: you lose about $\log_{10}\kappa$ digits.

## Mixed precision in one paragraph

Modern training keeps a float32 master copy of the weights, runs the forward and backward passes in bfloat16 or float16 (two to eight times faster
on tensor cores, half the memory for activations), accumulates matrix multiplications in float32, computes sensitive operations (softmax,
normalizations, losses) in float32, and, for float16, scales the loss up before backward and the gradients down after, so small gradients don't
underflow. PyTorch's `torch.autocast` and `GradScaler` implement exactly this recipe. Details in 25.3; the reason every step exists is in this lesson.

## Debugging numerical problems

When you see NaN or inf:

1. **Find the first one.** `torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True)` in PyTorch, or `np.seterr(all="raise")` in NumPy to turn warnings into
   exceptions at the exact operation.
2. **Check the inputs.** A NaN in the data (a missing value that became NaN, a division by a zero count in a feature) is the most common cause.
   Validate data (10.3), always.
3. **Check for exp/log of extreme values**: unfused log(softmax), log(0), exp of large logits, division by a tiny norm.
4. **Check the learning rate.** Divergence (03.3) produces inf, then NaN.
5. **Check precision.** Does it happen in float16 but not float32? Overflow above 65,504 or underflow of gradients.

1. $2^{24} + 1 = 16{,}777{,}217$. float32 has 24 bits of significand (23 stored plus the implicit leading 1), so above $2^{24}$ the spacing between
