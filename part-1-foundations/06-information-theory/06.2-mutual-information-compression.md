# 06.2 Mutual information and learning as compression

*About 7 hours. Prerequisites: 06.1. Lab: [`lab_06_2_mutual_information.py`](labs/lab_06_2_mutual_information.py).*

In 04.3 I told you that correlation only sees linear relationships: $X$ and $X^2$ can have zero correlation while one
completely determines the other. So what measures *any* kind of dependence? Mutual information. It's the quantity behind
the information gain that decision trees use to pick splits (20.1), behind feature selection methods, behind the
contrastive losses that trained CLIP (30.2), and behind a very old and very useful idea: that learning is compression,
and that the model that compresses the data best is the one that generalizes best. That last idea is also, I think, the
most honest answer to "why do large language models work at all".

## Conditional entropy

Start with how much uncertainty remains about $Y$ once you know $X$:

$$H(Y \mid X) = \sum_x p(x)\,H(Y \mid X = x) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x, y)\log p(y \mid x)$$

It's the average, over $x$, of the entropy of the conditional distribution. Knowing something can't increase uncertainty on average:
$H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y)$, with equality iff independent. (A specific observation *can* increase your uncertainty, like a test result that
contradicts everything else; the average can't.)

The **chain rule for entropy** mirrors the chain rule of probability (04.2):

$$H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X)$$

The bits to describe both = the bits to describe $X$ + the bits to describe $Y$ given that you already know $X$.

## Mutual information

$$I(X; Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y)$$

It's the reduction in uncertainty about one variable from learning the other: how many bits $X$ tells you about $Y$. Equivalently, it's a KL
divergence:

$$I(X; Y) = D_{\text{KL}}\big(p(x, y) \,\|\, p(x)\,p(y)\big) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$$

the "distance" between the actual joint and what the joint would be if the variables were independent. From this form:

- $I(X; Y) \ge 0$, and $= 0$ **iff $X$ and $Y$ are independent**. Not just uncorrelated: independent. Any dependence, linear or not,
  makes it positive. That's the thing correlation can't do.
- It's symmetric: $I(X; Y) = I(Y; X)$.
- $I(X; X) = H(X)$: a variable tells you everything about itself.
- It's invariant to invertible transformations of each variable: $I(X; Y) = I(f(X); g(Y))$ for bijections $f$, $g$. Log-transform a feature
  and its MI with the target doesn't change (in theory; estimates may).

For jointly Gaussian variables with correlation $\rho$, there's a closed form: $I = -\frac{1}{2}\log(1 - \rho^2)$. So in the Gaussian world, MI and
correlation carry the same information. Outside of it, they don't.

### The data processing inequality

If $X \to Y \to Z$ is a chain (Z depends on X only through Y), then

$$I(X; Z) \le I(X; Y)$$

**Processing can't create information.** No transformation of your features, however clever, can contain more information about the label than
the raw features did. Feature engineering (22.1) can make information *easier for a model to use*, never create new information. The same goes
for every layer of a neural network: each layer can only keep or lose information about the input. What networks do is reshape it into a form
where the relevant part is linearly accessible.

This inequality also has a security reading I like: if an anonymization step is a function of the data, it can only reduce information about the
individuals, but "reduce" may mean from 100 bits to 30 bits, and 30 bits identify one person among a billion. Removing names isn't anonymization
(15.2 and 54.2).

## Using mutual information

**Feature selection**: rank features by their estimated MI with the target. It catches nonlinear relationships that correlation misses
(`sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_classif` and `mutual_info_regression`). Two caveats: it scores features one at a time, so it misses
features that are only useful in combination (the XOR problem), and it doesn't account for redundancy (two copies of the same feature both score
high). Methods like mRMR (max-relevance, min-redundancy) address the second.

**Decision trees** (20.1): the **information gain** of a split is exactly the mutual information between the split indicator and the label,
estimated on the training data at that node: $H(Y) - H(Y \mid \text{split})$. Trees greedily pick the split that tells them the most about $Y$.

**Contrastive learning** (30.1): the InfoNCE loss used to train CLIP and SimCLR is a lower bound on the mutual information between two views of the
same thing (an image and its caption; two augmentations of the same image). Maximizing it pushes the representations to keep what the views have
in common.

### Estimating MI is hard

For discrete variables with enough data, plug-in estimates from counts work. For continuous variables, you need estimators, and they're tricky:

- **Binning** (discretize, then count) is simple and biased: too few bins and you miss structure; too many and every cell has 0 or 1 samples and the
  estimate is inflated (the plug-in estimate of MI is biased *upward*, so two independent variables look dependent). The lab shows this bias.
- **k-nearest-neighbour estimators** (Kraskov, Stögbauer and Grassberger, 2004) are what scikit-learn uses. Better, still noisy in high dimensions.
- **Neural estimators** (MINE, InfoNCE) scale to high dimensions but estimate bounds, not the MI itself.

Treat MI estimates as rankings, not as precise numbers, and always compare against a shuffled baseline (MI between the feature and a permuted
target) to see what "zero" looks like with your sample size.

## Learning as compression

Here's the idea that connects this module to the whole course. Suppose you want to send a dataset of labels to a friend who already has the inputs.
You could send the labels raw. Or you could send a **model** plus the labels **encoded with that model** (using $-\log_2 q(y \mid x)$ bits per label, as in
06.1). The total cost:

$$L(\text{model}) + L(\text{data} \mid \text{model})$$

- A trivial model is cheap to send, but encodes the data poorly.
- A model that memorizes the data encodes it perfectly, but the model itself is as big as the data.
- A model that captured the real **regularities** is small *and* encodes the data well.

The **minimum description length (MDL)** principle (Rissanen, 1978) says: prefer the model that minimizes the total. It's Occam's razor made
quantitative, and it's a principled way to trade off fit against complexity. BIC, the Bayesian information criterion used in classical model
selection, is an approximation of it. And regularization (04.4) is its cousin: the prior's negative log-probability is the "cost of describing
the parameters".

Why does compression relate to generalization? Because regularities are what let you compress, and regularities are what carry over to new data.
Noise is incompressible, and it doesn't generalize. A model that compresses the training data well *by exploiting patterns* (rather than
memorizing) will likely predict new data well. There are formal versions of this (Occam bounds in PAC learning, 16.3), and it's the intuition behind
a lot of modern thinking about why big models trained on huge datasets work: to predict the next token of the entire internet well, with far fewer
parameters than the training data has bits, a model is forced to find the regularities, meaning grammar, facts, and some fraction of the reasoning
that produced the text. Whether that amounts to "understanding" is an argument for philosophers and LinkedIn. It certainly amounts to compression.

## Things that will bite you

- **Upward-biased MI estimates** with small samples or too many bins. Always compare against a shuffled baseline.
- **Univariate MI screening** throwing away features that only matter in interaction.
- **Treating MI as an effect size.** High MI means "informative", not "important for the business", and not causal (05.4).
- **"Anonymized" data**: the data processing inequality says anonymization can't add information, not that it removes enough.

## Exercises

1. Let $X$ be uniform on $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ and $Y = X \bmod 2$. Compute $H(X)$, $H(Y)$, $H(Y \mid X)$, $H(X \mid Y)$ and $I(X; Y)$.
2. Show that $I(X; Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y)$ from the definition as a KL divergence.
3. $X$ and $Y$ are independent fair bits, and $Z = X \oplus Y$ (XOR). Compute $I(X; Z)$, $I(Y; Z)$ and $I(X, Y; Z)$. What does this tell you about
   univariate feature selection?
4. For a bivariate Gaussian with $\rho = 0.9$, what's the MI in nats and in bits? And for $\rho = 0.1$?
5. Using MDL, explain why a degree-15 polynomial fit to 20 noisy points from a line is a bad model, even though its training error is lower.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $H(X) = 2$ bits, $H(Y) = 1$ bit. $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ (Y is a function of X). $H(X \mid Y) = 1$ bit (given parity, two options remain). $I = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = 1$ bit.

2. $I = \sum p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} = \sum p(x,y)\log p(x,y) - \sum p(x,y)\log p(x) - \sum p(x,y)\log p(y) = -H(X,Y) + H(X) + H(Y)$, using
   $\sum_y p(x,y) = p(x)$ in the middle term and similarly in the last.

3. $Z$ is a fair bit independent of $X$ alone (knowing $X$, $Z$ is still 50/50 because $Y$ is), so $I(X; Z) = I(Y; Z) = 0$. But $I(X, Y; Z) = H(Z) = 1$ bit:
   together they determine it completely. Univariate screening would throw away both features as useless. That's the classic failure mode, and
   it's not exotic: interaction effects in real data (a feature that matters only for one customer segment) behave the same way.

4. $\rho = 0.9$: $-\frac{1}{2}\ln(0.19) \approx 0.830$ nats $\approx 1.20$ bits. $\rho = 0.1$: $-\frac{1}{2}\ln(0.99) \approx 0.005$ nats $\approx 0.007$ bits. MI grows fast as
   correlation approaches 1 (and is infinite at $|\rho| = 1$, where one variable determines the other exactly and continuously).

5. The degree-15 polynomial needs 16 coefficients stated to high precision (a long description), and it mostly encodes the noise, which gives no
   savings on new data. The line needs 2 coefficients plus the residuals encoded with a Gaussian model of their variance. Total description length is
   lower for the line, so MDL picks it. The lab runs this comparison with a BIC-style approximation over 300 datasets, and shows
   the honest version: with only 20 points, BIC still picks a wrong degree about a quarter of the time. Small data is small data.
